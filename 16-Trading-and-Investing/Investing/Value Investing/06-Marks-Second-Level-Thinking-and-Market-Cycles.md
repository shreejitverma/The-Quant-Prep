# Howard Marks: Second-Level Thinking and Market Cycles

## TL;DR

- Howard Marks, co-founder of Oaktree Capital Management, establishes that superior investment returns require "Second-Level Thinking": a thought process that is both fundamentally different from consensus and analytically superior.
- First-level thinkers focus on superficial, obvious attributes ("It's a great company; let's buy the stock"); second-level thinkers evaluate embedded expectations, probability distributions, consensus sentiment, and asset pricing.
- The psychological pendulum swings perpetually between extremes of euphoria and despair, greed and fear, credulity and skepticism, and risk tolerance and risk aversion.
- The Credit Cycle is the most volatile and powerful engine of modern capitalism, creating devastating financial collapses and extraordinary distressed buying opportunities.
- True risk is not price volatility or statistical beta; risk is exclusively the probability of permanent capital loss, and the greatest risk arises precisely when market consensus believes risk has been eliminated.

## Learning objectives

- Differentiate first-level thinking from second-level thinking across corporate and macroeconomic scenarios.
- Map the historical progression of the economic, psychological, and credit cycles.
- Measure market temperature to calibrate portfolio stance between aggressive risk deployment and defensive capital preservation.
- Redefine financial risk away from academic volatility (variance) to permanent capital impairment.
- Execute the asymmetric principle of "winning by not losing" across debt and equity asset classes.

## Core concepts

### 1. First-Level Thinking vs Second-Level Thinking

Howard Marks argues that because financial markets aggregate the collective opinions of millions of participants, simple, first-level analysis is already reflected in the market price:

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                    THE TWO LEVELS OF THINKING                         |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
| FIRST-LEVEL THINKING                   SECOND-LEVEL THINKING          |
| (Simplistic, Obvious, Herd Consensus)  (Deep, Probabilistic, Variant) |
+----------------------------------------+------------------------------+
| "The outlook for the company is        | "It is a good company, but   |
| great; the economy is booming; buy."   | everyone thinks it is great; |
|                                        | it is priced for perfection; |
|                                        | disappointment looms; sell." |
+----------------------------------------+------------------------------+
| "A recession is coming; inflation is   | "The recession is bad, but   |
| high; let's sell our equities."        | panic has depressed prices   |
|                                        | far below liquidation value; |
|                                        | bad news is priced in; buy." |
+----------------------------------------+------------------------------+
| "Interest rates are rising; bond       | "Yield spreads have exploded |
| prices will collapse; stay away."      | to 1,200 bps; default risk is|
|                                        | vastly overstated; buy debt."|
+----------------------------------------+------------------------------+
```

The Second-Level Checklist:
- What is the range of likely future outcomes?
- Which outcome does the consensus expect?
- How does my expectation differ from the consensus?
- How is the consensus expectation currently reflected in the asset price?
- What will happen to the asset price if consensus is right, and what will happen if I am right?

### 2. The Pendulum of Market Psychology

Financial markets behave like a giant pendulum swinging between emotional extremes.
The pendulum rarely pauses at the happy medium of rational valuation; it rushes past the midpoint on its way from one irrational extreme to the other:

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                    THE PSYCHOLOGICAL PENDULUM                         |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                  |                                    |
|         OPTIMISM                 |                 PESSIMISM          |
|          GREED                   |                   FEAR             |
|        CREDULITY                 |                SKEPTICISM          |
|      RISK TOLERANCE              |              RISK AVERSION         |
|   "There is no price             |           "No price is low         |
|    too high to pay"              |           enough to buy"           |
|            \                     |                     /              |
|             \                    |                    /               |
|              \                   |                   /                |
|               \                  v                  /                 |
|                +---------> FAIR VALUE <------------+                  |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

The Three Stages of a Bull Market (Marks):
1. **Stage 1**: When a few forward-looking people begin to believe things will get better.
2. **Stage 2**: When most investors realize improvement is actually taking place.
3. **Stage 3**: When everyone concludes that things will get better forever (Peak Euphoria; time to sell).

The Three Stages of a Bear Market:
1. **Stage 1**: When a few prudent investors recognize that despite prevailing euphoria, things cannot remain perfect.
2. **Stage 2**: When most investors recognize that economic deterioration is occurring.
3. **Stage 3**: When everyone becomes convinced that things can only get worse (Trough Despair; time to buy).

### 3. The Power of the Credit Cycle

Marks identifies the Credit Cycle as the most extreme and dynamic cycle in economics:

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                    THE SELF-REINFORCING CREDIT CYCLE                  |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. Economic growth improves; corporate profits expand.                |
| 2. Banks and bond markets become flush with capital.                  |
| 3. Lenders compete aggressively: interest rates fall, covenants drop. |
| 4. Unqualified borrowers and unsound, highly leveraged deals get funded.|
| 5. Economic shock hits; overleveraged borrowers default.              |
| 6. Lenders panic; credit standards tighten to extreme levels.         |
| 7. Refinancing freezes; even creditworthy borrowers face liquidity crunches.|
| 8. Distressed debt collapses to pennies on the dollar (Opportunity). |
| 9. Conservative restructuring heals balance sheets; cycle restarts.   |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

*"The worst loans are made at the best of times."*
When risk aversion evaporates, capital providers abandon diligence, underwriting "covenant-lite" debt.
This creates the toxic corporate balance sheets that fuel the next tidal wave of distress.

### 4. Taking the Temperature of the Market

Marks rejects macroeconomic timing: *"We may never know where we're going, but we'd better have a good idea where we are."*
Investors must calibrate portfolio aggressiveness by taking the market's temperature:

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                    MARKET TEMPERATURE DIAGNOSTIC                      |
+------------------------+-----------------------+----------------------+
| Diagnostic Indicator   | High Risk (Overheated)| Low Risk (Depressed) |
+------------------------+-----------------------+----------------------+
| High-Yield Spreads     | Narrow (< 300 bps)    | Blown out (> 800 bps)|
| New Issue Quality      | Covenant-lite, SPACs  | Highly restrictive   |
| P/E Multiples          | Historical 90th pctl  | Historical 10th pctl |
| Investor Sentiment     | Fear of missing out   | Fear of losing money |
| Media Headlines        | "New Economic Era"    | "Death of Equities"  |
| Portfolio Posture      | DEFENSIVE & CAUTIOUS  | AGGRESSIVE & COMMITTED|
+------------------------+-----------------------+----------------------+
```

### 5. Redefining Risk: The Perverse Paradox of Safety

Academic finance equates risk with volatility ($\sigma$, standard deviation).
Marks rejects this completely:
- Volatility is a statistical convenience invented by professors who needed a quantifiable metric to run mathematical equations.
- Investors do not fear volatility; they fear **permanent capital loss**.
- A volatile stock that falls 30% and then rebounds to 3x intrinsic value poses zero risk to a patient value investor.
- An overleveraged stock that goes bankrupt poses terminal risk, regardless of whether its daily price swings were smooth.

The Paradox of Risk:
- Risk is highest when broad market consensus believes there is no risk, because investors bid up prices, eliminate cash buffers, and use excessive leverage.
- Risk is lowest when everyone perceives catastrophic risk, because fear depresses asset prices so low that an enormous margin of safety is built into the purchase price.

### 6. Asymmetric Risk/Reward: Winning by Not Losing

In the classic paper *The Loser's Game* (Ellis 1975), tennis matches are categorized into:
- **Professional Tennis**: Players win points by hitting unreturnable winners (active skill).
- **Amateur Tennis**: Players lose points by committing unforced errors into the net; the player who avoids mistakes wins.

Investing in public markets is an amateur's game:
- Trying to hit miraculous 10x speculative winners regularly results in unforced errors, catastrophic drawdowns, and ruin.
- By systematically avoiding bad credits, avoiding overpriced momentum favorites, eliminating debt leverage, and maintaining strict valuation discipline, the investor wins by not losing.
- Oaktree's foundational motto: *"If we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves."*

## Worked numerical case study

Evaluate a portfolio allocation decision across the credit cycle:
Global credit markets enter an inflection point:
- 10-Year High-Yield Credit Spreads: Have compressed to 285 bps over Treasuries (historical 5th percentile, historical median is 450 bps).
- Default Rate over past 12 months: 1.2% (Historical average is 3.5%).
- Issuance Structure: 85% of newly issued junk bonds feature "covenant-lite" indentures.
- Retail Inflows: High-yield mutual funds and ETFs recorded 12 consecutive weeks of record inflows.

### Step 1: Execute Second-Level Analysis
1. **First-Level View**:
   - "High yield bonds yield 6.5% while Treasuries yield 3.65%. The economy is growing, corporate balance sheets look solid, and defaults are at historic lows. Buy high yield to boost portfolio income."
2. **Second-Level View**:
   - Defaults are low precisely because credit was cheap and easily refinanced over the past three years.
   - A spread of 285 bps provides zero cushion for credit degradation; if defaults merely mean-revert to 3.5% with a 40% recovery rate, the annual default loss is $3.5\% \times (1 - 0.40) = 2.10\%$.
   - Net expected spread above Treasuries is only $2.85\% - 2.10\% = 0.75\%$ (75 bps), offering negligible compensation for illiquidity and severe credit risk.
   - Covenant-lite issuance indicates lenders have abandoned underwriting discipline.

### Step 2: Calibrate Portfolio Temperature and Action
- Market Diagnostic: The credit market is in Stage 3 Euphoria; the pendulum is at an extreme of greed and risk tolerance.
- Capital Allocation Action:
  1. Liquidate high-yield corporate credit holdings.
  2. Transition capital into short-term US Treasury Bills (yielding 3.65% with zero credit risk).
  3. Build unallocated cash reserves to 35% of the portfolio.
  4. Wait patiently for the credit window to slam shut and spreads to widen past 800 bps before deploying capital into distressed debt.

## Practice questions

> [!question]- mkt-vi-161 | What is the fundamental difference between first-level thinking and second-level thinking?
> First-level thinking is simplistic and consensus-driven; second-level thinking is deep, probabilistic, and evaluates how consensus expectations are priced into the asset.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-162 | Why is being contrarian alone insufficient to generate superior investment returns?
> Being different is not enough; your variant perception must be different AND analytically superior to consensus reality.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-163 | What are the two psychological extremes between which the market pendulum swings?
> Between euphoria and depression, greed and fear, credulity and skepticism, and risk tolerance and risk aversion.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-164 | What are the three stages of a bull market identified by Howard Marks?
> 1. When a few forward-looking people realize things will improve.
> 2. When most see improvement occurring.
> 3. When everyone concludes things will get better forever.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-165 | What are the three stages of a bear market?
> 1. When prudent investors recognize perfection cannot last.
> 2. When most recognize deterioration is underway.
> 3. When everyone believes things can only get worse.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-166 | Why is the Credit Cycle more volatile and impactful than the underlying economic GDP cycle?
> Because lender emotions amplify economic swings: easy credit fuels over-investment during booms, and sudden lender panic cuts off liquidity during downturns.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-167 | What did Howard Marks mean by 'the worst loans are made at the best of times'?
> During peak economic euphoria, abundant capital forces lenders to lower underwriting standards, drop protective covenants, and finance fragile borrowers.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-168 | What is a 'covenant-lite' loan?
> A debt agreement that lacks traditional financial maintenance covenants (such as debt-to-EBITDA limits), signaling reckless lender complacency.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-169 | How does Howard Marks define true financial risk?
> The probability of permanent capital loss, rejecting academic definitions based on price volatility or standard deviation.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-170 | What is the Paradox of Risk?
> Risk is highest when everyone believes there is no risk (prices are high, buffers vanish); risk is lowest when everyone perceives extreme risk (prices collapse).

> [!question]- mkt-vi-171 | What does it mean to 'take the temperature of the market'?
> Measuring investor sentiment, credit spreads, new issue quality, and valuation multiples to determine whether to adopt a defensive or aggressive posture.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-172 | What is the concept of 'winning by not losing'?
> Focusing on risk control, avoiding unforced errors, eliminating low-quality debt, and avoiding overpriced fads so the portfolio compounds safely over decades.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-173 | What is Oaktree Capital's primary investment philosophy motto?
> 'If we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves.'

> [!question]- mkt-vi-174 | How does an investor create positive asymmetry in a portfolio?
> Generating performance that captures significant upside in bull markets while suffering far less downside in bear markets.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-175 | Why does Howard Marks argue that macro forecasting is largely useless?
> Because accurate consensus forecasts are already priced in, and non-consensus forecasts are rarely correct enough to generate reliable edge.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-176 | What should an investor do when high-yield credit spreads compress below 300 bps?
> Reduce credit risk, eliminate low-grade debt, accumulate cash, and adopt a strictly defensive portfolio posture.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-177 | What should an investor do when high-yield credit spreads widen past 1,000 bps during a panic?
> Aggressively deploy dry powder into senior secured and fulcrum distressed debt, capturing equity-like returns with structural debt protections.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-178 | What is the relationship between price paid and the risk of an investment?
> High-quality assets bought at high prices carry immense risk; low-quality assets bought at deep discounts carry minimal risk and high prospective return.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-179 | What is 'skepticism' in second-level thinking?
> Demanding evidence when Wall Street claims 'this time is different' during a boom, and demanding evidence when Wall Street claims 'the world is ending' in a crash.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-180 | Why is the fear of missing out (FOMO) so destructive to investment discipline?
> It compels investors to abandon risk control and buy overvalued securities at cyclical tops simply because they see peers getting rich.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-181 | What is the difference between risk control and risk avoidance?
> Risk avoidance means holding only Treasury bills, achieving zero real return; risk control means consciously taking mispriced risks where you are compensated.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-182 | How does Howard Marks view diversification across asset classes?
> Diversification protects against ignorance, but true risk control comes from knowing individual securities deeply and refusing to overpay.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-183 | What role does luck play in short-term investment performance?
> A massive role; a reckless strategy can appear brilliant for years during a bull market until an unexpected regime shift exposes its fatal flaws.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-184 | What is 'confusing brains with a bull market'?
> Attributing portfolio gains to managerial genius when returns were simply the mathematical result of taking excessive leverage in a rising tide.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-185 | Why does the market pendulum spend so little time at the rational midpoint?
> Because human emotions are self-reinforcing, driving momentum past equilibrium until economic friction forces a violent reversal.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-186 | What is the significance of the phrase 'being too far ahead of your time is indistinguishable from being wrong'?
> A value investor can correctly identify a bubble, but if market euphoria persists for three more years, the investor faces career risk from impatient clients.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-187 | How did Oaktree Capital navigate the 2008 Global Financial Crisis?
> Having built massive cash reserves by avoiding subprime debt, Oaktree deployed $500 million per week into distressed debt during the panic, reaping huge gains.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-188 | What is the 'cost of being wrong' in second-level analysis?
> Evaluating the catastrophic downside if your non-consensus thesis fails, ensuring that survival is guaranteed regardless of outcome.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-189 | Why do credit cycles reset more cleanly than equity cycles?
> Because debt has contractual maturity dates; defaults and Chapter 11 restructurings legally extinguish excess debt and transfer ownership to creditors.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-190 | What is the primary characteristic of an investor who has mastered market cycles?
> The emotional independence to be cautious when others are greedily complacent, and aggressive when others are terrified.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-191 | What is an 'unforced error' in portfolio management?
> Buying an asset without understanding its debt covenants, chasing momentum at peak multiples, or using margin debt that forces liquidation.

> [!question]- mkt-vi-192 | What is Howard Marks' ultimate takeaway on achieving superior investment results?
> You cannot do what everybody else is doing and expect to outperform; you must think differently, manage risk obsessively, and exploit cycles.
