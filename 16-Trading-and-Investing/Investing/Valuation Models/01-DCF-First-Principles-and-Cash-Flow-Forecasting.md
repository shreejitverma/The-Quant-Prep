# DCF First Principles and Cash Flow Forecasting

## TL;DR

- Intrinsic enterprise value is strictly the present value of all future cash flows expected to be generated by the business, discounted at the opportunity cost of capital.
- Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF) values the entire operating enterprise independent of capital structure and discounts at the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC).
- Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE) values equity claims directly after debt service and discounts at the Cost of Equity ($K_e$).
- Cash flow forecasting requires modeling revenue drivers (volume $\times$ price), operating leverage, working capital intensity, and capex requirements.
- The mid-year discounting convention accurately reflects that cash flows accrue continuously throughout the operating year rather than as a lump sum on December 31.
- Terminal value typically accounts for 60% to 80% of total DCF value, making sensitivity analysis and explicit fade modeling mandatory.

## Learning objectives

- Derive and contrast Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF) and Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE) from operating fundamentals.
- Build multi-stage explicit forecasting models linking revenue growth, operating margins, working capital, and capital expenditures.
- Apply the mid-year discounting convention and explain its mathematical impact on present value.
- Reconcile Enterprise Value to Equity Value by accounting for debt, excess cash, minority interests, and non-operating assets.
- Execute rigorous sensitivity and scenario analyses across growth rates, margins, and discount rates.

## Core concepts

### 1. The Philosophical Foundations of DCF

The value of any financial asset is governed by John Burr Williams' (1938) Law of Conservation of Value:

$$V_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{\text{CF}_t}{(1 + r)^t}$$

In corporate valuation, cash flows do not extend uniformly into infinity under static assumptions.
Modern DCF splits valuation into two distinct regimes:
1. **The Explicit Forecast Period ($t = 1 \dots N$)**: Detailed line-by-line operating forecasts during the company's Competitive Advantage Period (CAP), typically 5 to 10 years.
2. **Terminal Value ($t > N$)**: The present value of all cash flows beyond year $N$, assuming the business matures into stable growth at or below nominal GDP expansion.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                    TWO-STAGE DCF VALUATION TIMELINE                   |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
| Year 0        Year 1        Year 2      ...   Year N     Year N+1 ... |
| Today         Stage 1: Explicit Forecast (CAP)           Stage 2: TV  |
|   |             |             |                 |             |       |
|   |         FCFF_1        FCFF_2            FCFF_N        TV_N        |
|   |             |             |                 |             |       |
|   +<--- PV -----+<--- PV -----+<------ PV ------+<--- PV -----+       |
|   |                                                                   |
|   v                                                                   |
| ENTERPRISE VALUE = Sum of PV(Explicit FCFF) + PV(Terminal Value)      |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### 2. FCFF vs FCFE: Two Consistent Valuation Paths

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                      FCFF VS FCFE VALUATION MATRIX                    |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
| Attribute             | FCFF (Firm / Enterprise) | FCFE (Equity)      |
+-----------------------+--------------------------+--------------------+
| Cash Flow Definition  | Cash available to ALL    | Cash available to  |
|                       | capital providers        | common equity only |
| Formula from EBIT     | EBIT*(1-t) + D&A - Capex | Net Income + D&A   |
|                       | - Delta OWC              | - Capex - Delta OWC|
|                       |                          | + Net Borrowing    |
| Discount Rate         | WACC                     | Cost of Equity (Ke)|
| Resulting Value       | Enterprise Value (EV)    | Equity Value       |
| Path to Share Price   | EV - Debt - Pfd + Cash   | FCFE / Shares      |
|                       | / Diluted Shares         |                    |
+-----------------------+--------------------------+--------------------+
```

#### Reconciling Enterprise Value to Equity Value (The Equity Bridge)

To arrive at Common Equity Value from Enterprise Value (EV):

$$\text{Common Equity Value} = \text{Enterprise Value} - \text{Total Debt} - \text{Preferred Stock} - \text{Non-Controlling Interests} + \text{Cash \& Liquid Investments} + \text{Associates / Non-Operating Assets}$$

$$\text{Value Per Share} = \frac{\text{Common Equity Value}}{\text{Diluted Shares Outstanding}}$$

Diluted shares must include in-the-money executive stock options and restricted stock units calculated via the Treasury Stock Method.

### 3. Forecasting Architecture: Operating Drivers

A credible DCF never projects cash flows by slapping arbitrary percentage growth rates onto historical totals.
Forecasting must decompose into economic operating drivers:

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                    THE 4 FORECASTING ENGINE PILLARS                   |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. REVENUE ENGINE:                                                    |
|    - Price vs Volume breakdown (Units sold x Average Selling Price)   |
|    - Total Addressable Market (TAM) x Market Share penetration        |
|    - Segment and geographic expansion curves                          |
| 2. MARGIN ENGINE:                                                     |
|    - Fixed vs Variable cost structures                                |
|    - Operating leverage: incremental EBIT margin vs historical margin |
|    - Economies of scale in COGS and SG&A                              |
| 3. WORKING CAPITAL ENGINE:                                            |
|    - Tied explicitly to days metrics: DSO, DIO, DPO                   |
|    - Operating Working Capital as a percentage of incremental revenue |
| 4. REINVESTMENT ENGINE:                                               |
|    - Maintenance capex vs Growth capex                               |
|    - Capital turnover efficiency: Dollar of sales per dollar of PP&E  |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### 4. Mid-Year Discounting Mechanics

In financial statement accounting, cash flows are recorded annually.
In reality, corporations collect customer cash and pay vendor invoices every single day throughout the year.
Assuming all cash arrives on the final day of the fiscal year ($t = 1.0, 2.0, \dots$) systematically underestimates present value:

$$\text{End-of-Year PV Factor}: \quad \text{DF}_t = \frac{1}{(1 + \text{WACC})^t}$$

$$\text{Mid-Year PV Factor}: \quad \text{DF}_{t, \text{mid}} = \frac{1}{(1 + \text{WACC})^{t - 0.5}}$$

For a company with an 8% WACC, mid-year discounting increases explicit present value by approximately $(1 + 0.08)^{0.5} - 1 \approx 3.92\%$.

### 5. Terminal Value Formulations

Two rigorous methodologies govern terminal value:

#### Method 1: The Gordon Growth Model (Perpetuity Growth)
Assumes the business reaches a steady state growing at a constant long-term rate $g$ into perpetuity:

$$\text{TV}_N = \frac{\text{FCFF}_{N+1}}{\text{WACC} - g} = \frac{\text{FCFF}_N \times (1 + g)}{\text{WACC} - g}$$

Constraints on $g$:
- $g$ cannot exceed the nominal risk-free rate of the economy in which the firm operates; assuming $g > \text{GDP growth}$ implies the company will eventually swallow the entire global economy.
- Typically, $g$ is set between 2.0% and 3.0% for developed economies.

#### Method 2: The Exit Multiple Method
Applies an EV/EBITDA multiple to final-year normalized earnings:

$$\text{TV}_N = \text{Normalized EBITDA}_N \times \text{Exit Multiple}$$

Cross-check invariant:
Every exit multiple implies an exact perpetuity growth rate:

$$\text{Exit Multiple} = \frac{(1 - \text{Reinvestment Rate})}{\text{WACC} - g}$$

If an analyst assumes an exit multiple of $15\times$ EBITDA with an 8% WACC, they are implicitly assuming an aggressive perpetuity growth rate that must be checked for sanity.

## Worked numerical case study

Build an explicit 5-year DCF model for Horizon Software Inc:
- Year 0 Revenue: $1,000M
- Year 0 EBIT: $200M (Operating margin: 20.0%)
- Tax rate ($t$): 25.0%
- WACC: 8.5%
- Long-term perpetuity growth rate ($g$): 2.5%
- Balance Sheet today: Total Debt = $300M; Cash = $100M; Diluted Shares = 100M.

### Forecast Assumptions (Years 1 to 5):
- Revenue Growth: Year 1 = 15%, Year 2 = 12%, Year 3 = 10%, Year 4 = 8%, Year 5 = 6%.
- Operating Margin: Expands by 100 bps per year from 20% to 25% due to operating leverage.
- D&A: 5.0% of revenue.
- Capex: 6.0% of revenue.
- Net Working Capital Requirement: 4.0% of incremental revenue.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                        5-YEAR EXPLICIT FORECAST SCHEDULE ($M)                     |
+------------------------+---------+---------+---------+---------+---------+--------+
| Metric                 | Year 0  | Year 1  | Year 2  | Year 3  | Year 4  | Year 5 |
+------------------------+---------+---------+---------+---------+---------+--------+
| Revenue                | 1,000.0 | 1,150.0 | 1,288.0 | 1,416.8 | 1,530.1 | 1,621.9|
| Revenue Growth         | -       | 15.0%   | 12.0%   | 10.0%   | 8.0%    | 6.0%   |
| EBIT Margin            | 20.0%   | 21.0%   | 22.0%   | 23.0%   | 24.0%   | 25.0%  |
| EBIT                   | 200.0   | 241.5   | 283.4   | 325.9   | 367.2   | 405.5  |
| Taxes (25%)            | 50.0    | 60.4    | 70.8    | 81.5    | 91.8    | 101.4  |
| NOPAT                  | 150.0   | 181.1   | 212.5   | 244.4   | 275.4   | 304.1  |
| + D&A (5% Rev)         | 50.0    | 57.5    | 64.4    | 70.8    | 76.5    | 81.1   |
| - Capex (6% Rev)       | 60.0    | 69.0    | 77.3    | 85.0    | 91.8    | 97.3   |
| - Delta OWC (4% dRev)  | -       | 6.0     | 5.5     | 5.2     | 4.5     | 3.7    |
+------------------------+---------+---------+---------+---------+---------+--------+
| FCFF                   | -       | 163.6   | 194.1   | 225.0   | 255.6   | 284.2  |
+------------------------+---------+---------+---------+---------+---------+--------+
| Mid-Year Period (t-0.5)| -       | 0.5     | 1.5     | 2.5     | 3.5     | 4.5    |
| Discount Factor (8.5%) | -       | 0.9600  | 0.8848  | 0.8155  | 0.7516  | 0.6927 |
| Present Value of FCFF  | -       | 157.1   | 171.7   | 183.5   | 192.1   | 196.9  |
+------------------------+---------+---------+---------+---------+---------+--------+
```

### Step 1: Sum Explicit PV of Cash Flows
$$\text{PV(Explicit FCFF)} = 157.1 + 171.7 + 183.5 + 192.1 + 196.9 = \$901.3\text{M}$$

### Step 2: Compute Terminal Value
Year 6 FCFF = $\text{FCFF}_5 \times (1 + g) = 284.2 \times (1 + 0.025) = \$291.31\text{M}$

$$\text{Terminal Value at Year 5} = \frac{291.31}{\text{WACC} - g} = \frac{291.31}{0.085 - 0.025} = \frac{291.31}{0.060} = \$4,855.17\text{M}$$

Discount Terminal Value to present (using end of Year 5 discount factor, $1 / (1.085)^5 = 0.6651$):
$$\text{PV of Terminal Value} = 4,855.17 \times 0.6651 = \$3,229.17\text{M}$$

### Step 3: Compute Enterprise Value and Bridge to Equity
$$\text{Enterprise Value} = \text{PV(Explicit)} + \text{PV(TV)} = 901.3 + 3,229.2 = \$4,130.5\text{M}$$
(Notice: Terminal Value represents $3,229.2 / 4,130.5 = 78.2\%$ of total Enterprise Value).

Equity Bridge:
- Less Total Debt: -$300.0M
- Plus Cash & Equivalents: +$100.0M
- Net Debt: -$200.0M

$$\text{Common Equity Value} = 4,130.5 - 200.0 = \$3,930.5\text{M}$$

$$\text{Value Per Share} = \frac{\$3,930.5\text{M}}{100\text{M shares}} = \$39.31$$

## Practice questions

> [!question]- mkt-val-001 | What is the fundamental principle of intrinsic valuation?
> The value of any financial asset is the present value of all its expected future cash flows discounted at a rate reflecting their risk.

> [!question]- mkt-val-002 | What is the formula for Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF) starting from EBIT?
> FCFF = EBIT * (1 - t) + D&A - Capex - Delta Operating Net Working Capital.

> [!question]- mkt-val-003 | What discount rate must be matched with Free Cash Flow to Firm?
> The Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC), because FCFF represents cash available to all debt and equity capital providers.

> [!question]- mkt-val-004 | What discount rate must be matched with Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE)?
> The Cost of Equity (Ke), because FCFE represents cash flow available strictly to common shareholders after debt service.

> [!question]- mkt-val-005 | What is the formula for FCFE starting from Net Income?
> FCFE = Net Income + D&A - Capex - Delta Working Capital + Net Borrowing (Debt Issued - Debt Repaid).

> [!question]- mkt-val-006 | What is the Equity Bridge formula converting Enterprise Value to Equity Value?
> Equity Value = Enterprise Value - Total Debt - Preferred Stock - Minority Interest + Cash and Liquid Non-Operating Assets.

> [!question]- mkt-val-007 | Why does the mid-year discounting convention increase present value compared to end-of-year discounting?
> Because cash flows occur continuously throughout the year; discounting from mid-year points (t - 0.5) reflects earlier cash receipt than assuming year-end lumps.

> [!question]- mkt-val-008 | What is the formula for Terminal Value under the Gordon Growth Model?
> TV_N = [FCFF_N * (1 + g)] / (WACC - g), where g is the perpetual organic growth rate.

> [!question]- mkt-val-009 | What is the absolute economic ceiling on the perpetuity growth rate (g)?
> The sustainable nominal GDP growth rate of the economy in which the company operates (typically 2% to 3%).

> [!question]- mkt-val-010 | What typical percentage of Enterprise Value is represented by Terminal Value in a 5-year DCF?
> Between 60% and 80%, highlighting the critical importance of terminal growth and discount rate assumptions.

> [!question]- mkt-val-011 | What is operating leverage in cash flow forecasting?
> The degree to which revenue growth translates into operating income growth, determined by the ratio of fixed costs to variable costs.

> [!question]- mkt-val-012 | Why must stock-based compensation (SBC) be treated with caution in DCF projections?
> While non-cash on the cash flow statement, SBC dilutes future share count; omitting SBC expense without increasing future share dilution overstates value.

> [!question]- mkt-val-013 | What is the Treasury Stock Method for diluted shares?
> It assumes proceeds received from exercising in-the-money options are used to repurchase shares in the open market at the prevailing stock price.

> [!question]- mkt-val-014 | Why should non-operating assets (such as an art collection or idle land) be added to DCF value?
> Because DCF projects cash flows only from operating assets; surplus non-operating assets have realizable liquidation value that belongs to equity holders.

> [!question]- mkt-val-015 | How do minority interests (non-controlling interests) affect the Equity Bridge?
> Minority interests represent third-party equity claims on consolidated subsidiaries, and must be subtracted from Enterprise Value to isolate parent equity.

> [!question]- mkt-val-016 | What is the Exit Multiple method for calculating Terminal Value?
> Multiplying the final forecast year's normalized EBITDA by an industry-appropriate enterprise multiple (EV/EBITDA).

> [!question]- mkt-val-017 | How do you calculate the implied perpetuity growth rate from an exit EBITDA multiple?
> Solve for g in the perpetuity equation equating TV to EBITDA * Multiple, checking whether the resulting g is economically realistic.

> [!question]- mkt-val-018 | What is the difference between top-down and bottom-up revenue forecasting?
> Top-down forecasts market share of an expanding TAM; bottom-up forecasts capacity, store counts, sales reps, unit volumes, and pricing tiers.

> [!question]- mkt-val-019 | Why does inflation distort historical-cost depreciation in DCF models?
> Historical depreciation understates the true replacement cost of aging physical assets, creating a persistent cash drain where capex exceeds D&A.

> [!question]- mkt-val-020 | What is the Competitive Advantage Period (CAP) and how does it determine explicit DCF forecast length?
> CAP is the duration over which a firm can earn returns above its cost of capital; the explicit forecast period should match the estimated CAP.

> [!question]- mkt-val-021 | What is a multi-stage DCF model?
> A model featuring an initial high-growth stage, a transition stage where growth and margins fade toward industry averages, and a terminal steady state.

> [!question]- mkt-val-022 | How does an increase in Accounts Receivable affect forecasted FCFF?
> An increase in AR represents uncollected credit revenue, resulting in a cash deduction (-Delta AR) under working capital reinvestment.

> [!question]- mkt-val-023 | What happens to working capital cash flow when revenue growth decelerates?
> Working capital expansion slows, releasing cash flow and temporarily causing FCFF to appear high even as business momentum cools.

> [!question]- mkt-val-024 | Why is an unadjusted trailing twelve months (TTM) cash flow an unreliable base for DCF projections?
> TTM numbers often contain one-off working capital swings, non-recurring legal charges, or cyclical peak earnings that must be normalized.

> [!question]- mkt-val-025 | What is capital intensity in long-range DCF modeling?
> The amount of capital expenditure and working capital required to generate each incremental dollar of revenue ($Delta Capital / Delta Revenue).

> [!question]- mkt-val-026 | If a company has zero debt, what is the mathematical relationship between FCFF and FCFE?
> FCFF equals FCFE exactly, and WACC equals the Cost of Equity.

> [!question]- mkt-val-027 | Why does debt financing create a tax shield in FCFF valuation?
> Interest expense is tax-deductible, reducing corporate income tax liability, which is incorporated directly into the lower after-tax cost of debt in WACC.

> [!question]- mkt-val-028 | How should operating leases be incorporated into Enterprise Value?
> Under ASC 842, present value of operating leases is recognized on the balance sheet as lease liabilities and must be included as debt in the equity bridge.

> [!question]- mkt-val-029 | What is scenario analysis in DCF modeling?
> Evaluating valuation outcomes under discrete macro and operational narratives (e.g. Bull, Base, Bear) with correlated assumptions.

> [!question]- mkt-val-030 | What is Monte Carlo simulation in a DCF context?
> Assigning probability distributions to key drivers (growth, margins, WACC) and running thousands of iterations to generate a distribution of intrinsic values.

> [!question]- mkt-val-031 | What is the danger of circularity when modeling interest expense based on debt balances?
> Interest expense depends on debt, which depends on cash flow, which depends on net income, which depends on interest; resolved by iterative solver or average balances.

> [!question]- mkt-val-032 | What is the ultimate limitation of a standalone DCF valuation?
> It is extremely sensitive to long-term terminal value assumptions, requiring triangulation with reverse DCF and market expectations.
