## Core intuition

Real estate earns its own valuation machinery because the asset itself breaks the assumptions that public market valuation relies on.
Properties are heterogeneous building and land assets trading in fragmented local markets, bought and sold infrequently with high transaction costs (V8 p.64).
Each property has a unique location, size, age, and amenity set, so there is no continuous market price, only appraisals and occasional transactions (V8 p.64).
Unit values are high and management is intensive, so investors reach the asset class through a menu of vehicles rather than through one share class.
The value of developed land comes from its existing and expected future economic uses, which makes the tenant's economics, not just the building, the object of analysis (V8 p.63, p.64).

> [!abstract] Idea 1: NOI is the module's EBITDA, and everything hangs on it
> Net operating income is effective gross income minus operating expenses minus a property maintenance allowance, measured before financing costs and income taxes (V8 p.66).
> It plays the role EBITDA and FCFF play for companies: the numerator of the direct capitalization value, the cash flow stream in the DCF, the numerator of DSC, and the income leg of index returns (V8 p.66, p.69, p.93, p.102).
> Every question in this module is either about how a lease or expense term changes NOI, or about which rate to divide NOI by.

The lease contract decides how stable NOI is.
A single tenant on a long lease who reimburses property taxes, insurance, and a capped amount of servicing (a net style lease, as in the Wallonia warehouse) leaves the owner exposed only to costs above the recovery cap (V8 p.66, p.67).
An owner who absorbs operating costs (a gross style lease, as in the Pinebranch multifamily property) bears the full expense risk, and with many short leases also bears vacancy, collection, and concession deductions (V8 p.84, p.85).
Rent itself need not be flat: step-up clauses pre-specify future increases, indexed rents track an observed variable such as CPI, and overage rent pays the retail landlord a share of tenant sales above a breakeven level (V8 p.81).
When the holding period exceeds the lease term, income carries rollover risk, the chance of losing a tenant and forgoing rent until a new one is found (V8 p.82).

Capital structure is the second recurring theme, because real estate is bought with a cash down payment plus a mortgage secured by the property (V8 p.68).
Lenders monitor two ratios: LTV, mortgage principal over current property value, and DSC, NOI over debt service, both of which appear in loan covenants (V8 p.68, p.69).
Equity investors track the levered cash-on-cash return, the equity dividend rate, which is pre-tax cash flow (NOI minus debt service) over the initial equity (V8 p.70).
After-tax measures subtract taxes computed on NOI minus interest minus depreciation, where depreciation runs on the depreciable base, cost plus improvements excluding land, because land has an infinite life (V8 p.71).

> [!abstract] Idea 2: the four quadrants organize every vehicle
> Real estate investment forms sit in a two-by-two grid of private versus public and equity versus debt (V8 p.75).
> Private equity means direct ownership (sole, joint ventures, limited partnerships) or indirect ownership through real estate funds and private REITs; public equity means listed REITs, REOCs, and fund wrappers such as ETFs and UCITS (V8 p.75).
> Private debt means mortgages, construction loans, and mezzanine debt; public debt means MBS, CMBS, CMOs, covered bonds, mortgage REITs, and mortgage ETFs (V8 p.75).
> Risk and return rise along the strategy spectrum from senior debt through core, core-plus, and value-add to opportunistic (new development, mezzanine debt, distressed situations), with income-producing core assets bond-like and development equity-like (V8 p.64, p.65).

REITs deserve one extra sentence because they recur in the practice problems.
REITs primarily own and operate rental properties or purchase mortgages, and they must distribute nearly all earnings to avoid corporate income tax, whereas REOCs are ordinary taxable corporations with few restrictions (V8 p.75).
REIT performance is measured by funds from operations, net income plus depreciation and amortization minus net gains from property sales, which strips out the non-cash depreciation that dominates real estate accounting income (V8 p.76).

Macro drivers move the whole sector: GDP growth, job creation, and wage growth lift demand for space, while credit conditions matter more than for most industries because properties are heavily debt financed (V8 p.78, p.79).
Because construction takes years, supply reacts to demand with a lag, and that lag generates the real estate cycle: recovery (no new construction, occupancy and prices depressed), expansion (rising occupancy, rents, prices, and new construction), oversupply (pipeline completions glut the market as growth softens), and recession (occupancy and prices fall, construction stops) (V8 p.79, p.80).
Over the cycle NOI and DSC bottom in recovery, rise in expansion, peak in oversupply, and fall in recession, while LTV moves inversely, peaking in recovery and bottoming in oversupply (V8 p.80).
Property-level outcomes still depend on local factors: the business climate, major employers, infrastructure, schools, taxes, and zoning (V8 p.81).
In a portfolio, real estate offers current income, capital appreciation, an inflation hedge from rising rents and prices, diversification from historically low correlation with stocks and bonds, and tax benefits such as accelerated depreciation and pass-through REIT structures, though public vehicles correlate more with listed markets in the short run (V8 p.81, p.82).

Property types differ in drivers and lease structure (V8 p.86, p.87).
Multifamily demand tracks local employment, income, and the cost of owning versus renting, with short leases and tenant protections (V8 p.84).
Office demand tracks service employment, with long leases, anchor tenants de-risking development, and a structural headwind from remote work (V8 p.86).
Industrial and warehouse demand rose structurally with e-commerce, but purpose-built facilities are hard to convert (V8 p.86).
Retail depends on proximity to workers and residents, uses overage rent to share in tenant sales, and faces a structural shift to online sales that forces conversions (V8 p.81, p.86).
Hospitality is the most cyclical and management intensive, spanning business hotels, convention centers, and destination resorts with high fixed costs (V8 p.86, p.87).
Specialty segments (self-storage, senior housing, data centers, cell towers) each carry their own lease and demand quirks, such as month-to-month self-storage leases that reprice fast in both directions (V8 p.87).

Because there is no exchange price, due diligence substitutes for market transparency: market review and outlook, current lease review, future lease outlook, financial review, documentation review, and property inspection with service agreements (V8 p.91, p.92).
Everything except documentation review (a legal title, lien, tax, and zoning check) feeds the NOI forecast (V8 p.92, p.101).

> [!abstract] Idea 3: one discount rate, three approaches, and the cap rate is just r minus g
> The income approach discounts NOI: directly capitalize a single stabilized NOI at the cap rate, or run a multi-year DCF with a terminal value from the terminal cap rate (V8 p.93, p.94, p.95).
> The cap rate equals the required return minus constant NOI growth, so it is observable from comparables as NOI over price, and a lower cap rate (faster growth or lower risk) means a higher value (V8 p.93, p.96).
> The cost approach caps value at replacement cost less depreciation adjustments plus land, and the sales comparison approach prices the subject off adjusted comparable transactions; income dominates for large income-producing assets, sales comparison for homogeneous housing (V8 p.96, p.98, p.100).

Indexes close the module.
Private market indexes are built from appraisals or transactions and are generally not investable, while public REIT and real estate debt indexes are investable (V8 p.102).
Appraisal-based indexes (GREFI and similar) compute holding period returns from NOI minus capex plus appraised value change over beginning appraised value (V8 p.102).
Appraisals lag transactions in both rising and falling markets and are not refreshed every quarter, so the index is smoothed: volatility is understated, correlations with other asset classes are understated, and Sharpe ratios are overstated, which biases asset allocation toward real estate (V8 p.104, p.105).
The fixes are to unsmooth the series by reversing an autoregressive smoothing model or to use transaction-based indexes, either repeat sales (same property sold twice) or hedonic (regression controls for property characteristics), which lead appraisal indexes but add statistical noise (V8 p.103, p.104, p.105).
On the debt side, MBS indexes carry prepayment risk that shortens duration when rates fall and extends it when rates rise, while covered bonds, with dual recourse, collateral substitution, and prepayment penalties, deliver stable low-risk returns (V8 p.107, p.108).

## Formulas and derivations

**Net operating income** (V8 p.66)
$$
\text{NOI} = \text{Effective gross income} - \text{Operating expenses} - \text{Property maintenance allowance}
$$
^f-08-02-1

- Effective gross income: gross rent (base rent per unit area times rentable area) plus expense recoveries, pass-through costs, and other income, less vacancy and concession deductions (V8 p.66).
- Operating expenses: fixed costs (taxes, insurance, service, repairs) plus variable costs (utilities); the maintenance allowance covers capex needed to maintain the current economic use, not upgrades that change the use (V8 p.66, p.67).
- NOI is measured before financing costs and income taxes, the property-level analog of EBITDA for a company (V8 p.66).
- It is the input to direct capitalization, DCF, DSC, and index returns, so build it line by line before touching any rate.

**Loan-to-value ratio** (V8 p.69)
$$
\text{LTV} = \frac{\text{Mortgage debt outstanding}}{\text{Current property value}}
$$
^f-08-02-2

- Both terms in currency; LTV falls as principal amortizes or the property appreciates, and a lower LTV means a higher owner equity cushion (V8 p.69).
- Lenders impose a maximum LTV at origination and in covenants; the ratio moves with the appraisal in the denominator even when debt is unchanged (V8 p.69).

**Debt service coverage ratio** (V8 p.69)
$$
\text{DSC} = \frac{\text{Net operating income}}{\text{Debt service}}
$$
^f-08-02-3

- Debt service: the full periodic installment, interest plus scheduled principal (V8 p.69, p.70).
- Higher operating costs cut NOI and DSC one for one in the numerator, which is why covenants pair DSC with LTV (V8 p.69, p.70).

> [!tip] Calculator
> Wallonia's mortgage payment: `20 N, 4 I/Y, 3000000 PV, 0 FV, CPT PMT` gives -220,745.25.

**Equity dividend rate** (V8 p.70)
$$
\text{Equity dividend rate} = \frac{\text{Pre-tax cash flow}}{\text{Property purchase price} - \text{Mortgage loan}}
$$
^f-08-02-4

- Denominator: the investor's initial equity, price minus loan; the measure is a first-year levered cash-on-cash return (V8 p.70).
- Cash-based and pre-tax; it ignores taxes and any capital gain or loss from sale (V8 p.70).

**Pre-tax cash flow** (V8 p.70)
$$
\text{Pre-tax cash flow} = \text{NOI} - \text{Debt service}
$$
^f-08-02-5

- The equity holder's cash claim after the lender is paid; leverage means a fixed subtraction, so percentage swings in NOI are amplified at the equity line (V8 p.70).

**After-tax cash flow** (V8 p.71)
$$
\text{After-tax cash flow} = \text{Pre-tax cash flow} - \text{Taxes}
$$
^f-08-02-6

- Taxes come from formula 7, not from applying the tax rate to pre-tax cash flow; principal repayments are not deductible, depreciation is (V8 p.71).

**Taxes on property income** (V8 p.71)
$$
\text{Taxes} = t \times (\text{NOI} - \text{Interest expense} - \text{Depreciation expense})
$$
^f-08-02-7

- $t$: the investor's tax rate; the bracket is taxable income (V8 p.71).
- Depreciation runs straight line over the useful life on the depreciable base, construction or acquisition cost plus improvements, excluding land because land has an infinite life (V8 p.71).
- A lower land share means a larger depreciable base, larger depreciation, lower taxes, and higher after-tax cash flow for the same NOI (V8 p.71, p.78).

**Funds from operations** (V8 p.76)
$$
\text{FFO} = \text{Net income} + \text{Depreciation} + \text{Amortization} - \text{Net gains from property sales}
$$
^f-08-02-8

- The standard REIT performance measure; it adds back non-cash depreciation and amortization and strips one-off sale gains so the metric tracks recurring rental operations (V8 p.76).

**Gross potential rental income** (V8 p.84)
$$
\text{GPRI} = \text{Market rent} \times \text{Rentable space}
$$
^f-08-02-9

- Current market rent at full occupancy; the top line of a multifamily pro forma (V8 p.84).
- Subtract loss to lease (market rents minus current in-place leases) to get gross rental income, then vacancy, collection, and concessions to get net rental income (V8 p.85).
- A positive loss to lease means in-place rents are below market, an upside signal; a negative one means leases were signed above today's market, an oversupply signal (V8 p.118).

**Direct capitalization value** (V8 p.93)
$$
\text{Property value} = \frac{\text{Expected NOI}}{\text{Capitalization rate}}
$$
^f-08-02-10

- A perpetuity present value: one stabilized NOI divided by the cap rate (V8 p.93).
- Appropriate when NOI is consistent and relationships among cash flows and expenses are stable; if NOI is temporarily distorted, capitalize a normalized (stabilized) NOI instead (V8 p.94).

> [!tip] Calculator
> Wallonia: `406750 / (0.125 - 0.02) =` gives 3,873,809.52; no TVM keys needed, just guard the r minus g subtraction.

**Capitalization rate from required return and growth** (V8 p.93)
$$
\text{Capitalization rate} = r - g
$$
^f-08-02-11

- $r$: required return on similar properties, risk-free rate plus a property-specific risk premium; $g$: constant NOI growth assumed to continue indefinitely (V8 p.93).
- Direct consequence of the constant-growth perpetuity $V = \text{NOI}/(r-g)$: higher risk raises $r$ and lowers value, faster growth raises $g$, lowers the cap rate, and raises value (V8 p.93).
- $g < 0$ signals a property near the end of its useful life with declining NOI (V8 p.96).

**DCF property value** (V8 p.94)
$$
\text{Property value} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\text{NOI}_{t+i}}{(1+r)^i} + \frac{\text{Terminal value}}{(1+r)^n}
$$
^f-08-02-12

- $n$: the holding period in years; every NOI and the terminal value are discounted at the same required return $r$, never at the cap rate (V8 p.94, p.115).
- Preferred over direct capitalization when the analyst has visibility into changing NOI dynamics, such as a downside year followed by recovery (V8 p.94).

**Terminal value by capitalized NOI** (V8 p.95)
$$
\text{Terminal value} = \frac{\text{NOI}_n(1+g)}{r - g}
$$
^f-08-02-13

- The year-after-sale NOI, $\text{NOI}_{n+1} = \text{NOI}_n(1+g)$, divided by the terminal cap rate $r - g$ (V8 p.95).
- The going-in cap rate uses first-year income; the terminal cap rate uses expected income after the anticipated sale, and the two differ whenever early NOI is not representative (V8 p.96).

**Capitalization rate from comparables** (V8 p.96)
$$
\text{Capitalization rate} = \frac{\text{Expected NOI}}{\text{Property value}}
$$
^f-08-02-14

- Rearranged formula 10; observable from the income and sale prices of comparable properties (V8 p.96).
- Comparable to a bond's current yield or the inverse of EV/EBITDA: an income return that excludes capital gains (V8 p.96).

**Holding period return for property indexes** (V8 p.102)
$$
\text{HPR} = \frac{\text{NOI} - \text{Capex} + (\text{Ending market value} - \text{Beginning market value})}{\text{Beginning market value}}
$$
^f-08-02-15

- Beginning and ending values are appraisals in an appraisal-based index; the HPR is a single-period IRR as if bought at the start and sold at the end (V8 p.102).
- The income leg (NOI minus capex) is not a distribution to fund or REIT investors, unlike dividends in an equity total return, which limits comparability across asset classes (V8 p.103, p.109).

**Appraisal smoothing model** (V8 p.105)
$$
R_t^{*} = a R_t + (1-a) R_{t-1}^{*}
$$
^f-08-02-16

- $R_t^{*}$: appraisal index return in period $t$; $R_t$: true (actual) return; $a \in [0,1]$: the speed at which actual returns show up in appraisals, higher $a$ meaning faster adjustment (V8 p.105).
- A first-order autoregressive form: appraisers anchor on lagged prices, so reported returns are a weighted average of the truth and last period's report (V8 p.105).

**Unsmoothed actual return** (V8 p.105)
$$
R_t = \frac{R_t^{*} - (1-a) R_{t-1}^{*}}{a}
$$
^f-08-02-17

- The algebraic inversion of formula 16: subtract the lagged appraisal component, then rescale by $a$; with $a = 0.5$ the true return is double the current reported return minus the lagged one (V8 p.105).
- Warning: the curriculum's printed Equation 17 and Example 13 add the lagged term ($R_t^{*}/a + \frac{1-a}{a}R_{t-1}^{*}$) instead of subtracting it, which does not follow from Equation 16; see worked example 17 for both computations and the discrepancy (V8 p.105, p.106).
- Either way, the unsmoothed series has greater volatility and higher correlation with other asset classes than the reported series (V8 p.105).

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Wallonia Transit warehouse NOI** (V8 p.67, p.68)
A single-tenant Belgian warehouse has 10,000 m2 fully rented at EUR52.50 per m2 with a EUR10.25 per m2 operating expense recovery, pass-through property tax (EUR12,500) and insurance (EUR40,000), servicing and repairs of EUR120,750, and a EUR100,000 maintenance allowance.
Gross rent: $52.50 \times 10{,}000 = 525{,}000$.
Expense recovery: $10.25 \times 10{,}000 = 102{,}500$.
Effective gross income: $525{,}000 + 102{,}500 + 12{,}500 + 40{,}000 = 680{,}000$.
Operating expenses: $120{,}750 + 12{,}500 + 40{,}000 = 173{,}250$.
NOI: $680{,}000 - 173{,}250 - 100{,}000 = 406{,}750$ (Python confirms every line; the pro forma's "Gross Rent 525,500" is a curriculum typo for 525,000, as the EGI subtotal shows).
If servicing costs run 20% over estimate, they rise to $120{,}750 \times 1.20 = 144{,}900$, an increase of 24,150; the recovery is capped at 102,500, so NOI falls by the full 24,150 to 382,600 (Python confirms). ^ex-08-02-1

**Example 2: Wallonia Transit leverage and coverage** (V8 p.69, p.70)
The owner paid EUR3,750,000 funded with a EUR3,000,000 20-year fully amortizing 4% annual-pay loan; the payment is 220,745 (Python: 220,745.25), of which first-year interest is $3{,}000{,}000 \times 4\% = 120{,}000$.
Year-end principal: $3{,}000{,}000 - (220{,}745 - 120{,}000) = 2{,}899{,}255$.
LTV at constant value: $2{,}899{,}255 / 3{,}750{,}000 = 0.773$ (Python: 0.7731).
After 10% appreciation the value is $3{,}750{,}000 \times 1.10 = 4{,}125{,}000$ and LTV falls to $2{,}899{,}255 / 4{,}125{,}000 = 0.703$, less leverage and more owner equity (Python: 0.7028).
DSC: $406{,}750 / 220{,}745 = 1.84$ (Python: 1.8426).
With the higher repair costs, DSC drops to $382{,}600 / 220{,}745 = 1.73$ (Python: 1.7332): higher operating costs directly reduce coverage. ^ex-08-02-2

**Example 3: Wallonia Transit equity dividend rate** (V8 p.70, p.71)
Pre-tax cash flow: $406{,}750 - 220{,}745 = 186{,}005$.
Initial equity: $3{,}750{,}000 - 3{,}000{,}000 = 750{,}000$.
Equity dividend rate: $186{,}005 / 750{,}000 = 24.8\%$ (Python: 24.80%).
With the higher repair costs, pre-tax cash flow is $382{,}600 - 220{,}745 = 161{,}855$ and the rate falls to $161{,}855 / 750{,}000 = 21.6\%$ (Python: 21.58%); the denominator never changes because equity was set at purchase. ^ex-08-02-3

**Example 4: Wallonia Transit after-tax return** (V8 p.71, p.72)
The facility has a 30-year useful life, land worth EUR750,000, and a 25% tax rate.
Depreciable base: $3{,}750{,}000 - 750{,}000 = 3{,}000{,}000$; annual straight-line depreciation: $3{,}000{,}000 / 30 = 100{,}000$.
Taxes: $0.25 \times (406{,}750 - 120{,}000 - 100{,}000) = 46{,}688$ (Python: 46,687.50).
After-tax cash flow: $186{,}005 - 46{,}688 = 139{,}317$ (Python: 139,317.50).
Note the tax base uses interest and depreciation, while the cash flow uses the full debt service including principal. ^ex-08-02-4

**Knowledge check: real estate investment features** (V8 p.77, p.78)
Q1: with multiple tenants and a 10% vacancy rate, Wallonia's NOI deducts 10% of gross rent, $0.10 \times 525{,}000 = 52{,}500$, giving $406{,}750 - 52{,}500 = 354{,}250$ (answer B; Python confirms); taking 90% of NOI (366,075) wrongly shrinks recovered expenses too, and adding 52,500 goes the wrong way.
Q2: only lower service and repair rates raise NOI (answer C), because taxes and insurance are fully passed through, so negotiating them helps the tenant, not the owner.
Q3: two AUD2,000,000 properties with identical NOI but land values of 600,000 versus 800,000: the first has the larger depreciable base (1.4m versus 1.2m of annual-depreciation driver), hence larger depreciation, lower taxes, and higher after-tax cash flow (answer A); pre-tax cash flows are identical. ^ex-08-02-5

**Example 5: Chandra Shops overage rent** (V8 p.81, p.82)
Chandra renegotiated a 500 ft2 Chennai store lease: base rent cut 10% from INR300 to INR270 per ft2 per month, plus 8% overage rent on gross monthly sales above INR1,000,000.
Old rent: $300 \times 500 = 150{,}000$.
New base: $270 \times 500 = 135{,}000$.
Overage on sales of 1,250,000: $0.08 \times (1{,}250{,}000 - 1{,}000{,}000) = 20{,}000$.
New total: $135{,}000 + 20{,}000 = 155{,}000$, which is 5,000 more than the old lease at this sales level (Python confirms); the landlord traded certain base rent for participation in sales upside. ^ex-08-02-6

**Knowledge check: value drivers and portfolio characteristics** (V8 p.82, p.83)
Q1: over the cycle, NOI peaks in oversupply and bottoms in recovery (answer C); DSC does the same, so A is backwards, and LTV peaks in recovery and bottoms in oversupply, so B is backwards.
Q2: homebuilders beat rental developers when housing prices are low relative to household income and mortgage rates decline (answer B), because cheap ownership pulls households out of rentals; A helps both markets, and C describes oversupply, which hurts both.
Q3: at expected sales of 1,250,000, test each lease against the old 150,000: an 11.5% cut with 7.2% overage gives $150{,}000 \times 0.885 + 0.072 \times 250{,}000 = 132{,}750 + 18{,}000 = 150{,}750$; an 8.5% cut with 5.2% gives $137{,}250 + 13{,}000 = 150{,}250$; a 9.6% cut with 5.6% gives $135{,}600 + 14{,}000 = 149{,}600$, the only total below 150,000, so Chandra prefers it (answer C; Python confirms all three). ^ex-08-02-7

**Example 6: Pinebranch Estates GPRI and NOI** (V8 p.84, p.85, p.86)
A 240-unit Australian multifamily property averages 1,200 ft2 per unit at market rent of AUD2.00 per ft2 per month.
GPRI: $240 \times 1{,}200 \times 2.00 \times 12 = 6{,}912{,}000$ (Python confirms).
NOI: GPRI plus additional income minus rental deductions minus expenses, $6{,}912{,}000 + 820{,}211 - 1{,}404{,}560 - 3{,}408{,}000 = 2{,}919{,}651$ (Python confirms).
Waterfall: less loss to lease 128,200 gives gross rental income 6,783,800; less vacancy and collection 791,236 and concessions 485,124 gives net rental income 5,507,440; plus other income and expense recoveries (subtotal 820,211) gives total income 6,327,651; less operating and leasing expenses 2,753,000 and maintenance allowance 655,000 gives 2,919,651.
Small flag: the printed line items 295,211 plus 525,800 sum to 821,011, 800 above the 820,211 subtotal the NOI uses, a curriculum line-item typo that does not affect the method.
Contrast with Wallonia: Pinebranch absorbs taxes and insurance, and with many short leases the vacancy, concession, and loss-to-lease deductions become first-order drivers of NOI. ^ex-08-02-8

**Example 7: Eastmain Plaza mixed-use NOI** (V8 p.87, p.88)
A 350,000 ft2 Kuala Lumpur mixed-use tower is 90% office at MYR100 per ft2 and 10% retail at MYR250 per ft2.
Gross rent: $0.9 \times 100 \times 350{,}000 + 0.1 \times 250 \times 350{,}000 = 31{,}500{,}000 + 8{,}750{,}000 = 40{,}250{,}000$ (Python confirms).
Downside scenario: overage rent of 450,800 disappears and retail rent halves, losing $8{,}750{,}000 / 2 = 4{,}375{,}000$, a total hit of 4,825,800, which is 36.9% of the 13,085,217 NOI (curriculum rounds to 37%), leaving $13{,}085{,}217 - 4{,}825{,}800 = 8{,}259{,}417$ (Python confirms).
Mixed-use income streams are diversified in form but correlated in fact, because the office tenants are the retail outlets' customers. ^ex-08-02-9

**Knowledge check: commercial property characteristics** (V8 p.89, p.90)
Q1: GPRI minus gross rental income is the loss to lease, the gap between market rents and in-place rents (answer B); vacancies and concessions are deducted later, from gross rental income to net rental income.

**Example 13: unsmoothing appraisal index returns** (V8 p.105, p.106)

## Item-set patterns

> [!warning] Trap: vacancy applied to the wrong base
> The vignette gives a full NOI build and then adds a vacancy rate.
> Correct: deduct the vacancy percentage of gross rent (the revenue line it erodes) from effective gross income and NOI.
> Distractors: NOI times (1 minus vacancy), which wrongly shrinks expense recoveries and other income too, and NOI plus the deduction, which flips the sign (V8 p.77).

> [!warning] Trap: pass-through versus absorbed expenses
> A cost line rises and the question asks for the NOI effect.
> Correct: only costs the owner actually bears (or bears above a recovery cap) change NOI; fully passed-through taxes and insurance are NOI-neutral because they appear in both income and expenses.
> Distractors: treating every expense increase as an NOI hit, or crediting the owner for negotiating down a fully passed-through cost (V8 p.67, p.78).

> [!warning] Trap: cap rate assembly
> Direct capitalization with r and g given separately.
> Correct: divide expected NOI by r minus g.
> Distractors are always NOI/(r + g) and NOI/r, both offered as choices; also watch whether the NOI given is trailing or expected, and whether a stabilized NOI is called for because the current year is distorted (V8 p.93, p.117).

> [!warning] Trap: terminal value off the wrong NOI or wrong rate
> A DCF table shows NOI for years 1 to n+1 with going-in cap, terminal cap, and discount rates all listed.
> Correct: terminal value is the year n+1 NOI divided by the terminal cap rate, then discounted n years at the discount rate.
> Distractors: capitalizing year n NOI, using the going-in cap rate, or discounting the terminal value at a cap rate (V8 p.95, p.115).

> [!warning] Trap: cap rate versus discount rate identity
> A statement claims the discount rate minus the going-in cap rate equals near-term NOI growth.
> Correct: the identity cap rate equals r minus g holds for the constant-growth phase, so the terminal cap rate plus long-run growth equals the discount rate; early-year growth can be anything.
> Distractors exploit years where NOI is depressed, making the going-in cap rate low for scenario reasons, not risk reasons (V8 p.96, p.101, p.118).

> [!warning] Trap: maximum loan sizing
> A lender offers an interest-only loan subject to a maximum LTV and a minimum DSC.
> Correct: compute the loan under each constraint (LTV times value; NOI divided by minimum DSC gives maximum debt service, divided by the rate gives the loan) and take the smaller.
> Distractors: the larger of the two, or dividing the price by the DSC floor (V8 p.117, p.118).

> [!warning] Trap: loss to lease sign as a cycle signal
> A property table shows GPRI above or below gross rental income.
> Correct: GPRI minus gross rental income is loss to lease; positive means in-place rents below market (tight market, upside), negative means in-place leases above market (oversupply risk).
> Distractors call the gap vacancy or concessions, which are deducted one line lower (V8 p.85, p.89, p.118).

> [!warning] Trap: appraisal smoothing consequences
> An allocation study uses an appraisal-based index and finds real estate dominant.
> Correct: smoothing understates volatility and correlation and overstates the Sharpe ratio, so the model over-allocates to real estate; unsmooth the series or use a transaction-based index.
> Distractors assert appraisal indexes overstate volatility, or that smoothing overstates returns in rising markets (it understates them, because appraisals lag) (V8 p.104, p.105, p.117).

> [!warning] Trap: repeat sales versus hedonic
> A question asks which transaction index needs which data.
> Correct: repeat sales needs the same property sold at least twice and discards single sales; hedonic uses all transactions and controls for characteristics with regression variables.
> Distractors swap the two or claim repeat sales uses every transaction (V8 p.104, p.109).

## Flashcards

#flashcards/cfa-l2/08-Alternative-Investments/lm02

NOI formula::Effective gross income minus operating expenses minus property maintenance allowance (V8 p.66).
What NOI excludes::Financing costs and income taxes; it is the property-level analog of EBITDA (V8 p.66).
Effective gross income::Gross rent plus recoveries, pass-throughs, and other income, less vacancy and concession deductions (V8 p.66).
Property maintenance allowance::Capex needed to maintain the property's current economic use, excluding upgrades that change the use (V8 p.67).
LTV formula::Mortgage debt outstanding divided by current property value (V8 p.69).
DSC formula::Net operating income divided by debt service, where debt service includes interest and principal (V8 p.69).
Equity dividend rate::Pre-tax cash flow divided by initial equity (purchase price minus mortgage loan); a first-year levered cash-on-cash return (V8 p.70).
Pre-tax cash flow::NOI minus debt service (V8 p.70).
Taxes on property income::Tax rate times (NOI minus interest minus depreciation) (V8 p.71).
Depreciable base::Construction or acquisition cost plus improvements, excluding land, which has an infinite life (V8 p.71).
Why does a lower land share raise after-tax cash flow?::A larger depreciable base gives more depreciation, lowering taxes with no effect on pre-tax cash flow (V8 p.71, p.78).
FFO formula::Net income plus depreciation plus amortization minus net gains from property sales (V8 p.76).
REIT vs REOC::A REIT primarily owns and operates rental properties or mortgages and must distribute nearly all earnings to avoid corporate tax; a REOC is an ordinary taxable corporation with few restrictions (V8 p.75).
Four quadrants of real estate investment::Private equity (direct, JV, LP, funds, private REITs), public equity (REITs, REOCs, ETFs), private debt (mortgages, construction loans, mezzanine), public debt (MBS, covered bonds, mortgage REITs) (V8 p.75).
Strategy risk spectrum low to high::Senior debt, core, core-plus, value-add, opportunistic (V8 p.65).
Which strategies are equity-like?::Opportunistic investments such as new development, mezzanine debt, and distressed situations; core income-producing assets are bond-like (V8 p.64, p.65).
Step-up clause::Pre-specified, non-contingent future rent increases in a lease (V8 p.81).
Indexed rent::Rent changes tied to an observed market variable such as CPI (V8 p.81).
Overage rent::Retail rent adjustment paying the owner a share of tenant sales above a pre-agreed breakeven level (V8 p.81).
Rollover risk in leases::When the holding period exceeds the lease term, the owner may lose the tenant and forgo income until a new one is found (V8 p.82).
GPRI formula::Market rent times rentable space, at full occupancy (V8 p.84).
Loss to lease::The gap between gross potential rental income at market rents and gross rental income at in-place rents (V8 p.85).
What does negative loss to lease signal?::In-place leases above market rents, consistent with oversupply and falling lease rates (V8 p.118).
Four phases of the real estate cycle::Recovery, expansion, oversupply, recession (V8 p.80).
NOI and DSC over the cycle::{{Bottom in recovery, rise in expansion, peak in oversupply, fall in recession}} (V8 p.80).
LTV over the cycle::Peaks in recovery and reaches bottom in oversupply, moving inversely to property values (V8 p.80).
Why does oversupply follow the peak?::Construction lags demand, so pipeline projects complete after growth softens, creating a property glut (V8 p.80).
Portfolio roles of real estate::Current income, capital appreciation, inflation hedge, diversification, tax benefits (V8 p.81, p.82).
Which real estate vehicles correlate more with public markets?::Liquid public vehicles such as REITs and MBS, in the short to medium term (V8 p.82).
Property type most exposed to remote work::Office; demand historically tracked service employment (V8 p.86).
Property type structurally boosted by e-commerce::Industrial and warehouse; but purpose-built facilities are hard to convert (V8 p.86).
Most cyclical, management-intensive property type::Hospitality, especially convention centers and destination resorts with high fixed costs (V8 p.86, p.87).
Self-storage lease quirk::Month-to-month leases give pricing flexibility but expose owners to demand swings (V8 p.87).
Six elements of real estate due diligence::Market review and outlook, current lease review, future lease outlook, financial review, documentation review, property inspection and service agreements (V8 p.91).
Which due diligence element does not feed the NOI forecast?::Documentation review, the legal check of title, liens, taxes, and zoning (V8 p.92, p.101).
Direct capitalization value::Expected NOI divided by the capitalization rate (V8 p.93).
Cap rate from r and g::Required return minus constant NOI growth (V8 p.93).
Cap rate from comparables::Expected NOI divided by property value observed on comparable sales (V8 p.96).
Going-in vs terminal cap rate::Going-in uses first-year income at purchase; terminal capitalizes expected income after the anticipated sale (V8 p.96).
Terminal value formula::Final-year NOI times (1 plus g), divided by r minus g (V8 p.95).
When is DCF preferred to direct capitalization?::When NOI dynamics are visible and changing, such as a downside year followed by recovery (V8 p.94).
What does g < 0 imply in the cap rate?::The property is nearing the end of its useful life with declining NOI (V8 p.96).
Cost approach value::Replacement cost including developer profit, less depreciation and adjustments, plus land (V8 p.96, p.97).
When does replacement cost exceed market price?::In the oversupply phase, when completions depress prices while construction costs stay high (V8 p.100).
Which properties suit the sales comparison approach?::Homogeneous, frequently traded assets such as single-family homes; large income properties use the income approach (V8 p.100).
Index holding period return::(NOI minus capex plus appraised value change) divided by beginning appraised value (V8 p.102).
Three biases of appraisal-based indexes::Understated volatility, understated correlation with other assets, overstated Sharpe ratio (V8 p.104, p.105).
Appraisal lag direction::Appraisal indexes lag transactions in both rising and falling markets, understating gains and losses as they happen (V8 p.104, p.109).
Two fixes for appraisal lag::Unsmooth the index or use a transaction-based index (V8 p.105).
Unsmoothing formula (correct form)

Repeat sales index::Uses properties sold at least twice, allocating value changes to periods by regression; discards one-time sales (V8 p.104).

## Common mistakes

1. Multiplying NOI by (1 minus the vacancy rate) instead of deducting the vacancy share of gross rent from effective gross income (V8 p.77).
2. Charging fully passed-through property taxes or insurance against NOI, or crediting savings on them to the owner; only costs the owner bears above recovery caps move NOI (V8 p.67, p.78).
3. Using NOI/(r + g) or NOI/r in direct capitalization instead of NOI/(r minus g), the two standing distractors in every cap rate question (V8 p.93, p.117).
4. Capitalizing year n NOI (instead of year n+1) or using the going-in cap rate (instead of the terminal cap rate) in the terminal value, or discounting cash flows at a cap rate instead of the discount rate (V8 p.95, p.118).
5. Reading the going-in cap rate as a risk statement when first-year NOI is scenario-depressed; the discount rate carries risk, and the r minus g identity binds only in the constant-growth phase (V8 p.101, p.119).
