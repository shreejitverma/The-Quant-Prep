# Ostrander's Cross-Border Dividend Memo

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Marguerite Ostrander, CFA, advises the family office of the Vasquez family.
The family holds large positions in three listed companies, each domiciled in a country with a different system for taxing corporate earnings paid out as dividends.
Ostrander is preparing a memo that compares the tax cost of each holding's dividends and reviews each company's payout policy.
Exhibit 1 shows the tax regimes that apply to the family.

Exhibit 1: Tax regimes applicable to the Vasquez family

| Holding | Tax system | Corporate tax rate | Family's tax rate on dividends |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brennholt AG | Double taxation | 30% | 25% |
| Wattle Resources Ltd | Dividend imputation | 30% | 45% (marginal rate) |
| Solvik ASA | Split rate | 20% on distributed earnings, 35% on retained earnings | 25% |

Ostrander drafts the claim, "Per 100 of pretax earnings fully distributed, Solvik delivers the most after-tax cash to the family."

Brennholt, an industrial group, follows a stable dividend policy based on a target payout adjustment model.
Exhibit 2 provides Brennholt data (EUR).

Exhibit 2: Brennholt AG

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Dividend per share paid for 2025 | 1.10 |
| Actual EPS for 2025 | 3.10 |
| Expected EPS for 2026 | 3.60 |
| Expected EPS for 2027 | 4.00 |
| Target payout ratio | 45% |
| Adjustment period | 4 years |
| Current assets (including cash of 300 million) | 900 million |
| Current liabilities | 500 million |
| Total debt | 600 million |
| Total equity | 1,500 million |
| Shares outstanding / share price | 100 million / 25.00 |

At year end, Brennholt will pay an extra cash dividend of EUR 150 million from cash on hand and will simultaneously distribute a 10% stock dividend.
The dividend and EPS figures in Exhibit 2 are already restated for this stock dividend; the shares outstanding and share price are before both distributions.

Brennholt's board is debating a permanent increase in the target payout to 60%.
Brennholt would then need to issue new shares every year to fund its capital program, and its bankers estimate flotation costs of about 6% of the proceeds.
Director Elena Ruiz argues, "Our investors can create any income stream they want by selling shares, so a higher payout cannot change our share value."
Another director notes that Brennholt's two largest shareholders are tax-exempt pension funds, and a third argues that investors regard dividends as less risky than capital gains.

Wattle, a mining company, reported net income of AUD 180 million for 2025, down from AUD 400 million, because commodity prices fell.
Its bank loan agreement limits dividends in any year to 50% of that year's net income.
Wattle paid AUD 120 million of dividends for 2024 and has announced total dividends of AUD 90 million for 2025.
Alongside the cut, Wattle published a detailed plan to fund a lithium expansion internally, with a projected internal rate of return well above its cost of capital.
Wattle's shares rose 3.5% on the announcement.

Ostrander's colleague Henrik Dahl remarks, "The rise in Wattle's share price after a dividend cut shows that the market ignores dividend signals."

## Questions

1. Per 100 of pretax earnings fully distributed, the after-tax cash the Vasquez family receives from Solvik, compared with Wattle, is: (LOS: los-04-01-f)
   - A. lower by 6.25.
   - B. higher by 5.00.
   - C. higher by 21.50.
2. Using the target payout adjustment model, Brennholt's expected dividend per share for 2027 is closest to: (LOS: los-04-01-g)
   - A. EUR 1.28.
   - B. EUR 1.37.
   - C. EUR 1.80.
3. Immediately after the extra cash dividend and the stock dividend, Brennholt's current ratio and debt-to-equity ratio are closest to: (LOS: los-04-01-a)
   - A. 1.50 and 0.44.
   - B. 1.80 and 0.40.
   - C. 1.50 and 0.55.
4. Which point raised in the vignette most directly undermines the argument Ruiz uses to reach her conclusion? (LOS: los-04-01-b)
   - A. Brennholt would incur flotation costs of about 6% on the shares issued to fund the higher payout.
   - B. Brennholt's two largest shareholders are tax-exempt pension funds.
   - C. A director's argument that investors regard dividends as less risky than capital gains.
5. The factor that most directly caps Wattle's 2025 dividend at AUD 90 million is: (LOS: los-04-01-e)
   - A. the expected volatility of future earnings.
   - B. flotation costs.
   - C. contractual and legal restrictions.
6. Dahl's remark is best described as: (LOS: los-04-01-c)
   - A. correct, because dividend policy conveys no information when dividend policy is irrelevant.
   - B. incorrect, because a dividend cut always signals lower future earnings, so the price rise must reflect unrelated news.
   - C. incorrect, because a cut accompanied by a credible plan to reinvest in high-return projects can convey positive information.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Solvik (split rate): distributed earnings bear the lower 20% corporate rate, so $ETR = 0.20 + (1 - 0.20) \times 0.25 = 0.20 + 0.20 = 40.00\%$ and the family keeps $100 \times (1 - 0.40) = 60.00$.
> Wattle (imputation): the franking credit means distributed earnings are taxed once at the family's 45% marginal rate, so $ETR = 45\%$; the family pays $0.45 \times 100 - 30 = 15$ on top of the 30 of corporate tax and keeps $55.00$.
> Difference $= 60.00 - 55.00 = 5.00$ in Solvik's favor.
> For completeness, Brennholt (double taxation) has $ETR = 0.30 + 0.70 \times 0.25 = 47.50\%$ and delivers 52.50, so Ostrander's claim is correct.
> A is wrong because it applies Solvik's 35% retained-earnings rate to distributed earnings: $ETR = 0.35 + 0.65 \times 0.25 = 51.25\%$, net $48.75$, which is $6.25$ below Wattle's 55.00.
> C is wrong because it applies the double-taxation formula in an imputation country: $ETR = 0.30 + 0.70 \times 0.45 = 61.50\%$, net $38.50$, making Solvik appear $60.00 - 38.50 = 21.50$ higher.
> 2. **B.** Adjustment factor $A = 1/4 = 0.25$.
> 2026: $D_{2026} = 1.10 + (3.60 \times 0.45 - 1.10) \times 0.25 = 1.10 + (1.62 - 1.10) \times 0.25 = 1.10 + 0.13 = 1.2300$.
> 2027: $D_{2027} = 1.23 + (4.00 \times 0.45 - 1.23) \times 0.25 = 1.23 + (1.80 - 1.23) \times 0.25 = 1.23 + 0.1425 = 1.3725$.
> A is wrong because it skips the 2026 step and adjusts from the 2025 dividend in a single year: $1.10 + (1.80 - 1.10) \times 0.25 = 1.2750$.
> C is wrong because it pays the full target dividend $4.00 \times 0.45 = 1.80$, ignoring partial adjustment; that is what a constant payout ratio policy would pay.
> 3. **A.** The extra cash dividend reduces current assets and equity by 150 million; the stock dividend moves retained earnings into contributed capital and changes neither total equity nor any asset.
> Current ratio $= (900 - 150)/500 = 750/500 = 1.50$, down from $900/500 = 1.80$.
> Debt-to-equity $= 600/(1{,}500 - 150) = 600/1{,}350 = 0.4444$, up from $600/1{,}500 = 0.40$.
> B is wrong because it treats both distributions as ratio-neutral; only the stock dividend is.
> C is wrong because it also deducts the stock dividend's transfer from retained earnings ($10\% \times 100 \text{ million} \times 25.00 = 250$ million) from total equity: $600/(1{,}350 - 250) = 600/1{,}100 = 0.5455$.
> 4. **A.** Ruiz is invoking Miller and Modigliani's homemade dividend argument, which requires perfect markets with no transaction costs.
> If a higher payout forces Brennholt to replace the cash with new shares costing about 6% in flotation costs, the payout policy destroys value, which breaks the irrelevance result.
> B is wrong because tax-exempt holders face no tax wedge between dividends and capital gains, which is consistent with, not contrary to, MM's no-tax assumption.
> C is wrong because it restates the bird-in-the-hand argument, which MM rebut: paying a dividend today does not change the risk of future cash flows, it only lowers the ex-dividend price.
> 5. **C.** The loan agreement caps dividends at $0.50 \times 180 = 90$ million, exactly the amount announced, so the binding constraint is contractual.
> A is wrong because falling commodity prices make earnings volatile and argue for caution, but they do not produce the precise 90 million ceiling.
> B is wrong because Wattle funds its expansion internally; nothing in the vignette ties the dividend level to avoiding a new share issue.
> 6. **C.** Dividend changes do carry information, but the sign depends on context: a cut framed by a detailed, credible reinvestment plan in projects earning above the cost of capital can be read as prudent and positive, as with Telstra and BT.
> A is wrong because empirical evidence shows dividend changes convey information under asymmetric information; MM's assumption of symmetric information does not describe actual markets.
> B is wrong because cuts are negative only on average; the market's positive reaction here is consistent with signaling theory, not evidence of unrelated news.

## Distractor design

1. A: retained-earnings corporate rate applied to distributed earnings under split rate (wrong formula variant); C: double-taxation formula applied in an imputation country (wrong tax system).
2. A: one-step adjustment from the old dividend, skipping the intermediate year (period error); C: full target dividend with no partial adjustment (constant payout confused with stable policy).
3. B: cash dividend treated as ratio-neutral like a stock dividend; C: stock dividend's retained-earnings transfer deducted from total equity (misapplied accounting).
4. B: tax exemption cited, which supports rather than undermines MM (assumption misread); C: bird-in-the-hand argument that MM reject (wrong theory).
5. A: earnings volatility chosen over the binding covenant (factor misidentified); B: flotation costs chosen when funding is internal (misread vignette).
6. A: MM symmetric-information assumption taken as fact; B: every dividend cut treated as negative (sign of the signal fixed regardless of context).
