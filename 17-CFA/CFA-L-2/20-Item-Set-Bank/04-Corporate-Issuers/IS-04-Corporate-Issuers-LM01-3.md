# Straits Connect: Is the Dividend Safe?

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Wei Ling Tan, CFA, is a credit and equity analyst at a Singapore insurer.
She is reviewing the dividend of Straits Connect Ltd, a Singapore mobile and fixed-line telecom operator that reports in Singapore dollars (SGD).
Straits Connect has long described its policy as a stable dividend based on long-term sustainable earnings.
Exhibit 1 presents data from its statements.

Exhibit 1: Straits Connect, selected financial data (SGD millions)

| Item | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net income | 620 | 580 | 510 |
| Cash flow from operations | 1,220 | 1,120 | 1,010 |
| Fixed capital investment | 580 | 640 | 520 |
| Net borrowing (repayment) | (40) | 20 | 150 |
| Cash dividends | 460 | 470 | 480 |
| Share repurchases | 90 | 60 | 0 |

At the end of 2025 Straits Connect had 400 million shares outstanding, trading at SGD 24.00.
Its dividend yield was 5.0%, compared with a ten-year average of 5.1%.
Management's presentation notes that the 2025 reduction in fixed capital investment reflects "deferral of network upgrades to 2027," and that 2025 net borrowing was drawn on a revolving credit facility.

In January 2026, Straits Connect sold its portfolio of mobile towers to an infrastructure fund for SGD 600 million, net of tax.
The towers had generated a portion of the company's operating income.
The board is considering three uses of the proceeds: distribute them as a one-time dividend, use them to repurchase shares at the current market price of SGD 24.00, or keep them in deposits yielding 3.2% before tax.
The corporate tax rate is 17%.
Tan assumes that if the proceeds are kept in deposits, Straits Connect's annual net income will be SGD 510 million, including the after-tax interest on the deposits.

Tan's colleague Arjun Menon asks how Straits Connect's dividend would have looked under a different policy.
He wants to know the 2025 dividend had the company applied a constant payout ratio equal to its 2023 payout ratio.

Menon also points to Pelangi Telecom, a regional competitor.
Pelangi cut its dividend by 40% last quarter and announced that the cash saved would fund a 5G network build, publishing a detailed project schedule with expected returns above its cost of capital.
Pelangi's share price rose 4% on the day of the announcement, while the regional telecom index was flat.

Menon comments, "A cut by Straits Connect would be read the same way as Pelangi's."
Tan replies that the two companies' situations differ and that the market's reading of a cut depends on what accompanies it.

Tan plans to present her conclusions on dividend safety to the insurer's investment committee next week.
She ignores transaction costs and personal taxes throughout.

## Questions

1. Straits Connect's 2025 FCFE coverage ratio is closest to: (LOS: los-04-01-m)
   - A. 1.02.
   - B. 1.06.
   - C. 1.33.
2. Which observation most strongly indicates that Straits Connect may not be able to sustain its current dividend? (LOS: los-04-01-n)
   - A. FCFE covered 2025 distributions only because fixed capital investment was deferred and SGD 150 million was borrowed.
   - B. Straits Connect repurchased shares in 2023 and 2024 in addition to paying dividends.
   - C. Straits Connect's dividend yield of 5.0% compared with its ten-year average of 5.1%.
3. If Straits Connect uses the full tower sale proceeds to repurchase shares at the current market price, its EPS after the repurchase is closest to: (LOS: los-04-01-j)
   - A. SGD 1.31.
   - B. SGD 1.32.
   - C. SGD 1.36.
4. The answer to Menon's question about the constant payout ratio policy is closest to: (LOS: los-04-01-g)
   - A. SGD 378 million.
   - B. SGD 413 million.
   - C. SGD 480 million.
5. Pelangi's share price reaction is most likely explained by the market reading the dividend cut as a signal that: (LOS: los-04-01-c)
   - A. dividend policy is irrelevant to value.
   - B. Pelangi's future earnings will decline.
   - C. Pelangi has profitable reinvestment opportunities it can fund internally.
6. If Straits Connect distributes the tower sale proceeds as a one-time cash dividend, the payment is best described as a(n): (LOS: los-04-01-a)
   - A. extra dividend, and it would leave the debt-to-equity ratio unchanged.
   - B. liquidating dividend, and it would increase the debt-to-equity ratio.
   - C. liquidating dividend, and it would leave the debt-to-equity ratio unchanged.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **C.** $FCFE_{2025} = CFO - FCInv + \text{Net borrowing} = 1{,}010 - 520 + 150 = 640$.
> FCFE coverage $= 640 / (480 + 0) = 1.3333$.
> A is wrong because it omits net borrowing: $(1{,}010 - 520)/480 = 490/480 = 1.0208$.
> B is wrong because it is the earnings-based coverage ratio, $510/480 = 1.0625$, not the free cash flow measure the question asks for.
> 2. **A.** The 2025 FCFE coverage of 1.33 looks comfortable, but without the SGD 150 million of borrowing it is only 1.02, and capital investment was cut to 520 by deferring upgrades that must be spent in 2027.
> The trend confirms the pressure: FCFE coverage of dividends plus repurchases was $600/550 = 1.0909$ in 2023 and $500/530 = 0.9434$ in 2024, while earnings coverage fell from $620/460 = 1.3478$ to $510/480 = 1.0625$, close to the 1.0 jeopardy line.
> Supporting distributions with borrowing and capex cuts is the classic sign of an unsustainable dividend.
> B is wrong because returning cash through both dividends and repurchases is normal payout practice, and the repurchases were the flexible component that was stopped first.
> C is wrong because a dividend yield in line with the company's own history shows the market is not pricing in a cut; an abnormally high yield relative to history would be the warning sign.
> 3. **B.** Shares repurchased $= 600 / 24.00 = 25$ million, leaving $400 - 25 = 375$ million.
> The cash would otherwise earn $3.2\% \times (1 - 0.17) = 2.656\%$ after tax, so earnings lost $= 600 \times 0.02656 = 15.936$ million.
> $EPS = (510 - 15.936)/375 = 494.064/375 = 1.3175$, up from $510/400 = 1.275$ because the 5.31% earnings yield ($1.275/24.00$) exceeds the 2.656% after-tax return on the cash.
> A is wrong because it deducts the pretax interest forgone: $(510 - 600 \times 0.032)/375 = (510 - 19.2)/375 = 1.3088$.
> C is wrong because it treats the proceeds as non-earning cash and ignores the forgone interest: $510/375 = 1.3600$.
> 4. **A.** 2023 payout ratio $= 460/620 = 0.7419$.
> 2025 dividend under a constant payout ratio $= 0.7419 \times 510 = 378.39$ million.
> Under a constant payout policy the dividend moves with current earnings, so it would have fallen with net income instead of rising to 480 million.
> B is wrong because it uses the 2024 payout ratio, $470/580 = 0.8103$, giving $0.8103 \times 510 = 413.28$ million.
> C is wrong because it is the actual 2025 dividend paid under the stable policy.
> 5. **C.** Pelangi tied the cut to a detailed, credible plan to reinvest in projects earning more than the cost of capital, and its shares outperformed a flat index, so the market read the cut as funding value-creating growth rather than as distress.
> A is wrong because a 4% excess price move on the announcement shows the dividend action conveyed information; irrelevance would predict no reaction to the policy change itself.
> B is wrong because it is the default reading of a cut, which the vignette's reinvestment detail and positive reaction contradict.
> 6. **B.** Distributing the proceeds from selling a portion of the business is a return of capital, which is a liquidating dividend.
> Paying 600 million of cash reduces assets and equity, so with debt unchanged the debt-to-equity ratio rises.
> A is wrong because an extra dividend is a supplement paid out of earnings, not a distribution of proceeds from selling part of the business, and any cash dividend reduces equity.
> C is wrong because only stock dividends and splits leave leverage ratios unchanged; a cash distribution raises them.

## Distractor design

1. A: net borrowing omitted from FCFE (sign and component error); B: earnings coverage substituted for FCFE coverage (wrong formula variant).
2. B: normal combined payout mistaken for a warning sign; C: dividend yield in line with history mistaken for an abnormally high yield (misread exhibit).
3. A: pretax instead of after-tax interest forgone (before-tax vs after-tax); C: interest-earning cash treated as idle (wrong formula variant).
4. B: 2024 payout ratio used instead of 2023 (period error); C: stable-policy dividend reported instead of the constant payout dividend.
5. A: MM irrelevance applied to an information event; B: every cut treated as a negative signal (sign of the signal fixed regardless of context).
6. A: extra dividend confused with liquidating dividend and cash dividend treated as ratio-neutral; C: correct label but cash dividend treated as ratio-neutral like a split.
