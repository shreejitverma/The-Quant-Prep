# Norbridge Fasteners: Funding and Executing a Buyback

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Priya Raman, CFA, is an equity analyst at a Toronto pension fund.
She covers Norbridge Fasteners Inc., a Canadian maker of industrial fasteners that reports in Canadian dollars (CAD).
Norbridge generates strong and stable operating cash flow, but its own capital plan identifies few new projects that earn more than its 9% cost of capital.
The chief executive officer also chairs the board, and four of the seven directors are former Norbridge executives.
Norbridge has CAD 410 million of senior notes outstanding, and the indenture requires EBITDA to cover interest expense at least 4.0 times.

The board has approved the repurchase of 4 million common shares.
Exhibit 1 summarizes the data Raman gathered.

Exhibit 1: Norbridge Fasteners, selected data (fiscal 2025)

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Net income | CAD 84 million |
| Common shares outstanding | 40 million |
| Current share price | CAD 35.00 |
| Book value of common equity | CAD 1,120 million |
| Annual dividend per share | CAD 0.84 |
| Pretax rate on a new five-year term loan | 7.2% |
| Marginal corporate tax rate | 25% |
| Pretax yield earned on surplus cash | 1.5% |

The chief financial officer, Lena Marchetti, is weighing two funding sources.
The first is a new CAD 140 million term loan, sized to buy 4 million shares at the current price.
The second is the company's surplus cash.
Marchetti tells the board, "The term loan costs 7.2%, which is well above our 6.0% earnings yield, so a debt-financed repurchase will dilute EPS."

Marchetti has also received proposals on how to execute the repurchase, summarized in Exhibit 2.

Exhibit 2: Repurchase method proposals

| Method | Terms |
|---|---|
| Open market program | Purchases over 18 months at prevailing prices; no obligation to complete |
| Fixed price tender offer | 4 million shares at CAD 38.50 per share, open for 30 days |
| Dutch auction tender offer | 4 million shares within a range of CAD 36.00 to CAD 38.50, open for 30 days; advisers expect it to clear at CAD 37.25 |

The board wants the repurchase completed within six weeks and at the lowest cost consistent with that timetable.

Separately, Halvorsen Capital, an activist fund that owns 4 million Norbridge shares, has offered to sell its entire block back to Norbridge at CAD 39.20 per share in a privately negotiated transaction.
For her analysis of this offer, which she treats as a stand-alone transaction, Raman assumes the CAD 35.00 market price fairly reflects the value of the shares before any transaction, and she ignores the effect of the funding source on earnings.

At the board meeting, director Anita Bouchard comments on payout practice more broadly.

Statement 1: "Across developed markets, the proportion of listed companies that pay cash dividends has risen steadily over the last several decades."

Statement 2: "Share repurchases have trended upward in the United States since the early 1980s, and repurchase activity falls sharply in recessions while companies cut regular dividends far more reluctantly."

Raman ignores transaction costs throughout.

## Questions

1. If Norbridge finances the repurchase of 4 million shares at the current share price with the term loan, its EPS after the repurchase is closest to: (LOS: los-04-01-j)
   - A. CAD 2.05.
   - B. CAD 2.12.
   - C. CAD 2.33.
2. If Norbridge executes the Dutch auction and it clears at the price its advisers expect, book value per share after the repurchase is closest to: (LOS: los-04-01-k)
   - A. CAD 26.97.
   - B. CAD 27.22.
   - C. CAD 31.11.
3. Given the board's objectives, the repurchase method Norbridge should most likely select is the: (LOS: los-04-01-i)
   - A. open market program.
   - B. fixed price tender offer.
   - C. Dutch auction tender offer.
4. If Norbridge accepted Halvorsen's offer, the effect on the wealth of each remaining Norbridge shareholder, per share held, is closest to: (LOS: los-04-01-l)
   - A. no change.
   - B. a loss of CAD 0.42.
   - C. a loss of CAD 0.47.
5. Relative to not repurchasing shares, the debt-financed repurchase is most likely to: (LOS: los-04-01-d)
   - A. increase the conflict of interest between Norbridge's shareholders and its noteholders.
   - B. reduce the default risk borne by Norbridge's noteholders.
   - C. worsen the overinvestment conflict between Norbridge's managers and its shareholders.
6. Which of Bouchard's statements is correct? (LOS: los-04-01-h)
   - A. Statement 1 only.
   - B. Statement 2 only.
   - C. Both statements.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Current EPS $= 84 / 40 = 2.10$ and the earnings yield is $2.10 / 35.00 = 6.00\%$.
> After-tax cost of debt $= 7.2\% \times (1 - 0.25) = 5.40\%$, below the earnings yield, so the repurchase is accretive and Marchetti's statement is wrong.
> After-tax interest $= 140 \times 0.054 = 7.56$ million; shares remaining $= 40 - 140/35 = 36$ million.
> $EPS = (84 - 7.56) / 36 = 76.44 / 36 = 2.1233$.
> A is wrong because it deducts pretax interest, $140 \times 0.072 = 10.08$, giving $(84 - 10.08)/36 = 73.92/36 = 2.0533$; this is the same error Marchetti makes by comparing the pretax rate with the earnings yield.
> C is wrong because it ignores the interest cost of the borrowed funds entirely: $84 / 36 = 2.3333$.
> 2. **A.** Cost of the Dutch auction $= 4 \times 37.25 = 149$ million.
> Equity after the repurchase $= 1{,}120 - 149 = 971$ million; shares remaining $= 36$ million.
> $BVPS = 971 / 36 = 26.9722$, down from $1{,}120 / 40 = 28.00$, as expected because the clearing price of 37.25 exceeds BVPS.
> B is wrong because it values the repurchase at the CAD 35.00 market price instead of the auction clearing price: $(1{,}120 - 140)/36 = 980/36 = 27.2222$.
> C is wrong because it divides the old equity by the new share count: $1{,}120 / 36 = 31.1111$.
> 3. **C.** The board wants completion within six weeks at the lowest cost consistent with that speed.
> A Dutch auction is nearly as fast as a fixed price tender (both run 30 days) but discovers the minimum clearing price; at the expected 37.25 it costs $149$ million versus $4 \times 38.50 = 154$ million for the fixed price tender, a saving of 5.0 million.
> A is wrong because an open market program maximizes flexibility and is cost-effective but executes slowly, here over 18 months with no obligation to complete, which fails the six-week requirement.
> B is wrong because a fixed price tender is fast but pays the full premium to every tendering holder, so it is not the lowest-cost fast method.
> 4. **C.** Price after the negotiated repurchase $= (40 \times 35.00 - 4 \times 39.20) / (40 - 4) = (1{,}400 - 156.8)/36 = 1{,}243.2/36 = 34.5333$.
> Loss per remaining share $= 35.00 - 34.5333 = 0.4667$.
> The premium of $4 \times (39.20 - 35.00) = 16.8$ million is transferred from the 36 million remaining shares to Halvorsen: $16.8 / 36 = 0.4667$.
> A is wrong because wealth equivalence between dividends and repurchases holds only for a repurchase at the market price; a premium paid to one holder is a transfer from the others.
> B is wrong because it spreads the 16.8 million premium over all 40 million pre-transaction shares, $16.8/40 = 0.42$, including the shares Halvorsen sells, which do not bear the loss.
> 5. **A.** Borrowing to distribute cash to shareholders raises leverage and thins the asset cushion behind the notes, increasing default risk and transferring value from noteholders to shareholders; this is the shareholder-bondholder conflict that indentures limit.
> B is wrong because a debt-financed distribution raises, not reduces, the noteholders' default risk.
> C is wrong because distributing free cash flow and committing to interest payments reduces the cash available for empire building by a CEO-chair with few positive NPV projects; it mitigates, not worsens, the manager-shareholder overinvestment problem.
> 6. **B.** Statement 1 is incorrect: the fraction of companies paying dividends has declined over the long term in developed markets, even as aggregate dividends and payout ratios among payers rose.
> Statement 2 is correct: repurchases have trended upward since the early 1980s in the United States, and they are procyclical, collapsing in recessions when cash dries up, whereas regular dividends are cut far more reluctantly.
> A is wrong because it reverses the trend in the proportion of payers and rejects a correct statement.
> C is wrong because Statement 1 misstates the trend.

## Distractor design

1. A: pretax instead of after-tax cost of debt (before-tax vs after-tax); C: interest cost of borrowed funds omitted (wrong formula variant).
2. B: market price used instead of the auction clearing price (misread exhibit); C: old equity divided by new share count (mismatched numerator and denominator).
3. A: flexibility and low cost chosen over the stated six-week deadline (objective misread); B: speed chosen without the cost-minimization objective (premium ignored).
4. A: dividend-repurchase equivalence applied at a premium price (condition ignored); B: premium spread over pre-transaction share count including the seller's shares (wrong denominator).
5. B: direction of the bondholder effect reversed (sign); C: manager-shareholder effect reversed (sign).
6. A: trend in the proportion of dividend payers reversed; C: accepts the false payer trend alongside the correct repurchase trend.
