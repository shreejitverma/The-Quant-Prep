# Ceibal Alimentos: Pyramid Control and Board Independence

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Julia Mendes, CFA, is a governance analyst at Northgate Global Equity, a London-based institutional investor.
Northgate holds Class B shares of Ceibal Alimentos SA, a listed South American packaged-food producer, and Mendes must recommend how Northgate votes at the coming annual general meeting.

Ceibal Alimentos is the principal operating company of a group controlled by the Ferreira family.
The family owns 70% of Ceibal Holdings SA, an unlisted holding company; the remaining 30% of Ceibal Holdings is owned by unrelated private investors.
Ceibal Holdings in turn owns shares of Ceibal Alimentos and of two other listed companies in the group, one of which, Envases Ceibal, supplies most of Ceibal Alimentos's packaging.
Exhibit 1 summarizes Ceibal Alimentos's share capital.

Exhibit 1: Ceibal Alimentos share capital

| Share class | Shares outstanding (millions) | Votes per share | Held by Ceibal Holdings (millions) | Listed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Class A | 20 | 10 | 20 | No |
| Class B | 80 | 1 | 5 | Yes |

Both classes carry identical rights to dividends and to residual assets per share.
Foreign institutional investors, including Northgate, hold 18% of the Class B shares.
Banco Meridional holds 6% of the Class B shares and is also Ceibal Alimentos's lead lender.
Ceibal Alimentos paid a dividend of 1.10 per share last year, and its shares trade at 13 times earnings versus a regional peer average of 16 times.

Exhibit 2: Board of directors of Ceibal Alimentos

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Board size | 14 directors, single-tier board |
| Chair | Eduardo Ferreira, founder |
| Chief executive officer | Carla Ferreira Ruiz, not a board chair |
| Directors classified as independent by the company | 5 |
| Female directors | 4 |
| Remuneration committee | 3 members, including the CEO's brother, a non-independent director |
| Say-on-pay | Annual advisory shareholder vote on executive remuneration |
| Directors with tenure over 10 years | 3 |

Mendes reviews the biographies of the five directors the company classifies as independent.
One of them, Rafael Olmos, receives an annual consulting fee from Ceibal Alimentos for advising on distribution contracts.
The other four have no employment, ownership, or remuneration relationship with the company beyond their board fees.
The national corporate governance code recommends, on a comply-or-explain basis, that at least one third of the board be independent.

Mendes and her colleague Oliver Grant discuss the influential shareholders on the register.

Grant: "Because Banco Meridional is both lender and shareholder, it could encourage Ceibal Alimentos to take on large loans on terms that are unfavorable to other shareholders."

Mendes: "Because the Ferreira family controls both management and the board, Ceibal Alimentos faces greater principal-agent risk than a widely held peer with one vote per share."

Mendes notes that Envases Ceibal's packaging prices have risen faster than a regional packaging price index for three consecutive years.
She wants her voting recommendation to address both the control structure and the board.

## Questions

1. The Ferreira family's look-through economic interest in the cash flows of Ceibal Alimentos is closest to: (LOS: los-04-02-a)
   - A. 17.5%.
   - B. 25.0%.
   - C. 51.2%.
2. The percentage of Ceibal Alimentos's total votes controlled by Ceibal Holdings is closest to: (LOS: los-04-02-a)
   - A. 25.0%.
   - B. 71.4%.
   - C. 73.2%.
3. The governance conflict created by Ceibal Alimentos's ownership and voting structure is best described as a: (LOS: los-04-02-a)
   - A. principal-agent problem, because no shareholder owns a majority of the cash flow rights.
   - B. principal-principal problem, in which the controlling family can extract value from minority shareholders despite owning a minority of the cash flow rights.
   - C. structure of concentrated ownership with dispersed voting power arising from voting caps.
4. Which of the statements made by Grant and Mendes about influential shareholders is correct? (LOS: los-04-02-a)
   - A. Grant's statement only.
   - B. Mendes's statement only.
   - C. Both statements.
5. After applying the definition of an independent director, Ceibal Alimentos's board independence ratio and its status under the national code are best described as: (LOS: los-04-02-b)
   - A. 35.7%, compliant with the code.
   - B. 28.6%, compliant because the ratio lies within the typical 20% to 50% range recommended across OECD countries.
   - C. 28.6%, below the code's recommendation, so the company must disclose and explain its departure.
6. The board feature in Exhibit 2 that most likely increases Ceibal Alimentos's governance risk is the: (LOS: los-04-02-b)
   - A. annual advisory say-on-pay vote.
   - B. composition of the remuneration committee.
   - C. separation of the chair and CEO roles.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **A.** Ceibal Holdings owns $20 + 5 = 25$ million of the $20 + 80 = 100$ million shares, a stake of $25 / 100 = 0.2500$, and both classes share equally in dividends and residual assets.
> The family owns 70% of Ceibal Holdings, so its look-through interest is $0.70 \times 0.2500 = 0.1750$, or 17.5%.
> B is wrong because it stops at the holding company's stake and ignores that the family owns only 70% of that layer of the pyramid.
> C is wrong because it multiplies the family's 70% by Ceibal Holdings's voting share ($0.70 \times 0.7321 = 0.5125$), confusing voting power with cash flow rights.
> 2. **C.** Total votes $= 20 \times 10 + 80 \times 1 = 200 + 80 = 280$ million.
> Ceibal Holdings's votes $= 20 \times 10 + 5 \times 1 = 205$ million, so its share is $205 / 280 = 0.7321$, or 73.2%.
> Because the family controls Ceibal Holdings, it controls these votes through the pyramid.
> A is wrong because it counts shares rather than votes ($25 / 100 = 25.0\%$), ignoring the superior voting rights of Class A.
> B is wrong because it counts only the Class A votes and omits the 5 million Class B shares that Ceibal Holdings also holds ($200 / 280 = 71.4\%$).
> 3. **B.** The family controls about 73.2% of the votes while holding only a 17.5% look-through economic interest, achieved through a vertical (pyramid) structure combined with dual-class shares.
> Concentrated voting power in the hands of a controlling shareholder produces strong shareholders and weak managers, so the conflict is principal-principal: the controller can divert resources, for example through related-party purchases from Envases Ceibal, at the expense of minority shareholders.
> A is wrong because principal-agent problems arise when both ownership and voting power are dispersed, leaving weak shareholders facing strong managers; here a controller clearly exists.
> C is wrong because voting caps are legal restrictions on the voting rights of large positions; nothing caps the family's votes, and its voting power is concentrated, not dispersed.
> 4. **A.** Grant's statement is correct: a bank with both a lending relationship and an equity interest may push the company toward large loans, possibly on less favorable terms, to the detriment of other shareholders.
> Mendes's statement is incorrect: family control lowers principal-agent risk because ownership and management responsibility are concentrated in the same hands; the drawbacks of family control are poor transparency, weak consideration of minority rights, and difficulty attracting management talent.
> B is wrong because it accepts the reversed claim about family control and rejects the correct bank conflict.
> C is wrong because Mendes's statement misstates the effect of family control on principal-agent risk.
> 5. **C.** An independent director has no material relationship with the company regarding employment, ownership, or remuneration, and Olmos's consulting fee is a remuneration relationship, so he is not independent.
> Independence ratio $= 4 / 14 = 0.2857$, or 28.6%, below the code's one-third ($0.3333$).
> Under comply-or-explain, the company must disclose that it does not follow the recommendation and explain why.
> A is wrong because it accepts the company's classification ($5 / 14 = 0.3571$), treating a paid consultant as independent.
> B is wrong because it tests the ratio against the typical OECD range rather than against the national code that actually governs Ceibal Alimentos.
> 6. **B.** Board committees should be sufficiently independent, and a non-independent member on the remuneration committee, here a relative of the CEO, taints the integrity of executive compensation decisions.
> A is wrong because say-on-pay gives shareholders a voice on executive remuneration, which reduces rather than increases governance risk.
> C is wrong because CEO duality means one person holds both roles; here the chair and the CEO are different people, which supports the board's oversight role.

## Distractor design

1. B: pyramid layer skipped (holding company stake not multiplied by the family's 70%); C: voting share substituted for cash flow share.
2. A: shares counted instead of votes (dual-class weighting ignored); B: misread exhibit (Class B shares held by the holding company omitted).
3. A: ownership percentage below 50% read as absence of a controller (wrong cell of the ownership-voting matrix); C: voting caps confused with dual-class control.
4. B: sign of the family-control effect on principal-agent risk reversed; C: accepts the reversed claim alongside the correct bank conflict.
5. A: company classification accepted without applying the remuneration test; B: wrong benchmark (OECD range used in place of the governing national code).
6. A: shareholder-protective feature read as a risk; C: two related people in separate roles mistaken for CEO duality.
