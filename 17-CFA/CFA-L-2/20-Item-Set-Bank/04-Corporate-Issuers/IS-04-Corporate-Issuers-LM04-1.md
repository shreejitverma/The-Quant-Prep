# Kestrel Instruments Bid for Orsolo Diagnostics

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions (or 12 for 4). Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Priya Raman, CFA, is an equity analyst at Northgate Capital covering Kestrel Instruments, a US maker of laboratory analyzers with a BBB credit rating.
On 20 April 2026, Kestrel announced an agreement to acquire Orsolo Diagnostics, a listed maker of reagents and test kits sold to the same hospital laboratories.
Each Orsolo shareholder will receive USD12.00 in cash plus 0.30 Kestrel shares per Orsolo share.
Kestrel will fund the cash portion with a new USD800 million term loan and USD280 million of cash on hand, and it will assume all of Orsolo's existing debt.
Orsolo shares traded near USD24 until press reports of a possible bid appeared on 13 April 2026.
Kestrel shares fell 4% on the announcement day.

**Exhibit 1: Market data before the announcement (USD millions, except per-share data)**

| Item | Kestrel | Orsolo |
|---|---|---|
| Shares outstanding (millions) | 420 | 90 |
| Share price, 17 April 2026 (last close before announcement) | 62.00 | 27.50 |
| Share price, 10 April 2026 | 61.40 | 24.00 |
| Total debt | 5,200 | 850 |
| Cash and equivalents | 1,300 | 110 |

**Exhibit 2: Year 1 standalone forecasts (USD millions)**

| Line item | Kestrel | Orsolo |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 9,800 | 1,450 |
| Operating expenses excluding D&A | 7,450 | 1,090 |
| Depreciation and amortization (D&A) | 520 | 95 |
| Interest expense | 290 | 55 |
| Interest income | 39 | 0 |
| Net income | 1,200.0 | 159.6 |

**Exhibit 3: Deal assumptions for Year 1**

| Assumption | Value |
|---|---|
| Run-rate cost synergies | USD150 million, one-third realized in Year 1 |
| One-time integration costs in Year 1 | USD90 million |
| Amortization of acquired intangible assets | USD120 million per year |
| Interest rate on the new term loan | 6.0% |
| Interest rate earned on cash | 3.0% |
| Tax rate, both companies and combined | 24% |

**Exhibit 4: Kestrel component costs of capital**

| Component | Pre-deal | Post-deal |
|---|---|---|
| Before-tax cost of debt | 5.4% | 5.8% |
| Cost of equity | 9.4% | 9.6% |

For capital structure work, Raman assumes book debt equals market value, Kestrel's share price stays at USD62.00, and cash is not netted against debt.

On an investor call on 22 April 2026, Kestrel's CFO, Daniel Okafor, explained that the board had also considered buying a 30% stake in Orsolo or forming a jointly owned reagent venture with Orsolo.
Okafor then stated: "Our share price is near an all-time high, and high security prices are a well-documented driver of acquisition activity. The USD150 million of savings will come from closing two overlapping reagent plants, a cost synergy based on economies of scale."

In a note dated 23 April 2026, Raman's colleague Lena Fischer wrote: "Because Kestrel is buying in a strong economy at a high share price, the deal is more likely to create shareholder value than an otherwise identical deal struck during a recession."

## Questions

1. Using the size dimension of materiality, the transaction value as a percentage of Kestrel's pre-announcement enterprise value, and the resulting conclusion, are closest to: (LOS: los-04-04-b)
   - A. 9.2%, not a large transaction.
   - B. 12.0%, a large transaction.
   - C. 13.8%, a large transaction.
2. For premium paid analysis, the takeover premium Raman should record for Orsolo is closest to: (LOS: los-04-04-c)
   - A. 11.3%.
   - B. 21.6%.
   - C. 27.5%.
3. Kestrel's Year 1 pro forma EPS is closest to: (LOS: los-04-04-d)
   - A. USD2.67.
   - B. USD2.85.
   - C. USD2.88.
4. Kestrel's post-deal weighted average cost of capital is closest to: (LOS: los-04-04-d)
   - A. 8.52%.
   - B. 8.57%.
   - C. 8.85%.
5. Regarding the alternatives the board considered, which statement is most accurate? (LOS: los-04-04-e)
   - A. A 30% equity investment would have allowed Kestrel to consolidate Orsolo's revenue and capture the plant synergies at a fraction of the acquisition's cost.
   - B. A jointly owned reagent venture would have given Kestrel control of the reagent business with less capital than the acquisition.
   - C. Only the acquisition gives Kestrel control of Orsolo's operations, and it requires the largest capital commitment of the three structures.
6. Regarding the statements by Okafor and Fischer, which is most likely correct? (LOS: los-04-04-a)
   - A. Okafor's statement only.
   - B. Fischer's statement only.
   - C. Both statements.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Transaction value is cash paid plus stock issued plus debt assumed.
> Cash paid $= 12.00 \times 90 = 1{,}080$; new shares $= 0.30 \times 90 = 27$ million, worth $27 \times 62.00 = 1{,}674$; assumed debt $= 850$; $TV = 1{,}080 + 1{,}674 + 850 = 3{,}604$.
> Kestrel's pre-announcement EV $= 420 \times 62.00 + 5{,}200 - 1{,}300 = 26{,}040 + 3{,}900 = 29{,}940$.
> $TV/EV = 3{,}604/29{,}940 = 0.1204 = 12.04\%$, above the 10% rule of thumb, so the deal is large by size.
> A is wrong because it omits the assumed Orsolo debt: $2{,}754/29{,}940 = 9.20\%$.
> C is wrong because it divides by market capitalization instead of enterprise value: $3{,}604/26{,}040 = 13.84\%$; the conclusion happens to match, but the measure is not the one the rule of thumb uses.
> 2. **C.** The deal price per Orsolo share is $DP = 12.00 + 0.30 \times 62.00 = 30.60$.
> The unaffected price must predate the rumor run-up that began on 13 April, so $SP = 24.00$ (10 April).
> $PRM = (30.60 - 24.00)/24.00 = 0.2750 = 27.50\%$.
> A is wrong because it uses the rumor-inflated last close: $(30.60 - 27.50)/27.50 = 11.27\%$, which understates the price of control.
> B is wrong because it divides by the deal price instead of the unaffected price: $(30.60 - 24.00)/30.60 = 21.57\%$.
> 3. **A.** Combined revenue $= 9{,}800 + 1{,}450 = 11{,}250$.
> Operating expenses $= 7{,}450 + 1{,}090 - 50 + 90 = 8{,}580$, where Year 1 synergies are $150/3 = 50$; EBITDA $= 2{,}670$.
> D&A $= 520 + 95 + 120 = 735$; EBIT $= 1{,}935$.
> Interest expense $= 290 + 55 + 0.06 \times 800 = 393.0000$; interest income $= 39 - 0.03 \times 280 = 30.6000$.
> EBT $= 1{,}935 - 393.0000 + 30.6000 = 1{,}572.6000$; tax $= 0.24 \times 1{,}572.6000 = 377.4240$; net income $= 1{,}195.1760$.
> Shares $= 420 + 27 = 447$ million; pro forma EPS $= 1{,}195.1760/447 = 2.6738$.
> Standalone EPS is $1{,}200.0/420 = 2.8572$, so the deal is dilutive by about USD0.18 even though EBITDA rises.
> B is wrong because it divides by the pre-deal 420 million shares: $1{,}195.1760/420 = 2.8457$.
> C is wrong because it omits the after-tax intangible amortization: $(1{,}195.1760 + 120 \times 0.76)/447 = 1{,}286.3760/447 = 2.8778$, which would wrongly suggest accretion.
> 4. **B.** Post-deal debt $= 5{,}200 + 800 + 850 = 6{,}850$; post-deal equity $= 447 \times 62.00 = 27{,}714$.
> $w_d = 6{,}850/34{,}564 = 0.1982$ and $w_e = 0.8018$.
> $WACC = 0.1982 \times 5.8\% \times (1 - 0.24) + 0.8018 \times 9.6\% = 0.8736\% + 7.6975\% = 8.5710\%$.
> A is wrong because it is the pre-deal WACC, with pre-deal weights ($w_d = 5{,}200/31{,}240 = 0.1665$) and pre-deal costs: $0.1665 \times 5.4\% \times 0.76 + 0.8335 \times 9.4\% = 8.5185\%$; it ignores how the deal changes both weights and component costs.
> C is wrong because it omits the debt tax shield: $0.1982 \times 5.8\% + 0.8018 \times 9.6\% = 8.8469\%$.
> 5. **C.** Among equity investment, joint venture, and acquisition, only an acquisition transfers control, and it requires substantially more capital than the other two.
> A is wrong because a 30% stake is accounted for with the equity method: Kestrel would record one line of share of profit, with no revenue consolidation, and could not force the plant closures behind the synergies.
> B is wrong because a joint venture is jointly controlled; it gives more equal governance representation than an equity investment, not control.
> 6. **A.** Okafor is correct on both points: high security prices are a top-down driver of restructuring activity (CEO confidence, cheaper financing, overvalued stock as currency), and savings from consolidating plants are cost synergies from economies of scale.
> Fischer is wrong because the evidence runs the other way: deals struck in weak economies have created roughly 10% more three-year shareholder return than deals struck in strong economies.
> B and C are wrong because each endorses Fischer's reversed claim.

## Distractor design

1. A omits assumed debt from transaction value; C uses market capitalization instead of pre-announcement enterprise value as the denominator.
2. A uses the rumor-inflated price as the unaffected price; B divides by the deal price instead of the unaffected price.
3. B divides by the old share count; C forgets the intangible amortization and so reports accretion.
4. A keeps pre-deal weights and costs, ignoring the restructuring; C omits the $(1 - t)$ tax shield on debt.
5. A attributes consolidation to an equity-method stake; B attributes control to a jointly controlled venture.
6. B and C accept the reversed weak-economy versus strong-economy evidence.
