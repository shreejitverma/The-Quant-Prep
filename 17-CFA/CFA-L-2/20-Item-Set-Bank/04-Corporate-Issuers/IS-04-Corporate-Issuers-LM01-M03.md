# Ardmore Water: Franking Credits and a Share Split

> [!info] Timer
> 12 minutes for 4 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Fiona Achterberg, CFA, is the investment officer of Cressida Superannuation Fund, an Australian retirement fund.
Cressida is taxed at a concessional 15% rate on its investment income.
The fund holds 12,000 shares of Ardmore Water Ltd, a regulated water and wastewater utility that reports in Australian dollars (AUD).
Australia operates a dividend imputation system, and Ardmore's dividends are fully franked at the 30% corporate tax rate.
Achterberg gathers the data in Exhibit 1.

Exhibit 1: Ardmore Water, selected data

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Common shares outstanding | 150 million |
| Current share price | AUD 96.60 |
| Earnings per share, most recent year | AUD 5.40 |
| Annual dividend per share, fully franked, paid in cash | AUD 4.20 |
| Corporate tax rate | 30% |
| Cressida's tax rate on investment income | 15% |
| Top marginal tax rate for individual investors | 45% |
| Flotation costs on Ardmore's recent share issues | 5% of proceeds |

Ardmore has paid out close to 78% of its earnings for more than a decade.
Every two to three years it issues new shares to fund network expansion approved by the state regulator, which also sets the prices Ardmore may charge its customers.
Ardmore's board has announced a three-for-one stock split, effective next month, to bring the share price into a range that is more accessible to retail investors.

Achterberg meets with Ardmore's chief financial officer, Declan Oyelaran.
Oyelaran describes a debate at the most recent board meeting.
One director proposed eliminating the dividend entirely and funding network expansion from retained earnings, so that Ardmore would no longer pay flotation costs on new shares.
A second director replied that the proposal would not harm any shareholder: "An investor who needs income can simply sell a few shares each year and receive exactly the same cash."
Oyelaran notes that the board has not taken a decision.

Back at the fund, Achterberg asks her analyst, Tomas Brekke, to prepare three calculations for the fund's investment committee.
First, she wants the after-tax cash the fund receives from one year of Ardmore dividends, including the effect of franking credits.
Second, she wants to test the second director's argument under Miller and Modigliani's assumptions of no taxes, no transaction costs and symmetric information.
Specifically, she asks how many shares the fund would have to sell, at pre-split prices, to replicate its current annual cash dividend if Ardmore paid no dividend.
Third, she wants to know how the split will affect the value of the fund's position and the income yield the committee reports.

Brekke raises a question of his own.
"Ardmore pays out most of what it earns and then pays 5% flotation costs to raise equity again," he says.
"That round trip makes no sense unless the dividend is doing something that retained cash cannot."

Achterberg ignores transaction costs other than flotation costs, and assumes all other things are equal unless a question states otherwise.

## Questions

1. The after-tax cash that Cressida receives from one year of Ardmore dividends on its 12,000 shares, including any refund of franking credits, is closest to: (LOS: los-04-01-f)
   - A. AUD 42,840.
   - B. AUD 50,400.
   - C. AUD 61,200.
2. Under the first director's proposal and Miller and Modigliani's assumptions, the number of shares Cressida would need to sell at pre-split prices to replicate its current annual cash dividend is closest to: (LOS: los-04-01-b)
   - A. 522.
   - B. 545.
   - C. 634.
3. Immediately after the stock split, the market value of Cressida's Ardmore position and Ardmore's dividend yield are closest to: (LOS: los-04-01-a)
   - A. AUD 1,159,200 and 1.45%.
   - B. AUD 1,159,200 and 4.35%.
   - C. AUD 3,477,600 and 4.35%.
4. The practice Brekke describes is best explained by the fact that: (LOS: los-04-01-d)
   - A. raising equity repeatedly subjects managers and the regulator to the equity market's pricing of the new shares, which monitors both.
   - B. a high dividend payout reduces the default risk borne by Ardmore's lenders, which lowers Ardmore's overall cost of capital.
   - C. investors value a unit of dividends more highly than a unit of uncertain capital gains, so the high payout lowers the cost of equity by more than the flotation costs.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **C.** Under imputation, distributed earnings are taxed once at the shareholder's own rate, and the franking credit for corporate tax paid is refundable when the shareholder's rate is below the corporate rate (V4 p.23).
> Grossed-up pretax earnings per share $= 4.20 / (1 - 0.30) = 6.0000$.
> Tax at Cressida's rate $= 0.15 \times 6.00 = 0.9000$; franking credit $= 0.30 \times 6.00 = 1.8000$; refund $= 1.80 - 0.90 = 0.9000$.
> After-tax cash per share $= 4.20 + 0.90 = 5.1000$, and for the fund $12{,}000 \times 5.10 = 61{,}200$.
> Equivalently, the effective tax rate on the 6.00 of pretax earnings is Cressida's 15%, and $6.00 \times (1 - 0.15) = 5.10$.
> A is wrong because it applies double-taxation logic, taxing the cash dividend at 15% with no credit: $12{,}000 \times 4.20 \times (1 - 0.15) = 42{,}840$.
> B is wrong because it lets the franking credit offset the fund's tax but not produce a refund, leaving only the cash dividend: $12{,}000 \times 4.20 = 50{,}400$.
> 2. **A.** The cash dividend to be replicated is $12{,}000 \times 4.20 = 50{,}400$.
> If no dividend is paid, there is no ex-dividend price drop, so under MM the shares still trade at 96.60 when the fund sells: $50{,}400 / 96.60 = 521.7391$, about 522 shares (V4 p.12).
> Check: the fund keeps $12{,}000 - 521.7391 = 11{,}478.2609$ shares worth $11{,}478.2609 \times 96.60 = 1{,}108{,}800$, plus 50,400 of cash, a total of 1,159,200.
> With the dividend, it holds 12,000 shares at the ex-dividend price of $96.60 - 4.20 = 92.40$, worth 1,108,800, plus 50,400 of cash, the same 1,159,200, which is the homemade dividend argument.
> B is wrong because it sells at the ex-dividend price, which applies only when a dividend is paid: $50{,}400 / 92.40 = 545.4545$.
> C is wrong because it tries to replicate the after-tax franked cash from Question 1 rather than the cash dividend: $61{,}200 / 96.60 = 633.5404$; a homemade dividend cannot generate franking credits, and MM's world has no taxes.
> 3. **B.** A three-for-one split triples the share count and divides every per-share amount by 3 (V4 p.8, p.9).
> New price $= 96.60 / 3 = 32.20$ and new DPS $= 4.20 / 3 = 1.40$.
> Position value $= 36{,}000 \times 32.20 = 1{,}159{,}200$, unchanged from $12{,}000 \times 96.60$.
> Dividend yield $= 1.40 / 32.20 = 4.3478\%$, unchanged from $4.20 / 96.60$, because no cash enters or leaves the company.
> A is wrong because it rescales the dividend but divides it by the pre-split price: $1.40 / 96.60 = 1.4493\%$.
> C is wrong because it triples the share count without rescaling the price, $36{,}000 \times 96.60 = 3{,}477{,}600$, as if the split created wealth.
> 4. **A.** This is the regulated utility case: paying high dividends and then issuing equity forces Ardmore back to the equity market repeatedly (V4 p.18, p.19).
> If managers are inefficient or the regulator sets prices too low, the new shares are poorly received, which pressures both managers and the regulator; the flotation cost is the price of this monitoring, which resolves the manager-shareholder and regulator-shareholder conflicts.
> B is wrong because distributions thin the cash cushion behind debt and increase, rather than reduce, the default risk borne by lenders (V4 p.17, p.18).
> C is wrong because it restates the bird-in-the-hand argument, which MM rebut and which does not explain why the company would then raise the same cash again at a cost (V4 p.12).

## Distractor design

1. A: double-taxation formula applied in an imputation country (wrong tax system); B: franking credit treated as non-refundable (wrong formula variant).
2. B: ex-dividend price used when no dividend is paid (misread exhibit); C: after-tax franked cash replicated instead of the cash dividend (before-tax vs after-tax).
3. A: DPS rescaled but price not (mismatched per-share basis); C: share count rescaled but price not, so the split appears to create wealth (wealth effect misread).
4. B: direction of the shareholder-bondholder effect reversed (sign); C: bird-in-the-hand theory substituted for the agency explanation (wrong theory).
