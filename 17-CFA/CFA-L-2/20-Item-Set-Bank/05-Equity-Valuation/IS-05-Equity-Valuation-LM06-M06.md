# Karuna Veterinary Group: A Private Equity Buyout

> [!info] Timer
> 12 minutes for 4 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Fiona Achterberg, CFA, is a vice president at Southgate Capital Partners, an Australian mid-market private equity fund.
Southgate is negotiating to acquire 100% of Karuna Veterinary Group Pty Ltd., a private operator of 14 veterinary clinics in Queensland.
Karuna's founder, Dr. Anil Karuna, owns 65% of the shares, his wife Dr. Meera Karuna owns 30%, and the remaining 5% is held by the group's practice manager, who is Anil Karuna's sister.
Southgate owns no other veterinary or animal health businesses, and Achterberg expects no operating synergies from the acquisition.

Normalization adjustments for the founders' compensation and a clinic leased from a family trust have already been agreed with the sellers, and Achterberg works only from normalized figures.

Exhibit 1: Karuna normalized financial data (AUD millions)

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Normalized EBITDA, most recent year | 13.0 |
| Normalized FCFF, most recent year | 9.0 |
| FCFF growth rate, years 1 to 3 | 6.0% |
| FCFF growth rate, year 4 onward | 3.0% |
| Debt outstanding, face value | 12.0 |
| Debt outstanding, market value | 11.4 |

Achterberg estimates Karuna's cost of equity at 12.5% before any adjustment for projection risk.
Because the sellers' three-year plan assumes that newly opened clinics reach mature patient volumes much faster than Karuna's older clinics did, she adds 1.5 percentage points to the cost of equity for projection risk.
She weights the cost of capital at the optimal capital structure, consistent with an acquirer establishing that structure after the purchase, which Southgate's lenders have confirmed they will finance.

Exhibit 2: Capital structure and cost of capital inputs

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Pre-tax cost of debt | 7.0% |
| Tax rate | 30% |
| Debt to total capital, Karuna actual | 8% |
| Debt to total capital, optimal for Karuna | 25% |
| Debt to total capital, listed veterinary groups | 40% |
| Southgate portfolio average WACC | 9.5% |

Achterberg values Karuna with a two-stage FCFF model, with a terminal value at the end of year 3 based on the capitalized cash flow method.

As a cross-check, she applies the guideline public company method using listed veterinary groups, which trade at a median EV/EBITDA of 8.0 and a mean of 8.9.
Because the trading multiples reflect marketable minority interests, she adjusts the resulting equity value to a controlling basis using acquisition data.
She does not apply a marketability discount to a 100% interest being acquired through an active sale process.

Exhibit 3: Control premiums in Australian healthcare services acquisitions, 2021 to 2025

| Buyer type | Median control premium |
|---|---|
| All acquisitions | 32% |
| Strategic buyers | 40% |
| Financial buyers | 22% |

Achterberg's colleague, Declan Rourke, points to a share transfer 30 months ago, when Anil Karuna sold the 5% stake to his sister at a price implying a value of AUD 60 million for 100% of the equity.
The sister paid with a loan from Anil Karuna at an interest rate two percentage points below market rates.
Rourke says: "The best evidence of what Karuna is worth is what its own shares have actually sold for.
We should make the prior transaction method our primary approach and treat the DCF only as a cross-check."

## Questions

1. The WACC that Achterberg uses to value Karuna is closest to: (LOS: los-05-06-d)
   - A. 10.60%.
   - B. 11.73%.
   - C. 13.27%.
2. Using Achterberg's WACC, Karuna's equity value from the two-stage FCFF model is closest to: (LOS: los-05-06-h)
   - A. AUD 103.66 million.
   - B. AUD 106.31 million.
   - C. AUD 115.06 million.
3. Achterberg's controlling-basis equity value for Karuna from the guideline public company cross-check is closest to: (LOS: los-05-06-f)
   - A. AUD 112.97 million.
   - B. AUD 118.72 million.
   - C. AUD 129.64 million.
4. Rourke's proposal to make the prior transaction method the primary approach is best evaluated as: (LOS: los-05-06-g)
   - A. appropriate, because transactions in the subject company's own shares are the most direct market evidence of its value.
   - B. inappropriate, because the only transaction is stale, was not at arm's length, and was for a minority stake, so it is unlikely to indicate the value of a controlling interest.
   - C. inappropriate, because the prior transaction method is an income-based method that requires reliable projections, which Karuna lacks.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Cost of equity including the projection risk adjustment $= 12.5\% + 1.5\% = 14.0\%$.
> After-tax cost of debt $= 7.0\% \times (1 - 0.30) = 4.90\%$.
> At the optimal structure: $WACC = 0.25 \times 4.90\% + 0.75 \times 14.0\% = 1.225\% + 10.500\% = 11.725\%$, or about 11.73%.
> A is wrong because it omits the projection risk adjustment ($0.25 \times 4.90\% + 0.75 \times 12.5\% = 10.60\%$), leaving the optimistic plan's extra risk unpriced.
> C is wrong because it weights at Karuna's actual structure ($0.08 \times 4.90\% + 0.92 \times 14.0\% = 13.272\%$); a private firm with limited debt has a WACC above its optimal-structure WACC, but Achterberg explicitly uses the optimal structure.
> The 40% debt weight of the listed groups and Southgate's 9.5% portfolio WACC are decoys: the target's own risk and achievable structure govern, not the comparables' leverage or the buyer's cost of capital.
> 2. **A.** FCFF: $FCFF_1 = 9.0 \times 1.06 = 9.5400$, $FCFF_2 = 10.1124$, $FCFF_3 = 10.7191$ million.
> Terminal value at year 3: $10.7191 \times 1.03 / (0.11725 - 0.03) = 11.0407 / 0.08725 = 126.5412$ million.
> Present value of the three flows at 11.725% $= 24.3263$ million; present value of the terminal value $= 126.5412 / 1.11725^3 = 90.7362$ million.
> Firm value $= 24.3263 + 90.7362 = 115.0625$ million.
> Equity value $= 115.0625 - 11.4 = 103.6625$, or about AUD 103.66 million, subtracting the market value, not the face value, of debt.
> B is wrong because it grows the terminal numerator at the stage-one rate of 6% instead of the 3% terminal rate ($10.7191 \times 1.06 / 0.08725 = 130.2268$, firm value $117.7053$, equity $106.3053$).
> C is wrong because it reports firm value and does not subtract the debt.
> 3. **A.** Enterprise value $= 8.0 \times 13.0 = 104.0$ million, using the median as the vignette states; equity value on a marketable minority basis $= 104.0 - 11.4 = 92.6$ million.
> Southgate is a financial buyer with no synergies, so the financial-buyer control premium of 22% applies: $92.6 \times 1.22 = 112.972$ million.
> B is wrong because it grosses up as if 22% were a discount off control value ($92.6 / (1 - 0.22) = 118.7179$); a control premium is applied as $(1 + CP)$, and only the reverse move, from control to minority, uses $1 - 1/(1 + CP)$.
> C is wrong because it uses the strategic-buyer premium of 40% ($92.6 \times 1.40 = 129.64$), which pays for synergies Southgate cannot capture.
> 4. **B.** The prior transaction method uses actual trades in the subject's own shares, and its usefulness depends on those trades being recent, at arm's length, and on the same basis as the interest being valued.
> The single trade is 30 months old, was between related parties with concessionary seller financing, and was for a 5% non-controlling stake, so it does not indicate the fair market value of a 100% controlling interest.
> A is wrong because it states the method's appeal in principle but ignores that this trade fails the arm's length and timeliness tests.
> C is wrong because the prior transaction method is a market approach method, not an income approach method.

## Distractor design

1. A: projection risk adjustment omitted (discount rate factor ignored); C: actual instead of optimal capital structure weights (wrong structure for a sale).
2. B: terminal numerator grown at the stage-one rate (wrong growth rate in the terminal value); C: firm value reported as equity value (debt not subtracted).
3. B: control premium applied as if it were a discount (premium versus discount confusion); C: strategic premium used for a financial buyer (synergies paid for that do not exist).
4. A: method accepted without testing arm's length and staleness; C: market approach method misclassified as an income approach method.
