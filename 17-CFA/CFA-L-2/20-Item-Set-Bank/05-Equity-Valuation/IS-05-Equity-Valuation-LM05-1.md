# Harlow Precision Components: An EVA Bonus Plan

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Priya Nandakumar, CFA, is a compensation consultant retained by the board of Harlow Precision Components Inc., a US manufacturer of machined parts for aerospace and medical equipment customers.
The compensation committee wants to replace its earnings-per-share bonus target with a measure that charges managers for all of the capital they use.
Nandakumar begins with Harlow's reported figures for the most recent fiscal year, shown in Exhibit 1.

Exhibit 1: Harlow Precision Components, selected data (USD millions, except per-share data and rates)

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Total assets | 5,600 |
| Interest-bearing debt, book value, beginning of year | 1,800 |
| Shareholders' equity, book value, beginning of year | 2,700 |
| EBIT | 720 |
| Pretax cost of debt | 6.0% |
| Tax rate | 25% |
| Cost of equity | 11.0% |
| Weighted average cost of capital (book weights) | 8.4% |
| Shares outstanding (millions) | 120 |
| Share price at year end (USD) | 31.50 |
| Market value of debt at year end | 1,750 |
| Nandakumar's residual income model value per share (USD) | 28.40 |

The difference between total assets and the sum of debt and equity consists of non-interest-bearing operating liabilities such as trade payables.
Harlow paid out all of its net income as dividends during the year and issued no debt or shares, so its book values of debt and shareholders' equity at year end equal the beginning-of-year amounts in Exhibit 1.

Nandakumar then applies EVA-style adjustments.
She capitalizes the development spending that Harlow expenses, replaces deferred tax expense with cash taxes, and removes a nonrecurring litigation charge.
Her adjusted figures are shown in Exhibit 2.

Exhibit 2: Adjusted figures used for EVA (USD millions)

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Adjusted NOPAT | 585 |
| Adjusted total capital | 4,900 |

At the committee meeting, two directors comment on the proposal.

Committee chair Dana Whitfield: "Commercial residual income products such as EVA are marketed mainly as tools for valuing a company's shares, so using one to set executive pay is an unusual application of the idea."

Director Martin Osei: "Harlow's aerospace division earns a return on equity almost exactly equal to its cost of equity.
Doubling that division's growth rate would add little or nothing to Harlow's intrinsic value."

Chief Financial Officer Graham Pruitt then proposes that Harlow capitalize USD 90 million of development costs that it currently expenses, which he says would lift the return on equity reported to the committee.
For a first comparison, Nandakumar follows her firm's convention of ignoring tax and amortization effects and restating both fiscal year net income and beginning shareholders' equity by the full USD 90 million.
She warns the committee that the analyst community tends to extrapolate reported return on equity when forecasting residual income.

## Questions

1. Harlow's residual income for the fiscal year is closest to: (LOS: los-05-05-a)
   - A. USD -36 million.
   - B. USD 135 million.
   - C. USD 162 million.
2. Harlow's economic value added based on Nandakumar's adjusted figures is closest to: (LOS: los-05-05-a)
   - A. USD 128.4 million.
   - B. USD 173.4 million.
   - C. USD 207.0 million.
3. Harlow's market value added at year end is closest to: (LOS: los-05-05-a)
   - A. USD 1,030 million.
   - B. USD 1,080 million.
   - C. USD 2,830 million.
4. Whitfield's comment is best described as: (LOS: los-05-05-b)
   - A. correct, because residual income models are designed to value equity rather than to measure managerial performance.
   - B. incorrect, because commercial implementations are marketed mainly for measuring internal corporate performance and determining executive compensation.
   - C. incorrect, because residual income models cannot be used to estimate the intrinsic value of common equity.
5. Osei's comment is best described as: (LOS: los-05-05-d)
   - A. correct, because residual income is driven by the spread of return on equity over the cost of equity applied to book value, and a zero spread produces zero residual income at any growth rate.
   - B. incorrect, because faster growth in book value always increases residual income and therefore value.
   - C. incorrect, because a division is worth more than its book value whenever its return on equity is positive.
6. Using Nandakumar's convention, Harlow's return on equity after Pruitt's proposed capitalization is closest to: (LOS: los-05-05-k)
   - A. 17.00%.
   - B. 19.68%.
   - C. 20.33%.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **C.** Interest expense $= 0.06 \times 1{,}800 = 108$, so pretax income $= 720 - 108 = 612$ and net income $= 612 \times (1 - 0.25) = 459$.
> Equity charge $= 0.11 \times 2{,}700 = 297$, so $RI = 459 - 297 = 162$.
> Check with the capital charge approach: $NOPAT = 720 \times 0.75 = 540$; after-tax debt charge $= 0.06 \times 0.75 \times 1{,}800 = 81$; total capital charge $= 297 + 81 = 378$; $RI = 540 - 378 = 162$.
> A is wrong because it charges the cost of equity against total capital: $459 - 0.11 \times 4{,}500 = -36$.
> B is wrong because it uses the pretax cost of debt in the capital charge: $540 - (297 + 108) = 135$.
> Charging the cost of equity against total assets, the decoy in Exhibit 1, would give $459 - 0.11 \times 5{,}600 = -157$.
> 2. **B.** $EVA = NOPAT - C\% \times TC = 585 - 0.084 \times 4{,}900 = 585 - 411.6 = 173.4$.
> A is wrong because it pairs unadjusted NOPAT with adjusted capital: $540 - 411.6 = 128.4$.
> C is wrong because it pairs adjusted NOPAT with unadjusted capital: $585 - 0.084 \times 4{,}500 = 585 - 378 = 207.0$.
> The adjustments must be applied to both NOPAT and capital, because capitalizing development spending raises both.
> 3. **A.** Market value of the company $= 1{,}750 + 31.50 \times 120 = 1{,}750 + 3{,}780 = 5{,}530$; book value of total capital $= 1{,}800 + 2{,}700 = 4{,}500$; $MVA = 5{,}530 - 4{,}500 = 1{,}030$.
> B is wrong because it uses the book value of debt instead of its market value: $1{,}800 + 3{,}780 - 4{,}500 = 1{,}080$, which is simply market value of equity less book value of equity.
> C is wrong because it subtracts only book equity from the market value of debt plus equity: $5{,}530 - 2{,}700 = 2{,}830$.
> Using the 28.40 model value instead of the market price, the other decoy, would give $1{,}750 + 3{,}408 - 4{,}500 = 658$.
> 4. **B.** Residual income models are used for equity valuation, internal performance measurement and executive compensation, but the commercial implementations are marketed mainly for measuring internal corporate performance and setting executive compensation.
> Whitfield has the emphasis reversed, so a bonus plan is exactly the use they are sold for.
> A is wrong because it repeats Whitfield's reversal.
> C is wrong because residual income models are a standard equity valuation method; $V_0 = B_0 + \sum PV(RI_t)$ is the DDM rewritten through clean surplus accounting.
> 5. **A.** Residual income per period is $(ROE - r) \times B_{t-1}$, so its fundamental determinants are book value and the spread of return on equity over the cost of equity.
> With $ROE = r$ the spread is zero, residual income is zero whatever the growth in book value, and value equals book value.
> B is wrong because growth adds value only when the spread is positive; with a zero spread, more book value simply earns exactly its cost.
> C is wrong because value exceeds book only when return on equity exceeds the cost of equity, not whenever it is positive.
> 6. **B.** Expensed: $ROE = 459 / 2{,}700 = 0.1700$.
> Capitalized: both earnings and book value rise by 90, so $ROE = (459 + 90)/(2{,}700 + 90) = 549 / 2{,}790 = 0.1968$, or 19.68%.
> An analyst who extrapolates 19.68% would overvalue Harlow, because the higher return reflects an accounting choice rather than economic profitability; for a company that keeps growing its capitalized spending, the inflated return can persist.
> A is wrong because it assumes the accounting choice leaves return on equity unchanged, a misreading of Bauman's offsetting argument, which holds for value only under clean surplus accounting and does not stop reported return on equity from changing.
> C is wrong because it adds 90 to earnings but not to book value: $549 / 2{,}700 = 0.2033$.

## Distractor design

1. A: cost of equity charged against total capital instead of equity; B: pretax rather than after-tax cost of debt in the capital charge.
2. A: unadjusted NOPAT with adjusted capital; C: adjusted NOPAT with unadjusted capital (adjustments applied to one side only).
3. B: book value of debt used in place of market value; C: only book equity subtracted, omitting book debt from total capital.
4. A: commercial use reversed (valuation versus compensation); C: overcorrection that denies the valuation use altogether.
5. B: growth treated as a value driver independent of the ROE spread; C: positive ROE confused with ROE above the cost of equity.
6. A: capitalization assumed to leave ROE unchanged; C: numerator restated but denominator left at the expensed book value.
