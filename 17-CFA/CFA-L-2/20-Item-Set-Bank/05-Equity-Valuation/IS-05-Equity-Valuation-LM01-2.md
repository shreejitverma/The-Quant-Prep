# Talvik Industrial Group: An Activist Breakup Case

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Maren Holt, CFA, is a senior analyst at Skerry Partners, an activist hedge fund that has built a 4% stake in Talvik Industrial Group Oyj, a listed Finnish holding company.
Talvik has three operating segments in unrelated industries, 90 million shares outstanding, and a share price of EUR 11.40.
Skerry intends to argue publicly that Talvik should separate its businesses.
Holt prepares a sum-of-the-parts valuation, valuing each segment with the method she judges most appropriate for it.

Exhibit 1: Talvik segment data and Holt's valuation inputs (EUR millions unless stated)

| Segment | Ownership | Holt's method | Inputs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timberlands | 100% | Asset-based | 8.0 million cubic metres of harvestable standing timber; delivered timber price EUR 61 per cubic metre; harvesting and haulage cost EUR 19 per cubic metre |
| Specialty packaging | 100% | EV/EBITDA versus listed packaging peers | Segment EBITDA 150; peer median EV/EBITDA 7.5x; peer mean EV/EBITDA 8.2x |
| Logistics software | 80% | Discounted free cash flow | Value of 100% of the subsidiary's equity 640; the subsidiary has no debt |

Holt's firm policy is to apply peer median rather than peer mean multiples.
Talvik's unallocated head-office costs are EUR 28 million a year after tax, and Holt capitalises them at a multiple of 8.0.
Group net debt, excluding the software subsidiary, is EUR 520 million.

At a meeting with Skerry, Talvik's chief financial officer, Aino Lehto, defends the group structure.
She says the group's long record of paying a stable dividend makes a dividend discount model the natural way to value the company.
Holt replies: "Your dividend has been set for twenty years by reference to packaging cash flow alone, so it tells us almost nothing about how the timber and software businesses create value."

Lehto then acknowledges a practice that Skerry intends to cite: "Each year the head office takes the surplus cash generated by timberlands and allocates it to software development projects, even where those projects have not earned their cost of capital, because timber cash flow is the only internal funding we have for growth."

Holt learns that Norrvik Paper AB, a Swedish pulp and paper producer with mills adjacent to Talvik's forests, would pay a premium for the timberlands alone.
Norrvik estimates that owning the forests would reduce its wood procurement and transport costs by EUR 12 million a year after tax in perpetuity, and it discounts those savings at 8%.

Holt summarises her work for Skerry's investment committee.
She notes that two of the committee members disagree about the nature of her valuation.
One member argues that the whole exercise is relative valuation because the market price of timber and the peer packaging multiples drive most of the result.
The other argues that a sum-of-the-parts total is necessarily an absolute valuation because it adds up segment values.
Holt proposes to present the committee with the number of segment methods in Exhibit 1 that are absolute valuation models, the sum-of-the-parts value per share, and the implied conglomerate discount.

## Questions

1. Talvik's sum-of-the-parts equity value per share is closest to: (LOS: los-05-01-g)
   - A. EUR 13.66.
   - B. EUR 15.08.
   - C. EUR 16.14.
2. The conglomerate discount implied by Talvik's share price is closest to: (LOS: los-05-01-g)
   - A. 16.5%.
   - B. 19.8%.
   - C. 24.4%.
3. The practice Lehto acknowledges is most consistent with which explanation of the conglomerate discount? (LOS: los-05-01-g)
   - A. Inefficiency of internal capital markets.
   - B. Endogenous factors.
   - C. Research measurement error.
4. How many of the three segment valuation methods in Exhibit 1 are absolute valuation models? (LOS: los-05-01-f)
   - A. One.
   - B. Two.
   - C. Three.
5. The value of the timberlands to Norrvik is closest to: (LOS: los-05-01-c)
   - A. EUR 336 million.
   - B. EUR 486 million.
   - C. EUR 638 million.
6. Holt's reply to Lehto's dividend discount proposal most directly applies which model-selection criterion? (LOS: los-05-01-h)
   - A. Appropriateness given the availability and quality of data.
   - B. Consistency with the characteristics of the company being valued.
   - C. Consistency with the purpose of the valuation and the analyst's perspective.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **A.** Timberlands: $8.0 \times (61 - 19) = 8.0 \times 42 = 336.0$.
> Packaging: $150 \times 7.5 = 1{,}125.0$ at the peer median required by policy.
> Software: Talvik's share is $0.80 \times 640 = 512.0$.
> Corporate adjustment: $-28 \times 8.0 = -224.0$.
> $V_{\text{SOTP}} = 336.0 + 1{,}125.0 + 512.0 - 224.0 = 1{,}749.0$; equity $= 1{,}749.0 - 520 = 1{,}229.0$; per share $= 1{,}229.0 / 90 = 13.6556$.
> B is wrong because it includes 100% of the software subsidiary rather than Talvik's 80% ($336 + 1{,}125 + 640 - 224 - 520 = 1{,}357$; $1{,}357 / 90 = 15.0778$).
> C is wrong because it omits the capitalised head-office costs ($336 + 1{,}125 + 512 - 520 = 1{,}453$; $1{,}453 / 90 = 16.1444$).
> Using the gross timber price ($8.0 \times 61 = 488$) would give 15.3444 and the peer mean multiple would give 14.8222; neither is offered, and both break a stated input rule.
> 2. **A.** Conglomerate discount $= (V_{\text{SOTP}} - \text{Market value}) / V_{\text{SOTP}} = (13.6556 - 11.40) / 13.6556 = 2.2556 / 13.6556 = 0.1652$.
> B is wrong because it divides the gap by the market price instead of the sum-of-the-parts value ($2.2556 / 11.40 = 0.1979$).
> C is wrong because it starts from the 100%-software value of Question 1 option B ($(15.0778 - 11.40) / 15.0778 = 0.2439$).
> 3. **A.** Head office redirects one segment's cash into projects that do not earn their cost of capital, which is a misallocation of capital through the internal capital market.
> B is wrong because the endogenous factors explanation says poorly performing companies pursue diversifying acquisitions; nothing in Lehto's statement concerns why Talvik diversified.
> C is wrong because measurement error means the discount may not really exist; Lehto describes a real value-destroying practice, which supports rather than denies the discount.
> 4. **B.** Asset-based valuation (timberlands: harvestable volume times net realisable value per unit) and discounted free cash flow (software) both specify intrinsic value, so both are absolute models.
> The EV/EBITDA method values packaging relative to peers and is a relative valuation model.
> A is wrong because it treats the asset-based method as relative because it uses a market price of timber; using market inputs does not make a model relative, since the output is still the value of the asset itself rather than a value relative to a benchmark.
> C is wrong because it classifies the multiple-based packaging value as absolute because it is added into a sum-of-the-parts total; the model type is set by how each component is valued, not by the aggregation.
> 5. **B.** Standalone timberland value is $336.0$; the present value of Norrvik's synergies is $12 / 0.08 = 150.0$.
> Investment value to Norrvik $= 336.0 + 150.0 = 486.0$.
> A is wrong because it is the standalone value, a fair-market-value style figure that ignores the specific buyer's synergies.
> C is wrong because it adds the synergies to the gross timber value that ignores harvesting and haulage costs ($8.0 \times 61 + 150 = 638.0$).
> 6. **B.** Holt objects that the dividend reflects only packaging cash flow and not how the three unrelated segments generate value, which is a statement about the company's characteristics: its assets and how its cash flows are generated and distributed.
> A is wrong because twenty years of dividends is ample, reliable data; the problem is not availability or quality but that the data describe only one segment.
> C is wrong because Holt's reply says nothing about Skerry's purpose (for example, seeking control and redirecting cash flows); it is about the company, not the valuer.

## Distractor design

1. B: 100% of a partly owned subsidiary included (ownership share omitted); C: corporate cost adjustment omitted from the sum-of-the-parts.
2. B: wrong denominator (gap divided by price rather than by sum-of-the-parts value); C: carries forward the ownership error from Question 1.
3. B: endogenous factors confused with capital misallocation; C: measurement error chosen although the statement confirms a real cause.
4. A: market input read as relative valuation; C: aggregation into a sum-of-the-parts read as making every component absolute.
5. A: investment value confused with standalone value (synergies omitted); C: gross rather than net timber value (extraction cost omitted).
6. A: data availability chosen although the dividend history exists; C: purpose and perspective chosen although the reply concerns only company characteristics.
