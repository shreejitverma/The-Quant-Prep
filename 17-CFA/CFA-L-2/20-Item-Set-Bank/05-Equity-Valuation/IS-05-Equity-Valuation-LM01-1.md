# Ashbury Glassworks: A Special Situations Position

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Tomas Reyes, CFA, is an analyst at Calder Special Situations Fund, a London-based fund that holds positions for a maximum of 18 months.
He is presenting Ashbury Glassworks plc, a listed maker of glass containers for food and beverage bottlers, to the fund's investment committee.
Ashbury has reported net losses in each of the last three years, has suspended its dividend, and has 50 million shares outstanding.
The shares trade at GBP 8.40, and Ashbury's book value per share is GBP 7.20.

Reyes values Ashbury with a present value of free cash flow model and estimates its intrinsic value at GBP 11.20 per share.
He then solves the same model for the long-term growth rate that equates model value with the current market price, holding his other inputs fixed.
The price implies perpetual growth of 1.0%, whereas Reyes forecasts 3.0%, supported by Ashbury's own ten-year history and trade association volume forecasts.

Committee chair Helen Okafor responds with two comments.

Comment 1: "Suppose an independent study later showed that Ashbury's true intrinsic value was GBP 10.10.
Your model would still have told us something useful, but not everything in your gap would have been available to us as return."

Comment 2: "Even if your GBP 11.20 is exactly right, we have 18 months.
Tell me what event forces the market to re-price these shares inside that window."

Because Ashbury is loss-making, Okafor also asks Reyes to establish a downside floor.
He obtains realisation estimates for each asset class from an insolvency practitioner.
All liabilities, GBP 260 million in total, would be settled at their carrying amount, and closure costs (redundancy, legal and environmental) would total GBP 45 million under either sale schedule.

Exhibit 1: Ashbury asset realisation estimates (GBP millions)

| Asset | Carrying amount | Proceeds if sold immediately | Proceeds if sold over 18 months |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | 40 | 40 | 40 |
| Trade receivables | 90 | 72 | 81 |
| Finished glass inventory (nonperishable) | 120 | 54 | 84 |
| Furnaces and plant | 310 | 150 | 205 |
| Freehold land | 60 | 95 | 110 |
| Total | 620 | 411 | 520 |

Separately, Vitrea SpA, a continental glass producer, has approached Ashbury's board.
Vitrea's chief executive told the financial press that the combination would close two overlapping furnaces and share a logistics network, and that Vitrea's price would reflect "what Ashbury is worth to us specifically."

Reyes ends with his industry review of glass containers in the United Kingdom.

Exhibit 2: Industry characteristics noted by Reyes

| Characteristic | Observation |
|---|---|
| Entry | A new float-and-forming furnace costs over GBP 150 million and takes three years to permit and build |
| Buyers | Five large beverage bottlers account for roughly 70% of industry volume |
| Suppliers | Soda ash, a key input, is supplied by two producers |
| Substitutes | Aluminium cans and PET bottles are available, and bottlers can switch lines within one season at low cost |

## Questions

1. If Okafor's hypothetical true intrinsic value in Comment 1 is correct, the abnormal return available to the fund from true mispricing, as a percentage of the current price, is closest to: (LOS: los-05-01-a)
   - A. 13.1%.
   - B. 20.2%.
   - C. 33.3%.
2. Okafor's Comment 2 is best described as a concern about: (LOS: los-05-01-a)
   - A. estimation error in Reyes's intrinsic value estimate.
   - B. the Grossman-Stiglitz argument that prices cannot fully reflect intrinsic value.
   - C. whether price will converge to intrinsic value within the fund's horizon.
3. Based on Exhibit 1, the per-share floor that best reflects an orderly liquidation of Ashbury is closest to: (LOS: los-05-01-b)
   - A. GBP 2.12.
   - B. GBP 4.30.
   - C. GBP 5.20.
4. The value concept that Vitrea's offer price is most likely based on is: (LOS: los-05-01-c)
   - A. fair value.
   - B. investment value.
   - C. fair market value.
5. Reyes's use of the implied growth rate is best described as which application of equity valuation, and which conclusion does it support? (LOS: los-05-01-d)
   - A. Inferring market expectations; Ashbury is undervalued.
   - B. Inferring market expectations; Ashbury is overvalued.
   - C. Evaluating corporate events; Ashbury is undervalued.
6. Which characteristic in Exhibit 2 most likely supports sustained profitability for the UK glass container industry? (LOS: los-05-01-e)
   - A. The cost and time required to build a new furnace.
   - B. The concentration of volume among five large bottlers.
   - C. The small number of soda ash suppliers.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Perceived mispricing decomposes as $V_E - P = (V - P) + (V_E - V)$.
> Only the true mispricing $V - P = 10.10 - 8.40 = 1.70$ can produce abnormal return; as a share of price it is $1.70 / 8.40 = 0.2024$, or 20.2%.
> The perceived gap $V_E - P = 11.20 - 8.40 = 2.80$ contains estimation error $V_E - V = 11.20 - 10.10 = 1.10$, which earns nothing.
> A is wrong because it measures the estimation error ($1.10 / 8.40 = 0.1310$), the one term that contributes nothing to return.
> C is wrong because it takes the whole perceived gap as alpha ($2.80 / 8.40 = 0.3333$), ignoring the error component Okafor highlights.
> 2. **C.** Okafor explicitly accepts the estimate ("even if your GBP 11.20 is exactly right") and asks for an event that forces a re-evaluation within 18 months.
> That is the catalyst requirement: convergence of price to value may not occur within the investor's horizon.
> A is wrong because the comment assumes away estimation error.
> B is wrong because Grossman-Stiglitz explains why prices and values can differ at all, not the timing of convergence for a position already identified as mispriced.
> 3. **B.** Orderly liquidation value uses the 18-month proceeds: $40 + 81 + 84 + 205 + 110 = 520$.
> Net of liabilities and closure costs: $520 - 260 - 45 = 215$, or $215 / 50 = 4.30$ per share.
> A is wrong because it uses the immediate-sale proceeds ($411 - 260 - 45 = 106$, $106 / 50 = 2.12$), which is a forced liquidation value, not an orderly one.
> C is wrong because it omits the closure costs ($520 - 260 = 260$, $260 / 50 = 5.20$); a liquidation floor must be net of the costs of dissolving the company.
> 4. **B.** Investment value is value to a specific buyer, reflecting that buyer's synergies and requirements; Vitrea cites furnace closures and shared logistics and says the price reflects what Ashbury is worth "to us specifically."
> A is wrong because fair value is the financial-reporting measure used for purposes such as impairment testing.
> C is wrong because fair market value is the price between a hypothetical willing buyer and willing seller, not a price that embeds one buyer's synergies.
> 5. **A.** Holding other fundamentals fixed and solving for the growth rate that sets model value equal to price is inferring (extracting) market expectations.
> The market implies 1.0% growth, below Reyes's well-supported 3.0%, so the market's expectations are too pessimistic and the shares are undervalued, consistent with $V_E = 11.20 > P = 8.40$.
> B is wrong because it reverses the logic: a price that implies less growth than can reasonably be achieved is too low, not too high.
> C is wrong because evaluating corporate events concerns how mergers, divestitures or spin-offs change value; Reyes's exercise is a reverse-engineered growth rate, even though the Vitrea approach is also in the vignette.
> 6. **A.** High entry costs and long lead times deter new entrants, which supports industry profitability.
> B is wrong because a few large buyers hold bargaining power; industry profitability is higher when buyers are many.
> C is wrong because two suppliers of a key input hold bargaining power; profitability is higher when suppliers are many.
> The substitutes row also works against profitability, because switching costs are low.

## Distractor design

1. A: estimation error term $V_E - V$ read as alpha; C: whole perceived gap $V_E - P$ read as alpha (decomposition not applied).
2. A: comment misread as doubting the estimate; B: theory of why mispricing exists confused with the timing of convergence (catalyst).
3. A: immediate-sale column used (forced versus orderly liquidation); C: closure costs omitted (gross versus net liquidation value).
4. A: financial-reporting concept substituted; C: hypothetical market-participant concept substituted for a synergistic buyer's value.
5. B: direction reversed when comparing implied with forecast growth; C: wrong application named because a corporate event appears in the vignette.
6. B: buyer power direction reversed (few buyers hurts profitability); C: supplier power direction reversed (few suppliers hurts profitability).
