# Marisco Home Furnishings: Reviewing a Draft Report

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Priya Nandakumar, CFA, is director of research at a sell-side broker in Chicago.
She is reviewing a draft initiation report on Marisco Home Furnishings Inc., a US warehouse-format furniture retailer, written by junior analyst Owen Pratt.
Pratt's draft rates Marisco a buy, describing the company as "a best-in-class operator growing well ahead of its peers."

Exhibit 1: Selected Marisco financial data (USD millions, fiscal years ended 31 December)

| Item | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 1,480 | 1,610 |
| Net income | 151 | 184 |
| Cash flow from operating activities | 139 | 131 |
| Cash flow from investing activities | (47) | (58) |
| Average accounts receivable | 162 | 221 |
| Number of employees | 5,900 | 6,100 |

Nandakumar reads Marisco's notes and records three observations.

- In 2025, Marisco introduced a "flexible financing" plan that lets customers take delivery of furniture and pay within 12 months, and it recognises revenue at delivery.
- The defined benefit pension plan assumes an expected return on plan assets of 7.5%; 35% of plan assets are in bonds, which the plan's actuary expects to return 4.2%, and the remaining 65% are in equities.
- Marisco made three accounting choices in 2025: it extended the depreciable life of store fixtures from 7 to 10 years; it reported a gain on the sale of a surplus distribution centre within operating income; and it switched from capitalising store pre-opening costs to expensing them as incurred.

Pratt's draft contains a valuation section.
He writes: "Marisco trades at 14.0 times forecast earnings, against a median of 17.5 times for listed US home furnishings retailers.
On this basis the shares are 25% undervalued relative to their intrinsic value."
Nandakumar notes that the peer group itself trades at a premium to the broad US equity market this year.

Pratt's draft also quotes Marisco's chief financial officer from the fourth-quarter earnings call.
The chief financial officer said management uses a valuation model for two purposes.
First, the board used it to decide whether Marisco should issue new shares to acquire Hearthline, a smaller listed competitor, and at what exchange ratio.
Second, the company used it to estimate the grant-date value of restricted stock units awarded to store managers under the 2025 incentive plan.

Nandakumar asks Pratt to rework the report before publication.
She tells him that his quality of earnings section must quantify the accrual component of 2025 earnings, the trend in receivables collection, and the equity return implied by the pension assumption.
She also asks him to identify which of Marisco's 2025 accounting choices, if any, should not be treated as a warning sign.
Finally, she asks him to restate the valuation conclusion so that it is accurate given the method he used.
Pratt argues that the receivables increase simply reflects faster sales growth, and that Marisco's earnings quality is therefore not in doubt.

## Questions

1. The accrual component of Marisco's 2025 net income is closest to: (LOS: los-05-01-e)
   - A. USD -5 million.
   - B. USD 53 million.
   - C. USD 111 million.
2. Marisco's 2025 days receivable (using a 365-day year) is closest to: (LOS: los-05-01-e)
   - A. 40.0 days.
   - B. 50.1 days.
   - C. 54.5 days.
3. The return on the equity portion of plan assets implied by Marisco's pension assumption is closest to: (LOS: los-05-01-e)
   - A. 7.5%.
   - B. 9.3%.
   - C. 13.6%.
4. Which of Marisco's 2025 accounting choices is least likely a warning sign of low earnings quality? (LOS: los-05-01-e)
   - A. Extending the depreciable life of store fixtures.
   - B. Reporting the distribution centre gain within operating income.
   - C. Expensing store pre-opening costs as incurred.
5. Measuring the gap as a percentage of the peer median P/E, the most accurate restatement of Pratt's valuation conclusion is that Marisco's shares are: (LOS: los-05-01-f)
   - A. 20% relatively undervalued against the peer median, which is not necessarily undervalued relative to intrinsic value.
   - B. 25% relatively undervalued against the peer median, which is not necessarily undervalued relative to intrinsic value.
   - C. 20% undervalued relative to intrinsic value, because the peer median is a market-based estimate of fair value.
6. The two uses of valuation described by Marisco's chief financial officer are best classified as: (LOS: los-05-01-d)
   - A. rendering a fairness opinion and communicating with shareholders.
   - B. evaluating a corporate event and valuing share-based payment.
   - C. inferring market expectations and appraising a private business.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **C.** Cash component $= CFO + CFI = 131 + (-58) = 73$.
> Accruals $= NI - (CFO + CFI) = 184 - 73 = 111$, or $111 / 184 = 0.6033$ of net income, a high accrual share that signals lower earnings quality.
> A is wrong because it adds the investing outflow instead of subtracting it ($184 - (131 + 58) = -5$).
> B is wrong because it uses operating cash flow alone as the cash component ($184 - 131 = 53$).
> 2. **B.** 2025 receivables turnover $= 1{,}610 / 221 = 7.2851$; days receivable $= 365 / 7.2851 = 50.1025$.
> For 2024, turnover $= 1{,}480 / 162 = 9.1358$ and days $= 365 / 9.1358 = 39.9527$, so collection has slowed by about 10 days.
> Receivables grew $221 / 162 - 1 = 36.4\%$ against revenue growth of $1{,}610 / 1{,}480 - 1 = 8.8\%$, which rebuts Pratt's claim: faster sales alone would leave days receivable unchanged.
> A is wrong because it is the 2024 figure (misread exhibit column).
> C is wrong because it pairs 2025 receivables with 2024 revenue ($365 \times 221 / 1{,}480 = 54.5034$).
> 3. **B.** $r_{\text{equity}} = (r_{\text{plan}} - w_{\text{bonds}} r_{\text{bonds}}) / (1 - w_{\text{bonds}}) = (0.075 - 0.35 \times 0.042) / 0.65 = (0.075 - 0.0147) / 0.65 = 0.0928$.
> An equity return of 9.3% is aggressive, and because expected return on plan assets reduces pension expense, the assumption flatters net income.
> A is wrong because it is the blended plan assumption, not the return the equity sleeve must earn.
> C is wrong because it swaps the weights, applying 65% to bonds and dividing by 35% ($(0.075 - 0.65 \times 0.042) / 0.35 = 0.1363$).
> 4. **C.** Expensing pre-opening costs as incurred is the conservative treatment; it lowers current earnings and removes a deferred-cost asset, so it is not a warning sign.
> A is wrong because long depreciable lives are an aggressive estimate that understates depreciation expense.
> B is wrong because classifying a nonoperating gain as operating income is a listed warning sign that makes nonrecurring income look sustainable.
> 5. **A.** Relative to the peer median, $14.0 / 17.5 - 1 = -0.20$, so Marisco is 20% relatively undervalued.
> The method of comparables cannot establish intrinsic value unless the benchmark is fairly valued, and the peer group trades at a premium to the broad market, so "relatively undervalued" must not be read as "undervalued."
> B is wrong because it computes the gap on the wrong base ($17.5 / 14.0 - 1 = 0.25$), the error Pratt made, although it frames the conclusion correctly.
> C is wrong because it treats the peer median as intrinsic value, which is exactly the assumption relative valuation cannot make without evidence.
> 6. **B.** Deciding whether and on what terms to issue shares for Hearthline is evaluating a corporate event (an acquisition paid in the acquirer's own stock); valuing restricted stock units is valuing share-based payment.
> A is wrong because a fairness opinion is a third party's judgment on merger terms, and nothing describes communication with shareholders.
> C is wrong because no implied fundamental is solved from the market price, and Hearthline is listed, not a private business.

## Distractor design

1. A: sign error on investing cash flow; B: operating cash flow alone used as the cash component.
2. A: prior-year column read (misread exhibit); C: current receivables matched with prior-year revenue (period mismatch).
3. A: blended plan rate taken as the equity return; C: bond and equity weights swapped.
4. A: aggressive estimate misread as benign; B: operating versus nonoperating classification misread.
5. B: percentage computed on the wrong base; C: relative undervaluation equated with intrinsic undervaluation.
6. A: fairness opinion confused with management's own analysis of a deal; C: wrong applications triggered by the word "valuation model."
