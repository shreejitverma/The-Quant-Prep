# Lindqvist MedTech: Valuing a Control Stake

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Ingrid Salo, CFA, is a vice president at Nordhavn Capital, a Stockholm private equity firm.
Nordhavn is considering the purchase of a 60% controlling stake in Lindqvist MedTech AB, a listed maker of surgical stapling and wound-closure devices.
Lindqvist has 120 million shares outstanding, and the shares trade at SEK 119.50.
The board has paid out about 20% of net income as dividends for a decade, even in years when free cash flow was far higher, and has accumulated a cash balance that Salo treats as required for operations.

Salo forecasts free cash flow to equity from sales.
She expects sales growth to fall steadily over the next three years as a product cycle matures, and then to settle at 5% a year indefinitely.
Net fixed capital investment (capital expenditures minus depreciation) and working capital investment are forecast as fixed fractions of each year's increase in sales.
Lindqvist's policy is to finance a fixed share of net new investment with debt, and depreciation is its only noncash charge.

Exhibit 1: Forecast assumptions for Lindqvist (SEK millions)

| Item | Assumption |
|---|---|
| Sales, 2025 (year 0) | 6,000 |
| Sales growth, years 1, 2, 3 | 18%, 14%, 10% |
| Sales growth, year 4 onward | 5% |
| Net profit margin, years 1, 2, 3 | 11.0%, 11.5%, 12.0% |
| Net profit margin, year 4 onward | 12.0% |
| Net fixed capital investment | 45% of the increase in sales |
| Working capital investment | 20% of the increase in sales |
| Debt financing (target debt ratio) | 35% of net fixed capital and working capital investment |

Exhibit 2: Market and capital data

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Risk-free rate | 3.0% |
| Lindqvist equity beta | 1.30 |
| Equity risk premium | 5.5% |
| Pretax cost of debt | 5.2% |
| Tax rate | 20.6% |
| Median trailing P/E, listed medical device peers | 16.0 |

Salo computes the required return on equity with the CAPM.
For a cross-check on the terminal value, she also estimates the value of the shares at the end of year 3 as the median peer P/E applied to Lindqvist's year 3 net income, keeping the explicit forecast for years 1 to 3 unchanged.

At the deal team meeting, Pär Engström, a Nordhavn partner, makes two comments.

Comment 1: "Year 4 FCFE is simply year 3 FCFE grown at 5%. So we could skip the explicit forecast and use a single-stage model on next year's FCFE and the long-term 5% rate."

Comment 2: "Our analyst's dividend discount model gives a far lower value than your FCFE model, even though both use the same required return. The FCFE model must be overstating value."

Salo replies that the dividend discount model answers a different question from the one Nordhavn is asking.
She explains that once Nordhavn controls the board, it will decide how Lindqvist's free cash flow is used, including whether to pay special dividends or to buy back shares.

The deal team agrees that if the stake is bought, Nordhavn will pay a control premium only if the free cash flow value clearly exceeds the market price.

## Questions

1. Lindqvist's forecast FCFE for year 1 is closest to: (LOS: los-05-03-e)
   - A. SEK 76.8 million.
   - B. SEK 322.5 million.
   - C. SEK 533.1 million.
2. Given Salo's growth expectations, the most appropriate free cash flow model for Lindqvist is a: (LOS: los-05-03-j)
   - A. single-stage model, because FCFE will grow at 5% in the long run, as Engström suggests.
   - B. two-stage model in which growth declines during the first stage.
   - C. three-stage model, because sales growth changes more than twice.
3. Using a two-stage FCFE model with a constant-growth terminal value, the value of a Lindqvist share is closest to: (LOS: los-05-03-k)
   - A. SEK 102.55.
   - B. SEK 112.80.
   - C. SEK 123.19.
4. Using Salo's P/E cross-check for the terminal value, the value of a Lindqvist share and the conclusion relative to the market price are closest to: (LOS: los-05-03-l)
   - A. SEK 82.73; overvalued.
   - B. SEK 116.75; overvalued.
   - C. SEK 122.06; undervalued.
5. Engström's Comment 2 is best described as: (LOS: los-05-03-g)
   - A. correct, because the FCFE and dividend discount models must give the same value when they use the same required return.
   - B. incorrect, because Lindqvist's dividends are set well below its capacity to pay, so the dividend model understates the value of the cash a controlling owner can extract.
   - C. incorrect, because dividend discount models always overstate the value of dividend-paying companies.
6. The ownership perspective that Salo's reply reflects is best described as: (LOS: los-05-03-b)
   - A. a control perspective, because a controlling owner has discretion over the uses of free cash flow, and FCFE measures what the company could pay out.
   - B. a minority perspective, because FCFE is smoother than dividends and therefore easier to forecast.
   - C. a control perspective, because FCFE, unlike dividends, is unaffected by the company's financing decisions.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Year 1 sales $= 6{,}000(1.18) = 7{,}080$, an increase of 1,080.
> Net income $= 0.11(7{,}080) = 778.8$; net FCInv $= 0.45(1{,}080) = 486.0$; WCInv $= 0.20(1{,}080) = 216.0$.
> $FCFE = NI - (1 - DR)(FCInv - Dep) - (1 - DR)WCInv = 778.8 - 0.65(486.0 + 216.0) = 778.8 - 456.3 = 322.5$.
> A is wrong because it ignores debt financing and deducts all of the investment: $778.8 - 702.0 = 76.8$.
> C is wrong because it deducts DR rather than $(1 - DR)$ of the investment: $778.8 - 0.35(702.0) = 533.1$.
> 2. **B.** Sales growth declines through years 1 to 3 (18%, 14%, 10%) and then settles at a sustainable 5%, which is the two-stage pattern in which growth declines during the first stage.
> A is wrong because FCFE does not grow at 5% until year 5; FCFE rises from 724.39 in year 3 to 931.11 in year 4, a 28.54% jump, because investment needs fall when sales growth slows.
> C is wrong because a three-stage model is used for a distinct high-growth phase followed by a transition phase and then maturity; counting the number of different growth rates does not by itself justify it, and here growth falls continuously to the stable rate.
> 3. **C.** $r = 3.0\% + 1.30(5.5\%) = 10.15\%$.
> Year 2: sales 8,071.20 (increase 991.20), NI 928.188, equity-financed investment $0.65(446.04 + 198.24) = 418.782$, FCFE 509.406.
> Year 3: sales 8,878.32 (increase 807.12), NI 1,065.3984, equity-financed investment $0.65(363.204 + 161.424) = 341.0082$, FCFE 724.3902.
> Year 4: sales 9,322.236 (increase 443.916), NI 1,118.6683, equity-financed investment $0.65(0.65 \times 443.916) = 187.5545$, FCFE 931.1138; from year 5 FCFE grows at exactly 5%.
> PV of years 1 to 3 $= 322.5/1.1015 + 509.406/1.1015^2 + 724.3902/1.1015^3 = 292.7826 + 419.8509 + 542.0247 = 1{,}254.6581$.
> $TV_3 = FCFE_4/(r - g) = 931.1138/(0.1015 - 0.05) = 18{,}079.8796$; PV $= 18{,}079.8796/1.1015^3 = 13{,}528.2629$.
> Equity value $= 1{,}254.6581 + 13{,}528.2629 = 14{,}782.9210$; per share $= 14{,}782.9210/120 = 123.19$.
> A is wrong because it builds the terminal value from year 3 FCFE grown at 5% ($724.3902 \times 1.05/0.0515 = 14{,}769.1206$), ignoring the drop in investment when growth slows; value $= (1{,}254.6581 + 14{,}769.1206/1.1015^3)/120 = 102.55$.
> B is wrong because it discounts the terminal value four periods instead of three: $(1{,}254.6581 + 18{,}079.8796/1.1015^4)/120 = (1{,}254.6581 + 12{,}281.6731)/120 = 112.80$.
> 4. **B.** $TV_3 = 16.0 \times NI_3 = 16.0 \times 1{,}065.3984 = 17{,}046.3744$; PV $= 17{,}046.3744/1.1015^3 = 12{,}754.9429$.
> Equity value $= 1{,}254.6581 + 12{,}754.9429 = 14{,}009.6010$; per share $= 116.75$.
> The P/E-based value is below the SEK 119.50 price, so on this cross-check the shares are overvalued, even though the constant-growth terminal value gives 123.19.
> A is wrong because it applies the multiple to year 3 FCFE instead of earnings: $16.0 \times 724.3902 = 11{,}590.2432$, giving 82.73.
> C is wrong because it grows terminal earnings one year before applying a trailing multiple: $16.0 \times 1{,}065.3984 \times 1.05 = 17{,}898.6931$, giving 122.06 and the wrong conclusion.
> 5. **B.** The two models give the same value only when FCFE equals dividends or when all inputs are mutually consistent.
> Lindqvist pays about 20% of income while its capacity to pay (FCFE) is much higher, which is exactly the case in which free cash flow models are preferred and a dividend model gives a lower value.
> A is wrong because a common required return does not make the cash flows equal.
> C is wrong because it reverses the result and overgeneralizes; here the dividend model gives the lower value.
> 6. **A.** FCFE is the amount the company could afford to pay as dividends, and with control comes discretion over how free cash flow is used, so FCFE is the cash flow relevant to a controlling owner.
> B is wrong because dividends, not FCFE, are the smoother series, and the reply concerns control, not a minority position.
> C is wrong because FCFE does depend on financing decisions; net borrowing raises FCFE in the year of issue and after-tax interest lowers it later.

## Distractor design

1. A: debt financing ignored (all investment equity-funded); C: DR used in place of $(1 - DR)$ (wrong formula variant).
2. A: long-run FCFE growth assumed from year 1 (misread exhibit); C: model chosen by counting growth rates rather than by phase structure (wrong model variant).
3. A: terminal cash flow set to $FCFE_3(1 + g)$ instead of the forecast $FCFE_4$ (period error); B: terminal value discounted $n + 1$ periods (period error).
4. A: multiple applied to FCFE instead of earnings (wrong base); C: terminal earnings grown before a trailing multiple (period error), which also flips the conclusion.
5. A: equal discount rate mistaken for equal value (wrong condition for equivalence); C: direction of the dividend-model bias reversed (sign).
6. B: smoothing property assigned to the wrong series (FCFE vs dividends); C: FCFE treated as independent of leverage (financing effect ignored).
