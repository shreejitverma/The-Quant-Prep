# Duarte Ceramica: Three Engagements for a Family Company

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Rui Carvalho, CFA, leads the valuation practice of an independent advisory firm in Porto.
His client is Duarte Ceramica SA, a privately held Portuguese producer of floor and wall tiles owned equally by two cousins, Beatriz and Nuno Duarte.
Duarte's shares have never traded, it pays dividends irregularly when the cousins agree, and it has generated positive and stable free cash flow for a decade.
Its production assets are specialised kilns and glazing lines that have little use outside tile manufacturing.

Carvalho's firm has been retained for three separate engagements.

- Engagement 1: set the price at which either cousin must buy the other's shares under their shareholders' buy-sell agreement.
- Engagement 2: estimate the value of a tile brand that Duarte acquired in 2022, as required for Duarte's annual impairment test under IFRS.
- Engagement 3: advise Duarte on an unsolicited proposal from Solara Group, a listed Spanish tile maker, to buy 100% of Duarte.

For Engagement 3, Carvalho first forecasts Duarte's 2027 revenue.
He follows his firm's top-down policy and uses the data in Exhibit 1.

Exhibit 1: Revenue forecast inputs

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Iberian tile industry volume, 2026 | 138.0 million square metres |
| Forecast growth in real construction output, 2027 | 2.8% |
| Industry volume growth assumption | Equal to real construction output growth |
| Duarte market share, 2026 | 6.5% |
| Duarte market share, 2027 forecast | 7.0% |
| Duarte average selling price, 2026 | EUR 14.20 per square metre |
| Forecast selling price increase, 2027 | 2.0% |
| Duarte kiln capacity, 2026 | 11.5 million square metres |

Solara will pay cash and, if successful, will integrate Duarte's plants into its own distribution system and set Duarte's dividend and investment policy.
Solara's proposal implies a price-to-earnings multiple of 12.6 on Duarte's 2026 earnings.
Carvalho's two associates reach different conclusions about the proposal.
Associate Inês Costa compares 12.6 with the median P/E of 14.0 for listed European tile producers.
Associate Paulo Reis compares it with the median P/E of 11.2 for European small-capitalisation industrial companies.

Beatriz Duarte also asks Carvalho to evaluate the company's sanitaryware plant, which has lost money for four years.
Carvalho estimates the plant's value as a continuing operation at EUR 11.4 million, the present value of its forecast free cash flow.
Its equipment and site would raise EUR 9.0 million if sold immediately or EUR 14.8 million if sold over 18 months.
Closing the plant would incur severance and environmental remediation costs of EUR 2.6 million under either schedule.

Finally, Nuno Duarte asks two further questions.
He wants to know whether Carvalho's firm could, if the cousins agree to negotiate with Solara, provide a written opinion to Duarte's board on whether Solara's final terms are fair to the shareholders.
He also asks what the firm would be doing, in terms of the recognised uses of valuation, in Engagement 1.

## Questions

1. The definitions of value most relevant to Engagements 1, 2 and 3 (from Duarte's perspective in assessing what Solara could pay), respectively, are: (LOS: los-05-01-c)
   - A. fair market value, fair value, and investment value.
   - B. fair value, fair market value, and investment value.
   - C. fair market value, fair value, and going-concern value.
2. Carvalho's top-down forecast of Duarte's 2027 revenue is closest to: (LOS: los-05-01-e)
   - A. EUR 133.6 million.
   - B. EUR 141.0 million.
   - C. EUR 143.8 million.
3. For Engagement 3, the most appropriate absolute valuation model for estimating what Duarte is worth to a buyer such as Solara is: (LOS: los-05-01-h)
   - A. an asset-based model.
   - B. a free cash flow model.
   - C. a dividend discount model.
4. Which statement about Costa's and Reis's analyses is most accurate? (LOS: los-05-01-f)
   - A. The proposal is 10.0% below the peer benchmark and 12.5% above the small-cap benchmark, and both relative conclusions can be correct.
   - B. The proposal is 11.1% below the peer benchmark and 11.1% above the small-cap benchmark, and both relative conclusions can be correct.
   - C. The proposal is 10.0% below the peer benchmark and 12.5% above the small-cap benchmark, but only one of these conclusions can be correct.
5. The written opinion Nuno describes and the work in Engagement 1 are best classified, respectively, as: (LOS: los-05-01-d)
   - A. a fairness opinion and appraisal of a private business.
   - B. evaluation of a corporate event and share-based payment valuation.
   - C. a fairness opinion and inferring market expectations.
6. Based on Carvalho's estimates, the value-maximising decision for the sanitaryware plant, and the gain over continuing to operate it, is: (LOS: los-05-01-b)
   - A. continue to operate it, because its value as a going concern exceeds its liquidation value.
   - B. close it and sell the assets over 18 months, a gain of EUR 0.8 million.
   - C. close it and sell the assets over 18 months, a gain of EUR 3.4 million.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **A.** A buy-sell agreement between private owners seeks equitable treatment, so fair market value (willing buyer, willing seller, neither under compulsion) applies.
> An IFRS impairment test uses the accounting standard's fair value.
> What Solara could pay reflects Solara's own integration benefits and requirements, which is investment value.
> B is wrong because it swaps the transaction concept (fair market value) and the financial-reporting concept (fair value).
> C is wrong because going-concern value, although it describes Duarte's operating value, ignores the synergies specific to Solara that set the maximum price Solara could pay.
> 2. **C.** Industry volume 2027 $= 138.0 \times 1.028 = 141.8640$ million square metres.
> Duarte volume $= 141.8640 \times 0.070 = 9.9305$ million square metres.
> Price $= 14.20 \times 1.02 = 14.4840$.
> Revenue $= 9.9305 \times 14.4840 = 143.8331$, or EUR 143.8 million.
> A is wrong because it applies the 2026 market share of 6.5% ($141.8640 \times 0.065 \times 14.4840 = 133.5593$).
> B is wrong because it omits the 2.0% price increase ($9.9305 \times 14.20 = 141.0128$).
> Kiln capacity of 11.5 million square metres exceeds forecast volume of 9.93 million and does not constrain the forecast; building revenue up from capacity would be a bottom-up method, not the firm's policy.
> 3. **B.** A free cash flow model fits all three criteria: Duarte's cash flows are positive and stable (data availability), the value lies in operating the specialised plant as a going concern (company characteristics), and a controlling buyer that will set dividend and investment policy can redirect free cash flow (purpose and perspective).
> A is wrong because specialised kilns with little alternative use make an asset-based model a poor measure of an operating business's value.
> C is wrong because irregular dividends set by the cousins' agreement fail the data criterion and do not reflect what a controlling owner receives.
> 4. **A.** Against peers: $12.6 / 14.0 - 1 = -0.100$, so the price is 10.0% below the peer benchmark (Duarte would be sold cheaply relative to peers).
> Against small caps: $12.6 / 11.2 - 1 = 0.125$, so the price is 12.5% above that benchmark.
> Both are relative statements against different benchmarks and can be simultaneously true if listed tile producers trade at a premium to small-cap industrials.
> B is wrong because it uses the benchmark as the base ($14.0 / 12.6 - 1 = 0.1111$ and $11.2 / 12.6 - 1 = -0.1111$) rather than the subject's multiple.
> C is wrong because two relative valuations with different benchmarks do not contradict each other; neither establishes intrinsic value unless its benchmark is fairly valued.
> 5. **A.** A third party's opinion to a board on whether merger terms are fair is a fairness opinion.
> Setting the buy-sell price for unlisted shares is appraisal of a private business, a transaction-related valuation with no market price available.
> B is wrong because Engagement 1 involves no compensation award, and the written opinion is a formal fairness opinion rather than management's own event analysis.
> C is wrong because Duarte has no market price from which expectations could be inferred.
> 6. **B.** Orderly liquidation value net of closure costs $= 14.8 - 2.6 = 12.2$, which exceeds the going-concern value of 11.4 by $12.2 - 11.4 = 0.8$.
> The persistently unprofitable plant is worth more dead than alive when its assets are sold over a longer period.
> A is wrong because it compares going-concern value with the forced (immediate) liquidation value net of costs ($9.0 - 2.6 = 6.4$) and ignores the orderly liquidation alternative.
> C is wrong because it ignores closure costs ($14.8 - 11.4 = 3.4$).

## Distractor design

1. B: fair value and fair market value swapped (reporting versus transaction concept); C: going-concern value substituted for a specific buyer's investment value.
2. A: prior-period market share used (period error); B: price growth omitted (partial forecast).
3. A: asset-based model chosen despite specialised operating assets; C: DDM chosen despite irregular dividends and a control buyer.
4. B: percentage computed on the benchmark as base; C: relative conclusions against different benchmarks treated as mutually exclusive.
5. B: corporate event and share-based payment confused with a fairness opinion and a private appraisal; C: inferring market expectations assumed possible without a market price.
6. A: forced rather than orderly liquidation value used; C: closure costs omitted (gross versus net liquidation value).
