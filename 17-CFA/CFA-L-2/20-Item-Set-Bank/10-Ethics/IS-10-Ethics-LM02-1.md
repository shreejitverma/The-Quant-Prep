# Norrland Asset Management: The Solvik Robotics Allocation

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Karin Ekdahl, CFA, is a senior portfolio manager at Norrland Asset Management, a discretionary manager of separately managed accounts for institutions and wealthy families.
Norrland's written trade allocation policy, disclosed to all clients, states that new issues are allocated to suitable accounts in proportion to each account's indication of interest, rounded to the nearest 100-share lot.
Portfolio managers enter indications by account before an offering is priced.

In March 2026, Norrland entered indications for the initial public offering of Solvik Robotics at an expected price of SEK 142 per share.
The offering was heavily oversubscribed, and Norrland received 18,000 shares against total indications of 45,000 shares.

Exhibit 1: Indications of interest in the Solvik Robotics IPO

| Account | Market value (SEK m) | Indication (shares) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ekholm Foundation | 310 | 12,000 | Growth mandate; IPOs permitted |
| Lind Family Trust | 22 | 6,000 | Fee-paying account of Ekdahl's sister; run on the same growth model as other accounts |
| Vasa Municipal Pension Plan | 480 | 15,000 | Growth mandate; IPOs permitted |
| Strand Mutual Insurance | 260 | 9,000 | IPS limits equities to dividend payers listed for at least five years |
| K. Ekdahl personal account | 1.4 | 3,000 | Ekdahl is the beneficial owner |

The Strand indication was entered by a junior trader, Olle Berg, who explained: "Strand is a big account, and leaving it out of a hot deal would not be fair to them."
Ekdahl's draft allocation sheet contains two further choices.
First, she excludes the Lind Family Trust entirely, telling Berg: "I do not want anyone to say I favoured my sister."
Second, she fills her personal account at the same percentage as the client accounts, reasoning that "my order is the smallest on the sheet and I am paying the same price as everyone else."

Solvik listed on 24 March 2026 and rose 31% on its first day.
On 2 April, Norrland's analyst Petra Holm changed her Solvik rating from buy to sell after concluding that the valuation was stretched.
The change was scheduled for release to all clients through Norrland's research portal at 14:00.
At 11:30, Ekdahl telephoned the chief investment officer of the Ekholm Foundation, Norrland's largest client, to "give him a head start" on the change.
At 16:15, after the release, the treasurer of the Vasa plan called Ekdahl with an unsolicited order to buy 2,000 additional Solvik shares for the plan's self-directed sleeve, which Norrland executes on instruction.
The treasurer said that he had not opened the research portal that day and was unaware of any change to Norrland's view.
Ekdahl entered the order immediately and without comment.

Norrland's compliance officer, Mats Lund, CFA, reviews the Solvik file the following week.
He drafts three possible amendments to the allocation policy for the management committee.

Proposal 1: New issues will be allocated to each portfolio manager in proportion to the assets he or she manages, and each manager will then allocate among suitable accounts.

Proposal 2: Indications of interest will be recorded and time-stamped by client account before the offering, and partial fills will be prorated by indication size, subject to a minimum lot.

Proposal 3: Clients will be asked to sign a consent that allows portfolio managers discretion to favour long-standing clients in oversubscribed offerings.

Lund must also decide how Norrland should treat Ekdahl's personal indication and future employee requests to participate in new issues.

## Questions

1. Applying Norrland's allocation policy consistently with the Code and Standards, the number of Solvik shares allocated to the Vasa Municipal Pension Plan is closest to: (LOS: los-10-02-a)
   - A. 6,400.
   - B. 8,200.
   - C. 10,000.
2. Ekdahl's decision to exclude the Lind Family Trust from the allocation is most likely: (LOS: los-10-02-a)
   - A. required, because accounts of immediate family members must wait until all other clients are filled.
   - B. a violation, because a fee-paying family account managed like other client accounts must not be disadvantaged.
   - C. acceptable, provided Ekdahl discloses the exclusion to her sister.
3. Including the Strand Mutual Insurance account in the allocation, as Berg proposes, would most likely violate the Standard relating to: (LOS: los-10-02-a)
   - A. fair dealing.
   - B. suitability.
   - C. priority of transactions.
4. The most appropriate response by Norrland to Ekdahl's personal indication, and to future employee requests to participate in new issues, is to: (LOS: los-10-02-b)
   - A. approve her fill at the client percentage because she pays the offering price, and require that the purchase appear in her annual holdings report.
   - B. cancel her allocation, reallocate the shares to eligible client accounts, and require prior approval of any employee participation in IPOs.
   - C. approve her fill if she agrees to hold the shares for at least 90 days, so that she cannot profit from flipping them.
5. Which of Ekdahl's actions on 2 April is inconsistent with the Standard relating to fair dealing? (LOS: los-10-02-a)
   - A. Only the 11:30 call to the Ekholm Foundation.
   - B. Only entering the Vasa order without comment.
   - C. Both the 11:30 call and entering the Vasa order without comment.
6. Which of Lund's proposals is most consistent with the recommended procedures for fair dealing? (LOS: los-10-02-b)
   - A. Proposal 1.
   - B. Proposal 2.
   - C. Proposal 3.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Only suitable client accounts share in the allotment: Strand is excluded because an IPO fails its IPS (Standard III(C)), and Ekdahl's own account is excluded because members forgo participation in an oversubscribed issue while clients are unfilled (Standards VI(B) and III(B)).
> The eligible indications are $12{,}000 + 6{,}000 + 15{,}000 = 33{,}000$ shares.
> Vasa receives $18{,}000 \times 15{,}000 / 33{,}000 = 8{,}181.8182$, which rounds to 8,200 shares.
> Check: Ekholm $6{,}545.4545 \to 6{,}500$, Lind $3{,}272.7273 \to 3{,}300$, Vasa 8,200; total 18,000.
> A is wrong because it keeps Strand in the pool: $18{,}000 \times 15{,}000 / 42{,}000 = 6{,}428.5714 \to 6{,}400$.
> C is wrong because it drops the Lind Family Trust: $18{,}000 \times 15{,}000 / 27{,}000 = 10{,}000$.
> The market values in Exhibit 1 are a decoy; the policy prorates by indication, not by account size.
> 2. **B.** A fee-paying family account that is managed like any other client account is a client account and must be treated like one; excluding it "to avoid appearances" disadvantages a client (Standard III(A), with the family-account guidance under VI(B)).
> A is wrong because the priority rule makes accounts in which the member is beneficial owner wait; Ekdahl has no beneficial interest in her sister's trust, and the trust pays fees like other clients.
> C is wrong because disclosure does not cure disadvantaging a client in the allocation.
> 3. **B.** Strand's IPS limits equities to dividend payers listed for at least five years, so an IPO is inconsistent with its written constraints; allocating to it would violate Standard III(C) Suitability.
> A is wrong because fair dealing governs the distribution process among suitable clients; Berg's "fairness" argument misapplies III(B) to an account for which the issue is unsuitable.
> C is wrong because priority of transactions concerns accounts in which a member is beneficial owner, not client-versus-client allocation.
> 4. **B.** Members must forgo their own participation in an oversubscribed issue, so the shares go back to eligible clients, and the recommended procedure is to limit or require preclearance of employee IPO participation (Standard VI(B) procedures; I(B) also bars personal allocation of oversubscribed IPO shares to managers).
> A is wrong because paying the same price does not remove the harm: every share she takes is a share an unfilled client does not receive; after-the-fact holdings reporting does not prevent the conflict.
> C is wrong because a holding period does nothing about the priority conflict at allocation.
> 5. **C.** The 11:30 call gave the largest client an early look at a material rating change before other clients had a fair opportunity to act, violating Standard III(B).
> The Vasa order was placed contrary to the firm's current recommendation by a treasurer who was unaware of the change, and III(B) requires the member to advise such a client of the change before accepting the order; after that, the client's instruction controls.
> A is wrong because it treats executing an unsolicited order as a pure mechanical act; B is wrong because it ignores the selective early release.
> 6. **B.** Time-stamped indications recorded by client before the offering, and pro rata partial fills by order size subject to minimum lots, are the documented trade allocation procedures the Standards recommend.
> A is wrong because allocating by portfolio manager rather than by client means some suitable clients will not receive their pro rata share.
> C is wrong because client consent cannot bless a patently unfair allocation procedure.

## Distractor design

1. A: unsuitable account left in the pro rata pool (suitability screen skipped); C: fee-paying family account wrongly excluded from the pool.
2. A: priority of transactions misapplied to a family account in which the member has no beneficial interest; C: disclosure treated as a cure for disadvantaging a client.
3. A: fair dealing confused with suitability (the nearest-neighbour trap); C: priority of transactions applied to a client-versus-client question.
4. A: same price treated as no conflict, and after-the-fact reporting treated as prevention; C: a holding period substituted for forgoing participation.
5. A: duty to warn a client ordering against the current recommendation overlooked; B: selective early release overlooked.
6. A: allocation by manager rather than by client; C: consent treated as a cure for unfair allocation.
