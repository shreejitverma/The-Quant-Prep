# Tidewater Asset Management: IPO Allocation, Cash, and a CFO's Hint

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Tidewater Asset Management, based in Singapore, manages the Tidewater Asia Growth Fund (TAGF), a public equity fund, and separately managed accounts.
In January, Farid Osei, CFA, joined as head of Asian equities from Harbourline Capital, where he had been one of four members of a team managing an Asian equity fund.
Osei supervises analyst Mei Wong, CFA.

In February, Tidewater orders shares in the oversubscribed initial public offering of Selaras Logistics for the three client accounts for which the issue is suitable and for Tidewater's seed account, which holds only the firm's own capital.
Tidewater is allocated 150,000 shares.
Head trader Lily Tan allocates the shares pro rata by order size across all four orders.

Exhibit 1: Selaras Logistics IPO orders

| Account | Assets (USD millions) | Shares ordered |
|---|---|---|
| TAGF | 600 | 120,000 |
| Pensiun Mandiri pension plan | 450 | 80,000 |
| Chen family trust | 150 | 50,000 |
| Tidewater seed account | 40 | 40,000 |

The TAGF prospectus states that cash and equivalents will not exceed 10% of net assets, that the fund will hold 40 to 60 stocks, and that all investment decisions are made by a three-member investment committee.
At the end of March, TAGF has total assets of USD 675 million and liabilities of USD 75 million for unsettled purchases and a bank credit line.
In March, Osei makes three changes, none of which he discloses.

- Change 1: Expecting a market correction, he raises TAGF's cash to USD 66 million.
- Change 2: He increases the number of holdings from 44 to 57.
- Change 3: He makes all buy and sell decisions himself, and the committee reviews the portfolio quarterly after the fact.

Osei explains: "Cash is 9.8% of fund assets, the holdings are within range, and the committee still meets, so clients have nothing new to hear."

Tidewater's new TAGF brochure shows a table headed "Portfolio Manager Track Record: Farid Osei" with a five-year annualized return of 14.2% net of fees against 9.6% for the benchmark.
The figures are copied accurately from Harbourline's public fund fact sheets.
The brochure does not mention Harbourline or the team.

In April, Osei dines with Aziz Rahman, CFO of Pantai Resorts.
Rahman tells him that Pantai's board will consider next week an offer from a Japanese hotel group for Pantai's Bali unit at "around USD 300 million."
The next morning, Osei instructs Wong to value the Bali unit at USD 300 million in her sum-of-the-parts model, without telling her why.
Wong's revised model moves Pantai to a Buy, and Osei buys Pantai for TAGF that afternoon.
Two weeks later, Wong learns the source of the USD 300 million figure and tells Osei the purchase was improper.
Osei tells her to keep the matter to herself because "the board may still reject the offer."

## Questions

1. Under the Code and Standards, the number of Selaras shares that should be allocated to the Pensiun Mandiri account is closest to: (LOS: los-10-03-a)
   - A. 41,379.
   - B. 48,000.
   - C. 56,250.
2. Which of Osei's changes most likely require disclosure to TAGF's current and prospective clients under Standard V(B) Communication with Clients and Prospective Clients? (LOS: los-10-03-b)
   - A. Change 3 only
   - B. Changes 1, 2, and 3
   - C. Changes 1 and 3
3. With respect to Standard III(D) Performance Presentation, the brochure's track record table most likely: (LOS: los-10-03-b)
   - A. complies, because the returns are copied accurately from Harbourline's public fact sheets.
   - B. violates the Standard, because it omits that the returns were earned at Harbourline and that Osei was one of four team members.
   - C. violates the Standard, because performance earned at a prior employer may never be presented.
4. With respect to the Pantai purchase, who most likely violated Standard II(A) Material Nonpublic Information? (LOS: los-10-03-a)
   - A. Osei only
   - B. Wong only
   - C. Neither, because the purchase followed the Buy output of Wong's model
5. After Osei tells her to keep the matter to herself, Wong's most appropriate next step is to: (LOS: los-10-03-b)
   - A. resign from Tidewater.
   - B. contact TAGF's shareholders directly.
   - C. report the matter to Tidewater's compliance department or senior management.
6. Which firm policy would most likely have prevented the problem in Tan's Selaras allocation? (LOS: los-10-03-b)
   - A. Rotating IPO allocations among client accounts in order of assets under management
   - B. Written procedures that allocate partial fills pro rata by order size among participating client accounts before any firm or employee account receives shares
   - C. Allocating oversubscribed IPOs only to the largest client accounts so that minimum lot sizes are met

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Clients' interests come before the employer's under Standard III(A), so the seed account, which holds only Tidewater's capital, should receive no shares until client orders are filled; among clients, Standard III(B) calls for pro rata allocation by order size (V10 p.264, p.265).
> Client orders $= 120{,}000 + 80{,}000 + 50{,}000 = 250{,}000$.
> Fill ratio $= 150{,}000 / 250{,}000 = 0.6000$.
> Pensiun Mandiri $= 0.6000 \times 80{,}000 = 48{,}000$ shares (TAGF 72,000; Chen trust 30,000).
> A is wrong because it is Tan's method, which includes the seed account: $150{,}000 / 290{,}000 = 0.5172$ and $0.5172 \times 80{,}000 = 41{,}379$, diverting 20,690 shares from clients to the firm.
> C is wrong because it allocates by account assets rather than order size: $150{,}000 \times 450/1{,}200 = 56{,}250$.
> 2. **C.** The cash limit is stated as a percentage of net assets.
> Net assets $= 675 - 75 = 600$ (USD millions), so cash $= 66/600 = 0.1100$, or 11.0%, which breaches the 10% prospectus limit and is a material change to the mandate (V10 p.260).
> Moving from a three-member committee to a single decision maker is a material change to the investment process.
> Both must be disclosed promptly to current and prospective clients under V(B); growing from 44 to 57 holdings stays within the stated 40 to 60 range and needs no disclosure.
> A is wrong because it uses total assets as the denominator, $66/675 = 0.0978$, or 9.78%, which is Osei's "9.8% of fund assets" and hides the breach.
> B is wrong because it treats a change inside the stated mandate as material.
> 3. **B.** III(D) permits a manager to show prior-firm performance only with disclosure of where it was earned and the manager's specific role; the table names neither Harbourline nor the four-person team, and its heading credits Osei alone (V10 p.260, p.261).
> A is wrong because accurate numbers do not cure a misleading attribution; the 4.6 percentage points of excess return were a team result at another firm.
> C is wrong because prior-firm performance may be presented when the venue and role are disclosed.
> 4. **A.** A CFO's statement that the board will consider a specific offer at about USD 300 million is material by source, substance, and specificity even though the board has not voted, and Osei acted on it for TAGF (V10 p.261).
> Wong did not know the figure came from an insider when she used it, so she did not knowingly act on material nonpublic information.
> B is wrong because it blames the analyst whose model mechanically carried the tip, while the person who possessed and acted on the information is Osei.
> C is wrong because a model built on material nonpublic information does not launder it; the Buy output was contaminated by the tip.
> 5. **C.** Having raised the matter with her supervisor without result, Wong should escalate to compliance or senior management; stepping away comes only if that fails (V10 p.246).
> A is wrong because resignation is the extreme end of dissociation, not the next step.
> B is wrong because contacting clients directly is not a prescribed intermediate step.
> 6. **B.** Written allocation procedures that fill client orders pro rata by order size, with firm and employee accounts participating only after clients, would have produced the 48,000-share allocation and kept the seed account's 20,690 shares with clients (V10 p.264, p.265).
> A is wrong because allocating by assets under management disadvantages smaller clients whose orders are equally suitable.
> C is wrong because excluding smaller suitable clients from a scarce asset is itself a III(B) violation.

## Distractor design

1. A: firm account included in the pro rata pool; C: allocation weighted by account assets instead of order size.
2. A: cash measured against total assets instead of net assets (the character's own figure); B: within-mandate change treated as material.
3. A: numerical accuracy confused with complete disclosure; C: disclosable practice treated as prohibited.
4. B: responsibility assigned to the unknowing intermediary; C: model output treated as laundering the tip.
5. A: extreme dissociation chosen as the next step; B: client contact used as an escalation step.
6. A: allocation by assets under management; C: largest-client allocation that repeats the III(B) failure.
