# Linden Ridge Wealth: Carbon Credits for Every Client

> [!info] Timer
> 12 minutes for 4 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Rafael Ortega, CFA, is an adviser at Linden Ridge Wealth, the wealth management division of Linden Ridge Bank.
The bank also runs a carbon credit brokerage desk that earns commissions arranging trades in voluntary carbon credits for corporate buyers and sellers.
Ortega owns 12 hectares of forest that generates certified carbon credits.
He sells the credits through an independent online exchange and earns about USD 18,000 a year.
He has not told Linden Ridge about the forest or the income.

In May, Ortega spends three evenings reading a fund sponsor's webinar slides and online forums about carbon credits.
He concludes that units of the Verdant Forest Carbon Trust (VFCT), a thinly traded listed trust that holds forestry carbon credits, will double within two years.
He does not read the trust's offering document or the third-party verification reports on the forests behind its credits.
VFCT's average daily trading volume is about USD 90,000, and its annual expense ratio is 1.35%.
In June, he recommends that each of his 64 clients invest 4% of the investment portfolio in VFCT, explaining to his supervisor, Grace Pham, CFA, that "a single model position keeps things simple."
Pham approves the model position without reviewing any individual client account.
Linden Ridge's policy classifies VFCT as an illiquid asset.

Ortega already owns 25,000 VFCT units, bought before his research and worth about USD 355,000 at the June price of USD 14.20.
He did not mention the holding when he made the recommendation.
In July review meetings, he tells clients who ask about VFCT, "I own some too."

Because VFCT trades thinly, Ortega offers his five largest clients the chance to buy units directly from his personal holding at the previous day's closing price, "to save them the spread."

One of Ortega's clients is Ines Ruiz, a retired teacher who draws USD 54,000 a year from her portfolio for living expenses.
She describes herself as a cautious investor and has never held a listed trust.

Exhibit 1: Selected data, Ines Ruiz

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Investment portfolio value | USD 1,200,000 |
| Existing illiquid holding (private real estate fund) | USD 18,000 |
| Total net worth, including residence | USD 2,000,000 |
| IPS limit on illiquid assets | 5% of investment portfolio |
| IPS return objective | 4% real per year |

The VFCT purchase would be funded by selling listed bonds in her portfolio, leaving the portfolio value unchanged.
When Pham asks about Ruiz, Ortega replies: "Her 4% position is below her 5% illiquid limit, and she signed our standard risk acknowledgment."

## Questions

1. After the recommended VFCT purchase, the illiquid-asset weight in Ruiz's investment portfolio and the most accurate conclusion are: (LOS: los-10-03-b)
   - A. 5.5%, which exceeds her IPS limit, so the recommendation is unsuitable for her under Standard III(C).
   - B. 3.3%, which is within her IPS limit, so the recommendation is suitable for her.
   - C. 4.0%, which is within her IPS limit, so the recommendation is suitable for her.
2. Ortega's sale of carbon credits from his forest most likely: (LOS: los-10-03-a)
   - A. violates Standard VI(A) only, because he must disclose the income to his clients.
   - B. does not violate the Code and Standards, because the income comes from his own property rather than from clients.
   - C. violates Standard IV(B) Additional Compensation Arrangements, because the income competes with his employer's business and he lacks Linden Ridge's written consent.
3. With respect to his offer to sell VFCT units from his personal holding, Ortega's most appropriate action is to: (LOS: los-10-03-b)
   - A. proceed, because the previous day's close is a fair price and the clients save the spread.
   - B. withdraw the offer, because selling his own units only to his largest clients violates Standard III(B), and selling personal holdings to clients is a conflict he should avoid outright.
   - C. extend the same offer to all 64 clients at the previous day's close, which fully satisfies the Code and Standards.
4. Ortega's disclosure of his VFCT holding is most likely: (LOS: los-10-03-b)
   - A. sufficient, because he disclosed the holding to every client who asked about it.
   - B. unnecessary, because he bought the units before he began his research.
   - C. insufficient, because Standard VI(A) requires prominent, plain-language disclosure to all clients before they rely on the recommendation.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **A.** The recommended VFCT position is $0.04 \times 1{,}200{,}000 = 48{,}000$.
> Total illiquid holdings after purchase $= 18{,}000 + 48{,}000 = 66{,}000$.
> Illiquid weight $= 66{,}000 / 1{,}200{,}000 = 0.0550$, or 5.5%, above the 5% IPS limit; the existing holding is already $18{,}000 / 1{,}200{,}000 = 0.0150$, or 1.5%.
> Recommending a uniform position to every client without checking each client's objectives and constraints violates Standard III(C) Suitability, and for Ruiz it breaches a written constraint (V10 p.265).
> A signed risk acknowledgment does not make an unsuitable position suitable.
> B is wrong because it divides by total net worth including her residence, $66{,}000 / 2{,}000{,}000 = 0.0330$, or 3.3%, while the IPS limit applies to the investment portfolio.
> C is wrong because it looks at the new position alone, 4.0%, and ignores the 1.5% already in the private real estate fund, which is Ortega's own error.
> 2. **C.** Selling carbon credits through an outside exchange competes with the bank's carbon brokerage business, so Standard IV(B) requires Linden Ridge's written consent before Ortega accepts the income (V10 p.266).
> A is wrong because it names the wrong standard and the wrong audience; the conflict is with the employer, and disclosing to clients would not supply the employer's consent.
> B is wrong because the source of the income does not matter; what matters is that it competes with his employer's interest.
> 3. **B.** Offering a limited, thinly traded asset only to the largest clients disadvantages every other client for whom it is suitable, violating III(B), and selling from his own holding means client purchases directly benefit him, a conflict that VI(A) requires be avoided or disclosed prominently beforehand (V10 p.264, p.265, p.267).
> Selling personal holdings to clients is a transaction he should avoid altogether.
> A is wrong because a fair price does not cure the selective offer or the undisclosed conflict.
> C is wrong because offering to all 64 clients cures only the selectivity; the conflict remains, and a uniform offer ignores suitability, as Ruiz's account shows.
> 4. **C.** A personal holding of about USD 355,000 in a thinly traded trust he recommends to all his clients could reasonably impair his objectivity, and VI(A) requires full, prominent, plain-language disclosure before clients act, not a late remark to those who happen to ask (V10 p.267).
> A is wrong because disclosure that is late, partial, and only given when a client asks does not meet the Standard.
> B is wrong because the conflict arises from owning the asset while recommending it, whenever the units were bought.

## Distractor design

1. B: wrong denominator (total net worth including residence instead of investment portfolio); C: existing illiquid holding omitted (the character's own error).
2. A: wrong standard and wrong audience (client disclosure instead of employer written consent); B: income judged by its source rather than by its conflict with the employer.
3. A: fair price mistaken for a cure of selectivity and conflict; C: selectivity cured but conflict and suitability ignored.
4. A: late disclosure given only when asked treated as sufficient; B: timing of purchase confused with the existence of the conflict.
