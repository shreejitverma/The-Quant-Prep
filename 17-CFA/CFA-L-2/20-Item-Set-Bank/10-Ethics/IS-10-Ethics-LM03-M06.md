# Solstice Crowd Markets: The Luma Listing

> [!info] Timer
> 12 minutes for 4 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Solstice Crowd Markets operates an equity crowdfunding platform in Lisbon where retail investors buy shares in young companies, and a secondary board where those shares later trade.
Kwame Asante, CFA, is head of due diligence and decides which applicant companies are listed.
Imogen Hart, CFA, is head of market operations and supervises the two traders who run Solstice's own accounts on the secondary board.

At the January board meeting, Solstice's founders told Asante that the platform was losing ground to competitors.
They asked him to raise the share of applicants accepted from about 6% to 12% and to cut each review from 30 days to 10.
Last year Solstice received 420 applications and listed 25 companies.
Solstice has no board-approved due diligence procedure; Asante applies criteria he wrote himself.

One applicant is Luma Mobility, which makes electric bicycles for teenagers and sells them through an app used mainly by 13- to 17-year-olds.
Luma plans to promote its crowdfunding round by sending in-app messages to all app users, inviting them to "tell your parents about owning a piece of Luma."
The app collects users' names, ages, and locations.
Several countries where Luma operates prohibit collecting personal data from minors without parental consent, and Luma's legal documents do not say whether it obtains that consent.
Luma's offering document states that only adults may invest.

Luma's shares from an earlier round already trade on Solstice's secondary board.
To make Luma look actively traded before the new round opens, Hart instructs her traders to enter matching buy and sell orders in Luma between Solstice's two proprietary accounts in weeks 3 and 4, so that no shares change beneficial owner.

Exhibit 1: Luma shares traded on Solstice's secondary board

| Week | Reported volume (shares) | Of which, matched trades between Solstice's accounts | Solstice market-making fills with outside investors |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 12,000 | 0 | 2,100 |
| 2 | 14,000 | 0 | 2,600 |
| 3 | 40,000 | 24,000 | 3,000 |
| 4 | 48,000 | 31,000 | 3,400 |

Asante also pays three social media influencers EUR 2,500 each to post that Luma "is in late-stage talks with a major carmaker."
Asante has no information that any such talks exist.

Asante personally holds shares in two private electric-scooter makers that compete with Luma.
He has not told Solstice or its investors about the holdings.

In a note to the founders, Asante writes: "Luma's offering document limits investment to adults, so how Luma markets the round to its app users is Luma's business, not ours."

## Questions

1. Based on Exhibit 1, the share of Luma's reported volume in weeks 3 and 4 that came from the matched trades, and the most accurate evaluation of Hart's instruction, are: (LOS: los-10-03-a)
   - A. 62.5%; Hart violates Standard II(B) Market Manipulation.
   - B. 62.5%; Hart does not violate the Code and Standards, because the trades were executed at market prices between Solstice's own accounts and no investor's account was involved.
   - C. 48.2%; Hart violates Standard II(B) Market Manipulation.
2. Which firm policy would most likely best protect Asante from the threat posed by the founders' January request? (LOS: los-10-03-b)
   - A. A board-approved, documented due diligence procedure that Asante applies to every applicant, so that listing decisions do not depend on acceptance-rate targets
   - B. A target acceptance rate of 12%, disclosed to platform investors in the platform's terms of use
   - C. A 30-day minimum review period, with no change to how listing decisions are made
3. With respect to his holdings in the two scooter makers, Asante most likely: (LOS: los-10-03-a)
   - A. does not violate the Code and Standards, because the companies are private and their shares cannot trade on any market.
   - B. violates Standard VI(A) Avoid or Disclose Conflicts, and should divest the holdings or disclose them to Solstice and, if the companies list on the platform, to platform investors.
   - C. violates Standard II(A) Material Nonpublic Information, because he may learn Luma's confidential plans during due diligence.
4. Asante's statement in his note to the founders is most likely: (LOS: los-10-03-b)
   - A. correct, because Luma's offering document restricts investment to adults.
   - B. incorrect, because Solstice must reject any company whose products are sold to minors.
   - C. incorrect, because Luma's marketing to app users who are minors may breach data protection law, so under Standard I(A) Asante should confirm with legal counsel that Luma complies before listing it.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **A.** Reported volume in weeks 3 and 4 $= 40{,}000 + 48{,}000 = 88{,}000$ shares.
> Matched trades $= 24{,}000 + 31{,}000 = 55{,}000$ shares.
> Share $= 55{,}000 / 88{,}000 = 0.6250$, or 62.5%; genuine volume is only $88{,}000 - 55{,}000 = 33{,}000$ shares.
> Matched buy and sell orders with no change in beneficial ownership are wash trades, which create a false impression of liquidity to attract investors to the new round, transaction-based manipulation under Standard II(B) (V10 p.235).
> As supervisor of the traders who executed her instruction, Hart also fails Standard IV(C).
> B is wrong because trading between the firm's own accounts at market prices is exactly what makes the volume fictitious; the harm falls on outside investors misled by the reported volume.
> C is wrong because it divides the matched trades by all four weeks of volume, $55{,}000 / 114{,}000 = 0.4825$, although the matched trades occurred only in weeks 3 and 4.
> The market-making fills are genuine trades with outside investors and are not part of the matched volume.
> 2. **A.** The founders' commercial pressure to double the acceptance rate and cut reviews to a third of their length threatens the objectivity of Asante's due diligence under Standard I(B) (V10 p.271).
> A board-approved, documented due diligence procedure gives him an objective standard to apply and to defend, whatever the acceptance rate it produces.
> The rate they ask for would mean listing about $0.12 \times 420 = 50.4$, or 50, companies a year, against 25 last year.
> B is wrong because a target rate builds the pressure into policy; disclosing it does not restore objectivity.
> C is wrong because a longer review period alone does not stop listing decisions from being bent toward the target.
> 3. **B.** Owning competitors of companies he evaluates for listing could reasonably be expected to impair Asante's objectivity, so Standard VI(A) requires him to avoid the conflict by divesting or to disclose it to his employer and, if the companies list, to platform investors (V10 p.272).
> A is wrong because the conflict comes from his economic interest in Luma's competitors, not from whether their shares trade.
> C is wrong because it names a standard that applies only if he acts on material nonpublic information; the issue raised by the holdings themselves is the undisclosed conflict.
> 4. **C.** Luma plans to market its round through messages to app users who are mainly 13 to 17 and to collect their data, which several of its countries prohibit without parental consent, so the strategy may be illegal (V10 p.270, p.271).
> Under Standard I(A), Asante must not knowingly participate in or assist a violation of applicable law, so he should confirm with legal counsel that Luma complies before listing it.
> A is wrong because an adults-only clause in the offering document does not make marketing to minors lawful.
> B is wrong because no standard bars listing a company that sells products to teenagers; the problem is the possibly unlawful marketing and data collection.
> The EUR 7,500 paid to influencers to spread unfounded talk of carmaker negotiations is a separate II(B) violation that none of these questions tests.

## Distractor design

1. B: trades between the firm's own accounts at market prices treated as harmless; C: wrong denominator, matched trades divided by all four weeks of volume (misread exhibit).
2. B: pressure written into policy and cured by disclosure; C: procedural timing fix that leaves decision criteria open to the target.
3. A: conflict judged by tradability rather than by economic interest; C: wrong standard sharing the keyword "confidential" with the fact pattern.
4. A: the character's own rationalization that an adults-only clause cures the marketing; B: over-restrictive rule that bans listing companies whose products are sold to minors.
