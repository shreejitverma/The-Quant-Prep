# Halden University Endowment: Property, Equity Premiums, and the Cycle

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Grace Okafor, CFA, is a senior investment analyst at the Halden University Endowment.
The endowment's investment committee, chaired by trustee Robert Vance, has asked her to evaluate a direct commercial property purchase and to review the endowment's listed equity sleeve.

The property is Wexford Logistics Park, a warehouse let to Tollan Freight plc on a seven-year lease.
Exhibit 1 presents the terms and the rates Okafor will use.

Exhibit 1: Wexford Logistics Park

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Annual rent, fixed, paid at the end of each year | EUR 1,200,000 |
| Remaining lease term | 7 years |
| Estimated resale value at the end of year 7 | EUR 18,000,000 |
| Yield on Tollan's seven-year senior unsecured bond | 6.10% |
| Seven-year government bond yield | 4.60% |
| Required property risk premium | 0.75% |
| Required liquidity premium | 1.25% |
| Asking price | EUR 17,000,000 |

Okafor builds the property's required return from the tenant's bond yield, because that yield already includes the real rate, expected inflation, the inflation-uncertainty premium, and Tollan's credit premium.
A second property, let to a national government agency with rent indexed each year to consumer prices, is also available.
Okafor intends to value it later with a discount rate appropriate to its cash flows.

At the committee meeting, Vance makes the following comments.

"Over the long run stocks always beat inflation, so investors should not need much of an equity risk premium."
"Property rents barely move over the cycle, so real estate is a good hedge for bad times."

Okafor then turns to the listed equity sleeve.
Its largest holding is Carrow Industrials, a maker of construction machinery.
Exhibit 2 shows her inputs for Carrow's required return on equity.

Exhibit 2: Carrow Industrials required return inputs

| Component | Value |
|---|---|
| Long-term real default-free rate | 1.00% |
| Long-term expected inflation | 2.20% |
| Inflation-uncertainty premium | 0.50% |
| Credit premium on Carrow's long-term debt | 1.40% |
| Additional premium for Carrow's equity over its debt | 2.90% |

The endowment's equity benchmark is also under review.
Exhibit 3 provides index data.

Exhibit 3: Domestic equity index

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Current index level | 4,200 |
| Trailing 12-month earnings per index unit | 240 |
| 10-year average of inflation-adjusted earnings per index unit | 168 |
| Long-run average cyclically adjusted P/E | 17.0 |

Vance argues that the market is cheap because its trailing P/E is close to its long-run average.
Okafor notes that the economy is at the end of a long expansion and that corporate profit margins are at a record high.

Finally, Okafor's colleague expects the economy to enter a short recession next year and then recover.
The endowment also holds Linden Foods, a producer of packaged staple foods, and the committee wants to know how earnings forecasts for Carrow and Linden should differ as the economy comes out of the recession.

## Questions

1. The value of Wexford Logistics Park at Okafor's required return, and the resulting recommendation, are closest to: (LOS: los-09-01-k)
   - A. EUR 16,661,338; do not buy.
   - B. EUR 17,821,220; buy.
   - C. EUR 18,567,385; buy.
2. For the second property, let to a government agency with rent indexed to consumer prices, the discount rate applied to its cash flows should most appropriately include: (LOS: los-09-01-k)
   - A. the real default-free rate, an equity-like premium for the uncertain property value, and a liquidity premium.
   - B. the real default-free rate, expected inflation, the inflation-uncertainty premium, an equity-like premium, and a liquidity premium.
   - C. the real default-free rate, expected inflation, the inflation-uncertainty premium, a credit premium, an equity-like premium, and a liquidity premium.
3. Vance's comment about the equity risk premium is: (LOS: los-09-01-h)
   - A. correct, because equities provide long-run protection against inflation.
   - B. incorrect, because equity returns are positively correlated with consumption, making equities a poor hedge that requires a large premium.
   - C. incorrect, because equity returns are negatively correlated with consumption, making equities a good hedge that requires a large premium.
4. Based on Exhibit 2, the required return on Carrow's equity is closest to: (LOS: los-09-01-h)
   - A. 6.6%.
   - B. 8.0%.
   - C. 9.4%.
5. Based on Exhibit 3, the cyclically adjusted P/E and the best response to Vance's argument are: (LOS: los-09-01-j)
   - A. 17.5; the market is fairly valued relative to history.
   - B. 25.0; trailing earnings are cyclically high, so the trailing P/E understates how expensive the market is.
   - C. 25.0; trailing earnings are cyclically depressed, so the trailing P/E overstates how expensive the market is.
6. As the economy emerges from the expected recession, Okafor should most likely forecast: (LOS: los-09-01-i)
   - A. faster short-term earnings growth for Carrow than for Linden, with long-term growth expectations for both changing much less than short-term expectations.
   - B. faster short-term earnings growth for Linden than for Carrow, because non-cyclical earnings recover first.
   - C. sharply lower long-term earnings growth for Carrow, because its earnings fell further during the recession.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **A.** Required return $= 6.10\% + 0.75\% + 1.25\% = 8.10\%$, which stacks the equity-like premium $\kappa^i_{t,s}$ and the liquidity premium $\phi^i_{t,s}$ on the tenant's yield.
> Present values at 8.10%: years 1 to 6 give 1,110,083; 1,026,904; 949,957; 878,777; 812,929; 752,016, and year 7 gives $19{,}200{,}000/1.081^7 = 11{,}130{,}672$.
> The total is EUR 16,661,338, which is below the EUR 17,000,000 asking price, so the purchase does not clear the 8.10% hurdle and Okafor should not buy.
> B is wrong because it omits the 1.25% liquidity premium and discounts at 6.85%, which gives EUR 17,821,220 and a false buy signal.
> C is wrong because it discounts at the tenant's bond yield of 6.10%, omitting both property premiums, which gives EUR 18,567,385.
> 2. **A.** Rent indexed to consumer prices is certain in real terms, so neither expected inflation nor the inflation-uncertainty premium belongs in the rate applied to it, and a government tenant carries no credit premium.
> What remains is the real rate plus the equity-like premium for the uncertain end-of-lease value and the liquidity premium: $l_{t,s} + \kappa^i_{t,s} + \phi^i_{t,s}$.
> B is wrong because it treats the indexed rent as a nominal cash flow, adding $\theta_{t,s} + \pi_{t,s}$.
> C is wrong because it also adds a tenant credit premium $\gamma^i_{t,s}$, which is appropriate for a corporate tenant, not a government agency.
> 3. **B.** Equities pay off well in good times and poorly in recessions, so their returns move with consumption.
> Equivalently, their future value covaries negatively with the inter-temporal rate of substitution, so they are a poor hedge against bad consumption outcomes and investors demand a large premium.
> A is wrong because long-run inflation protection does not address when the payoffs occur; the premium depends on the covariance with bad times.
> C is wrong because it reverses the correlation: a good hedge would carry a small or negative premium, not a large one.
> 4. **B.** The equity risk premium over a default-free bond is $\lambda^i_{t,s} = \gamma^i_{t,s} + \kappa^i_{t,s} = 1.40\% + 2.90\% = 4.30\%$.
> The required return is $l + \theta + \pi + \lambda = 1.00\% + 2.20\% + 0.50\% + 4.30\% = 8.0\%$.
> A is wrong because it treats $\kappa$ alone as the equity risk premium and omits the credit premium: $1.00\% + 2.20\% + 0.50\% + 2.90\% = 6.6\%$.
> C is wrong because it adds $\gamma$ on top of $\lambda$ and so counts the credit premium twice: $1.00\% + 2.20\% + 0.50\% + 1.40\% + 4.30\% = 9.4\%$.
> 5. **B.** The cyclically adjusted P/E is $4{,}200 / 168 = 25.0$, against a trailing P/E of $4{,}200 / 240 = 17.5$.
> At the end of a long expansion with record margins, trailing earnings are cyclically high, so the trailing P/E looks normal while the cycle-smoothed measure is well above its 17.0 long-run average.
> A is wrong because it uses the trailing P/E, which is flattered by peak earnings, as if it were the cyclically adjusted measure.
> C is wrong because it gets the number right but reverses the cyclical position: earnings are elevated, not depressed, late in an expansion.
> 6. **A.** Cyclical earnings fall hardest into a recession and rebound fastest out of it, often off a reduced cost base, so Carrow's near-term growth should exceed Linden's as recovery begins.
> Long-term growth expectations reflect trend growth and move far less with the cycle than short-term expectations do.
> B is wrong because it reverses the cyclical pattern: non-cyclical earnings are the steadier ones, with a smaller fall and a smaller rebound.
> C is wrong because it extrapolates a temporary cyclical decline into the long-term growth rate.

## Distractor design

1. B: liquidity premium omitted from the real estate discount rate (component omitted); C: tenant bond yield used as the property discount rate (both property premiums omitted).
2. B: indexed rent treated as a nominal cash flow (real vs nominal); C: corporate-tenant rate applied to a government tenant (wrong formula variant).
3. A: inflation protection confused with consumption hedging (wrong risk concept); C: sign of the consumption correlation reversed (sign).
4. A: $\kappa$ treated as the whole equity risk premium (component omitted); C: credit premium counted twice (double counting).
5. A: trailing P/E used instead of the cyclically adjusted P/E (wrong measure); C: late-cycle earnings treated as depressed (cycle phase reversed).
6. B: cyclical and non-cyclical recovery patterns swapped (direction); C: short-term cyclical decline extrapolated to long-term growth (horizon).
