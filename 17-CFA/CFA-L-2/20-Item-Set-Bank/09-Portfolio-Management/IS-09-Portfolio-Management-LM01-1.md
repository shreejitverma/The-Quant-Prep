# Fjellheim Municipal Pension Fund: Rates, Policy, and Break-Even Inflation

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Sofia Brandt, CFA, is the head of fixed income at Fjellheim Municipal Pension Fund, a defined benefit plan whose liabilities are indexed to consumer prices.
The fund's chief investment officer, Anders Lind, has asked Brandt to prepare a macro briefing before the fund rebalances its sovereign bond portfolio.

Brandt begins by screening four foreign sovereign markets for a new allocation to short-dated government bills.
Exhibit 1 summarizes the data her team collected.

Exhibit 1: Selected macroeconomic data

| Country | Nominal GDP growth (trend) | Inflation (trend) | Volatility of real GDP growth | Policy rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aurelia | 7.0% | 4.5% | Low | 6.25% |
| Beltrania | 5.5% | 2.5% | High | 4.00% |
| Corvia | 4.0% | 1.0% | Low | 1.50% |
| Dunmark | 6.0% | 4.0% | High | 5.50% |

Lind reminds Brandt that Corvia suffered a banking crisis two years ago.
He notes that since the crisis the volatility of Corvian real GDP growth has risen from a very low base, while remaining low relative to the other markets in Exhibit 1, yet short-dated Corvian index-linked government bonds now trade at negative real yields.
He asks Brandt to explain the apparent contradiction.

Brandt then turns to the home economy.
The fund's economist estimates the equilibrium real short-term interest rate at 1.25% and the central bank's inflation target at 2.0%.
Exhibit 2 shows current conditions and the economist's projection for one year from now.

Exhibit 2: Home economy conditions

| Item | Current | Projected in one year |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation | 3.6% | 2.4% |
| Output gap | -1.5% | -3.0% |
| Central bank policy rate | 4.25% | not given |
| Three-month bill yield | 4.20% | not given |

The home sovereign yield curve is currently almost flat.
The economist believes the economy is sliding into recession and that the central bank will set its policy rate according to the Taylor rule.

Because the fund's liabilities are indexed, Brandt tracks the spread between conventional and index-linked home sovereign bonds.
Exhibit 3 shows today's yields.

Exhibit 3: Home sovereign yields (zero-coupon equivalent)

| Maturity | Conventional yield | Index-linked real yield |
|---|---|---|
| 2 years | 3.10% | 1.05% |
| 10 years | 3.85% | 1.20% |

The economist estimates that investors currently demand a 0.45% premium for inflation uncertainty at the 10-year horizon.
She expects that, as the recession deepens, 10-year inflation expectations will fall by 0.40 percentage points.
She also expects the 10-year inflation-uncertainty premium to rise by 0.15 percentage points because investors will become less sure how the central bank will respond.

During the meeting, Lind comments on Exhibit 3.
"The 10-year spread is the market's inflation forecast, pure and simple, so our indexed liabilities should be projected at that rate."
Brandt replies that she wants to separate the forecast from any compensation for risk before she projects the liabilities.

## Questions

1. Based on Exhibit 1, the average real short-term interest rate is most likely highest in: (LOS: los-09-01-c)
   - A. Aurelia.
   - B. Beltrania.
   - C. Corvia.
2. The best explanation of the Corvian observation that Lind raises is that since the crisis: (LOS: los-09-01-c)
   - A. expected future real growth has fallen by enough to outweigh the effect of higher volatility, and safe-haven demand for scarce index-linked bonds has pushed their yields down.
   - B. higher growth volatility has reduced the compensation risk-averse investors require, which lowers equilibrium real rates.
   - C. higher expected inflation has reduced real yields one for one, because index-linked yields embed expected inflation.
3. Based on Exhibit 2, the current policy rate implied by the Taylor rule is closest to: (LOS: los-09-01-d)
   - A. 3.30%.
   - B. 4.90%.
   - C. 6.40%.
4. If the economist's projection is realized and the central bank follows the Taylor rule, over the next year the policy rate and the home yield curve will most likely: (LOS: los-09-01-d)
   - A. fall to about 2.35%, with the curve steepening.
   - B. rise to about 5.35%, with the curve flattening.
   - C. fall to about 2.35%, with the curve inverting.
5. The expected rate of inflation over the next 10 years implied by Exhibit 3 and the economist's premium estimate is closest to: (LOS: los-09-01-e)
   - A. 2.20%.
   - B. 2.65%.
   - C. 3.10%.
6. If the economist's expectations for the 10-year horizon are realized, the 10-year break-even inflation rate will be closest to: (LOS: los-09-01-e)
   - A. 2.25%.
   - B. 2.40%.
   - C. 2.80%.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Real growth is nominal growth less inflation: Aurelia $7.0\% - 4.5\% = 2.5\%$, Beltrania $5.5\% - 2.5\% = 3.0\%$, Corvia $4.0\% - 1.0\% = 3.0\%$, Dunmark $6.0\% - 4.0\% = 2.0\%$.
> Real short rates are positively related to both trend real growth and the volatility of that growth.
> Beltrania ties Corvia for the highest real growth and has high rather than low volatility, so its real rate should be highest.
> A is wrong because it ranks on nominal growth (7.0%, the highest) without subtracting Aurelia's 4.5% inflation.
> C is wrong because it uses real growth alone and ignores volatility, which breaks the Beltrania-Corvia tie in Beltrania's favor.
> 2. **A.** Higher volatility of growth, taken alone, raises the real rate because risk-averse investors demand more compensation for income risk.
> Real yields can still fall if expected future growth falls by more, since lower expected growth raises the demand to save and lowers the market-clearing real rate.
> Index-linked bonds are also scarce and in crisis demand as a safe haven, which can push their yields below zero.
> B is wrong because it reverses the sign of the volatility effect: more volatility raises, not lowers, the required real rate.
> C is wrong because a real (index-linked) yield excludes expected inflation, so a change in $\theta_{t,s}$ does not move it one for one.
> 3. **B.** $pr_t = l_t + \iota_t + 0.5(\iota_t - \iota_t^*) + 0.5(Y_t - Y_t^*) = 1.25 + 3.6 + 0.5(3.6 - 2.0) + 0.5(-1.5) = 1.25 + 3.6 + 0.80 - 0.75 = 4.90\%$.
> The check form gives the same answer: $1.25 + 1.5(3.6) - 0.5(2.0) + 0.5(-1.5) = 4.90\%$.
> A is wrong because it uses the 2.0% target instead of actual inflation as the compensation term: $1.25 + 2.0 + 0.80 - 0.75 = 3.30\%$.
> C is wrong because it adds the output gap with the wrong sign: $1.25 + 3.6 + 0.80 + 0.75 = 6.40\%$.
> 4. **A.** The projected Taylor rate is $1.25 + 2.4 + 0.5(2.4 - 2.0) + 0.5(-3.0) = 1.25 + 2.4 + 0.20 - 1.50 = 2.35\%$, below the neutral rate of $1.25\% + 2.0\% = 3.25\%$.
> A cut from 4.25% to about 2.35% drives the short end down, while long yields fall less because investors expect policy to normalize as the recession recedes, so the flat curve steepens.
> B is wrong because it flips the sign of the negative output gap, $1.25 + 2.4 + 0.20 + 1.50 = 5.35\%$, and then applies the late-expansion flattening pattern.
> C is wrong because it gets the policy rate right but confuses the inversion that predicts a recession with the steepening that happens during one.
> 5. **A.** The 10-year break-even inflation rate is $3.85\% - 1.20\% = 2.65\%$, and $BEI = \theta_{t,s} + \pi_{t,s}$, so $\theta_{t,10} = 2.65\% - 0.45\% = 2.20\%$.
> B is wrong because it treats the break-even rate as a pure inflation forecast, which is exactly Lind's error.
> C is wrong because it adds the premium instead of subtracting it: $2.65\% + 0.45\% = 3.10\%$.
> 6. **B.** $\Delta BEI = \Delta\theta + \Delta\pi = -0.40 + 0.15 = -0.25$ percentage points, so the new break-even rate is $2.65\% - 0.25\% = 2.40\%$.
> The two components move in opposite directions, so the size of each move decides the net effect.
> A is wrong because it includes only the expectations leg: $2.65\% - 0.40\% = 2.25\%$.
> C is wrong because it includes only the premium leg: $2.65\% + 0.15\% = 2.80\%$.

## Distractor design

1. A: ranking on nominal instead of real growth (inflation not subtracted); C: real growth used without the volatility dimension (one driver dropped).
2. B: sign of the volatility effect reversed; C: real yield treated as if it contained expected inflation (nominal vs real).
3. A: target inflation substituted for actual inflation in the compensation term (wrong formula variant); C: output gap added with the wrong sign (sign).
4. B: sign of the output gap flipped, leading to a hike and the late-cycle shape (sign); C: correct rate but inversion confused with the in-recession steepening (predictor vs response).
5. B: break-even rate read as expected inflation (premium ignored); C: premium added instead of subtracted (sign).
6. A: only the expectations component moved (one leg dropped); C: only the premium component moved (other leg dropped).
