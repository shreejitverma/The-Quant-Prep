# Halvorsen Family Office: Choosing an Emerging Markets ETF

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Marta Lindqvist, CFA, is a portfolio manager at Halvorsen Family Office, a single-family office in New York with USD 1.4 billion in assets.
The investment committee has approved two separate emerging markets equity positions.
The first is a tactical overweight to be held for four months and then sold.
The second is a strategic core allocation expected to be held for five years.
Lindqvist has narrowed the choice to three US-listed ETFs that track similar broad emerging markets indexes.

Exhibit 1: Candidate emerging markets ETFs

| | ETF P | ETF Q | ETF R |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual management fee | 0.09% | 0.30% | 0.19% |
| One-way commission | 0.03% | 0.03% | 0.03% |
| Quoted bid-ask spread | 0.28% | 0.04% | 0.12% |
| Median daily volume | USD 6 million | USD 2.1 billion | USD 140 million |
| Number of holdings | 1,820 | 890 | 1,240 |
| Assets under management | USD 3.9 billion | USD 31.5 billion | USD 7.2 billion |

Lindqvist asks Oren Pataki, the office's trader, why ETF P's spread is so much wider than ETF Q's.
Pataki obtains the following component estimates from a market maker that quotes ETF P.

Exhibit 2: Market maker's spread component estimates for ETF P (% of value)

| Component | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Creation/redemption fees and other direct trading costs | 0.05% |
| Bid-ask spreads of the underlying securities | 0.16% |
| Risk premium for hedging or carrying the position until close | 0.05% |
| Market maker's desired profit spread | 0.04% |
| Discount for likelihood of an offsetting order in a short time | 0.02% |

At 3:45 p.m. New York time on 14 August, Pataki notes that ETF Q last traded at USD 39.20.
Its indicated NAV (iNAV) was USD 38.10.
The iNAV is calculated from the creation basket using the closing prices of the Asian and European markets that hold most of ETF Q's securities.
The S&P 500 Index had risen 2.4% since those markets closed.

Separately, the family holds ETF S, a US large-cap equity ETF, for a trust account.
On the same afternoon ETF S traded at USD 51.87 while its iNAV was USD 52.00.
Pataki estimates that the costs of arbitrage for ETF S are well below this gap.

Pataki then makes three statements about how the office's own ETF trades work.

Statement 1: "When we buy ETF Q on the exchange, the fund sponsor issues new shares to us and receives our cash."

Statement 2: "Our exchange purchase of ETF Q settles through the clearing corporation on a T+2 basis, although market makers are permitted a longer settlement window of up to six days."

Statement 3: "Because we are a large investor, we can exchange cash directly with the sponsor for ETF Q shares at NAV whenever the spread looks too wide."

Lindqvist ends the meeting by asking Pataki to prepare a cost comparison.
He is to assume that each position is bought and later sold in one trade at the quoted spread and commission, and to exclude compounding.

## Questions

1. Given the pricing of ETF S, the transaction by authorized participants that is most likely to occur is that APs will: (LOS: los-09-03-a)
   - A. buy the creation basket securities, deliver them to the sponsor for new ETF S shares, and sell those shares.
   - B. buy ETF S shares on the exchange, deliver them to the sponsor for the redemption basket, and sell the securities.
   - C. sell ETF S shares short on the exchange and wait for the sponsor to repurchase shares in the secondary market.
2. At 3:45 p.m., ETF Q's premium or discount to iNAV and its most likely interpretation are: (LOS: los-09-03-e)
   - A. a premium of 2.89% that largely reflects a stale iNAV, with the ETF price acting as a price-discovery vehicle.
   - B. a premium of 2.81% that largely reflects a stale iNAV, with the ETF price acting as a price-discovery vehicle.
   - C. a premium of 2.89% that is a mispricing APs can capture by buying the basket and creating shares.
3. Which of Pataki's statements about ETF trading is correct? (LOS: los-09-03-b)
   - A. Statement 1
   - B. Statement 2
   - C. Statement 3
4. Based on Exhibit 2, the upper bound on the bid-ask spread the market maker would quote for ETF P is closest to: (LOS: los-09-03-d)
   - A. 0.24%.
   - B. 0.28%.
   - C. 0.32%.
5. The expected total holding-period cost of the four-month tactical position if it is implemented with ETF Q is closest to: (LOS: los-09-03-f)
   - A. 0.20%.
   - B. 0.24%.
   - C. 0.40%.
6. Based on expected total holding-period costs, the ETFs most appropriate for the tactical position and for the five-year core position are, respectively: (LOS: los-09-03-h)
   - A. ETF P and ETF Q.
   - B. ETF Q and ETF P.
   - C. ETF Q and ETF Q.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** ETF S trades at a discount: $(51.87 - 52.00)/52.00 = -0.0025$, or $-0.25\%$, and the gap exceeds arbitrage costs.
> APs buy the cheap ETF shares, redeem them with the sponsor for the redemption basket, and sell the basket securities at fair value, which pushes the ETF price back toward iNAV.
> A is wrong because buying the basket and creating new shares is the premium-side trade.
> C is wrong because sponsors do not trade in the secondary market; shares outstanding fall only when an AP redeems in the primary market.
> 2. **A.** Premium $= (39.20 - 38.10)/38.10 = 1.10/38.10 = 0.028871$, or $2.89\%$.
> The iNAV uses closing prices from Asian and European markets that closed hours earlier, while US equities have since risen 2.4%, so the iNAV is stale and the live ETF price is incorporating information the underlying markets have not yet priced.
> B is wrong because it divides by the ETF price rather than the iNAV: $1.10/39.20 = 0.028061$, or $2.81\%$.
> C is wrong because the apparent premium is informational; an AP buying the basket at stale closing values is not locking in a riskless gap, since the basket will reopen at prices that reflect the same news.
> 3. **B.** US ETF trades settle T+2 through the clearing corporation's continuous net settlement, and market makers are allowed up to six days.
> A is wrong because a secondary-market purchase is a trade between two investors; the issuer is not involved and receives no cash.
> C is wrong because only authorized participants transact with the sponsor in the primary market, and they do so in creation-unit size, usually in kind; being a large investor does not confer that access.
> 4. **B.** The spread is bounded above by the sum of the cost and risk components less the offsetting-order discount: $0.05 + 0.16 + 0.05 + 0.04 - 0.02 = 0.28\%$, which matches ETF P's quoted spread in Exhibit 1.
> A is wrong because it omits the market maker's profit spread: $0.05 + 0.16 + 0.05 - 0.02 = 0.24\%$.
> C is wrong because it adds the offsetting-order discount instead of subtracting it: $0.05 + 0.16 + 0.05 + 0.04 + 0.02 = 0.32\%$.
> 5. **A.** Round-trip trading cost $= 2 \times 0.03\% + 0.04\% = 0.10\%$.
> Prorated fee $= (4/12) \times 0.30\% = 0.10\%$.
> Total $= 0.10\% + 0.10\% = 0.20\%$.
> B is wrong because it charges the spread twice, once on purchase and once on sale: $0.06 + 0.08 + 0.10 = 0.24\%$; each trade pays only half the quoted spread.
> C is wrong because it charges the full annual fee: $0.06 + 0.04 + 0.30 = 0.40\%$.
> 6. **B.** Round-trip costs are P $0.34\%$, Q $0.10\%$, R $0.18\%$.
> Four-month totals: P $0.34 + (4/12)(0.09) = 0.3700\%$, Q $0.2000\%$, R $0.18 + (4/12)(0.19) = 0.2433\%$, so ETF Q is cheapest for the tactical trade.
> Five-year totals: P $0.34 + 5(0.09) = 0.79\%$, Q $0.10 + 5(0.30) = 1.60\%$, R $0.18 + 5(0.19) = 1.13\%$, so ETF P is cheapest for the core position.
> Trading costs dominate short holds and management fees dominate long holds, which is why tactical users select on liquidity and spread and buy-and-hold users select on fee.
> A is wrong because it reverses the two horizons.
> C is wrong because it ignores fee accrual; ETF Q's 0.30% fee costs 1.50% over five years, lifting its five-year total to 1.60%, more than double ETF P's 0.79%.

## Distractor design

1. A: premium-side trade applied to a discount (direction flipped); C: sponsor credited with a secondary-market role (wrong actor).
2. B: wrong denominator (ETF price instead of iNAV); C: stale iNAV treated as a true arbitrage mispricing.
3. A: primary and secondary market confused (issuer assumed to receive cash); C: investor size confused with AP status.
4. A: profit spread component omitted; C: sign error on the offsetting-order discount.
5. B: spread double-counted for purchase and sale; C: annual fee not prorated for a four-month hold.
6. A: horizons reversed; C: selection made on trading cost alone, ignoring fee accrual over five years.
