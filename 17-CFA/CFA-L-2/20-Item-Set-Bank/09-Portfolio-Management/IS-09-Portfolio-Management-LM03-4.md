# Kestrel County Retirement System: ETFs in the Pension Toolkit

> [!info] Timer
> 12 minutes for 4 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Adaeze Nwosu, CFA, is chief investment officer of the Kestrel County Employees' Retirement System, a defined benefit plan with USD 6.8 billion in assets.
Almost all assets are run by external managers in separate accounts.
At a quarterly meeting with the plan's consultant, Marcus Bell, Nwosu reviews three recent actions in which the plan used ETFs.

Action 1: The plan received USD 30 million of employer contributions that will not be allocated to managers for about two months, and the staff bought a large-cap equity ETF so the money would track the plan's equity benchmark in the meantime.

Action 2: The board terminated the plan's small-cap equity manager.
Until a replacement manager is hired, the staff holds a Russell 2000 ETF, and it expects to deliver the ETF shares to the new manager as initial funding.

Action 3: The staff found that the plan's combined equity managers held almost no real estate investment trusts, although REITs are 3% of the plan's equity benchmark, and it bought a REIT ETF to fill the gap.

Bell reviews the trade in Action 1.
The staff bought USD 30 million of the large-cap ETF with a commission of USD 4,500, and expects to pay the same commission when selling.
The ETF's quoted bid-ask spread is 0.02%, its annual management fee is 0.07%, and its 12-month tracking error is 0.03%.
Bell asks the staff to assume the quoted spread applies to the full trade size and to exclude compounding.

Bell then shows a comparison of two funds the staff considered for other mandates.

Exhibit 1: Selected ETF trading data

| | Russell 2000 ETF | Equal-weighted large-cap ETF |
|---|---|---|
| Number of holdings | About 2,000 | About 600 |
| Median daily volume | USD 3.8 billion | USD 0.9 million |
| Average bid-ask spread | 0.01% | 0.12% |
| Index futures available for hedging | Yes | No |
| Maximum premium over 12 months | 0.12% | 0.96% |

Bell remarks: "The Russell 2000 ETF holds many small stocks whose own spreads are wide, so I expected its spread to be the wider of the two."

On the day the plan bought the Russell 2000 ETF, Bell observed it trading at USD 64.18 against an iNAV of USD 64.05.
He explains how an authorized participant might respond.
The AP estimates that trading the creation basket would cost 0.08% of the basket's value.
The sponsor charges a fixed creation fee of USD 2,500 per creation unit of 50,000 shares.
The AP can trade both the ETF and the basket at the observed prices.

## Questions

1. Actions 1, 2, and 3 are best described, respectively, as: (LOS: los-09-03-h)
   - A. cash equitization, transition management, and completion.
   - B. cash equitization, completion, and transition management.
   - C. rebalancing, transition management, and completion.
2. The expected total holding-period cost of the ETF position in Action 1 is closest to: (LOS: los-09-03-f)
   - A. USD 18,500.
   - B. USD 24,500.
   - C. USD 36,000.
3. The most likely explanation for the Russell 2000 ETF's narrower spread in Exhibit 1 is that: (LOS: los-09-03-d)
   - A. its creation/redemption fees are lower than those of the equal-weighted ETF.
   - B. its heavy, continuous order flow and liquid index futures let market makers offset or hedge trades quickly.
   - C. fewer market makers compete to quote it, so each can quote a tighter spread.
4. Given the Russell 2000 ETF's price relative to iNAV, the AP's most likely transaction and its net profit per creation unit are: (LOS: los-09-03-a)
   - A. buy the basket, exchange it for a creation unit, and sell the ETF shares, for a net profit of about USD 1,438.
   - B. buy the basket, exchange it for a creation unit, and sell the ETF shares, for a net profit of about USD 4,000.
   - C. buy ETF shares, exchange them for the redemption basket, and sell the securities, for a net profit of about USD 1,438.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **A.** Action 1 invests cash awaiting allocation in a benchmark ETF to avoid cash drag, which is cash equitization.
> Action 2 keeps market exposure while one external manager is replaced by another, with the ETF possibly used to fund the new manager, which is transition management.
> Action 3 fills a gap in exposure left by the combined managers, which is completion.
> B is wrong because it swaps the labels on Actions 2 and 3; completion addresses a structural exposure gap, while transition management covers a manager change.
> C is wrong because rebalancing restores drifted asset class weights, whereas Action 1 invests new contributions.
> 2. **A.** One-way commission $= 4{,}500/30{,}000{,}000 = 0.0150\%$.
> Round-trip trading cost $= 2 \times 0.0150\% + 0.02\% = 0.0500\%$.
> Prorated fee $= (2/12) \times 0.07\% = 0.011667\%$.
> Total $= 0.0500\% + 0.011667\% = 0.061667\%$, and $0.00061667 \times 30{,}000{,}000 = \text{USD } 18{,}500$.
> B is wrong because it charges the full spread on both purchase and sale: $(0.03 + 0.04 + 0.011667)\% \times 30$ million $= \text{USD } 24{,}500$.
> C is wrong because it charges a full year of the fee for a two-month hold: $(0.05 + 0.07)\% \times 30$ million $= \text{USD } 36{,}000$.
> The 0.03% tracking error is a decoy: it is a dispersion statistic, not an expected cost.
> 3. **B.** ETF spreads are driven by ETF-level order flow and the ease of hedging, not only by the holdings.
> With USD 3.8 billion of median daily volume, trades are usually matched quickly with offsetting orders, and active Russell 2000 futures let market makers hedge large trades cheaply, so the ETF trades tighter than its underlying small-cap stocks.
> The equal-weighted fund has thin volume and no futures for hedging, so its spread must carry more of the underlying spread, hedging risk, and creation/redemption cost.
> A is wrong because very liquid ETFs rarely need creation/redemption to meet order flow, so those fees have little influence on their spreads.
> C is wrong on sign: more competition among market makers narrows spreads.
> 4. **A.** The ETF trades at a premium of $64.18 - 64.05 = 0.13$ per share, so the AP buys the basket, delivers it for new ETF shares, and sells the shares.
> Gross gain $= 0.13 \times 50{,}000 = 6{,}500$.
> Basket trading cost $= 0.0008 \times 64.05 \times 50{,}000 = 0.0008 \times 3{,}202{,}500 = 2{,}562$.
> Net profit $= 6{,}500 - 2{,}562 - 2{,}500 = \text{USD } 1{,}438$.
> The AP's costs are ultimately passed to investors who trade the ETF through its spread, not to the plan as a non-transacting holder.
> B is wrong because it deducts the creation fee but omits the basket trading costs: $6{,}500 - 2{,}500 = 4{,}000$.
> C is wrong because buying ETF shares and redeeming is the discount-side trade; at a premium it would lose money.

## Distractor design

1. B: completion and transition management labels swapped; C: investing new cash confused with rebalancing.
2. B: spread double-counted for purchase and sale; C: annual fee not prorated for a two-month hold.
3. A: creation/redemption fees credited as a driver for a very liquid ETF; C: sign error on market-maker competition.
4. B: basket transaction costs omitted from the arbitrage profit; C: discount-side trade applied to a premium (direction flipped).
