# Aldergrove Endowment Equity Assumptions

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions (or 12 for 4). Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Priya Natarajan, CFA, is senior strategist at the Aldergrove University endowment, which resets its 20-year capital market assumptions each spring.
This year she is revising the long-run expected return for the equity market of Tessaly, a high-income economy that represents a large share of the endowment's public equity allocation.
An external vendor projects a 9.5% annual nominal return for Tessalian equities, extrapolated from the past 15 years, during which the market P/E rose from 17 to 24 and corporate profits climbed to a record share of GDP.

Natarajan prefers to anchor the forecast on Tessaly's sustainable growth rate.
She assumes that Tessaly is currently on its neoclassical steady-state growth path and assembles Exhibit 1.

**Exhibit 1: Tessaly, macroeconomic inputs**

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| TFP growth | 1.1% per year |
| Labor share of national income | 62% |
| Labor force growth | 0.4% per year |
| Depreciation rate of the capital stock | 8.0% per year |
| Gross national saving rate | 22% of GDP |
| Expected inflation | 2.3% per year |
| Real GDP growth, most recent year | 1.4% |

**Exhibit 2: Tessaly, equity market inputs**

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Dividend yield | 2.1% |
| Net new share issuance by listed companies | 0.6% of shares per year |
| Relative dynamism (growth accruing to unlisted firms) | 0.9% per year |
| Current market P/E | 24 |
| Historical average market P/E | 17 |

For the 20-year horizon, Natarajan sets expected repricing to zero and uses steady-state real output growth as the real growth input.

Tessaly's parliament is debating a pension reform that would raise the gross national saving rate permanently from 22% to 27%.
Natarajan's colleague Martin Osei believes the neoclassical model understates the payoff.
On 14 April 2026, Osei tells the investment committee: "Tessaly's growth is driven by knowledge capital, not machines. I model the economy with an endogenous growth production function in which the marginal product of broadly defined capital is constant at 0.44, with the same depreciation and labor force growth as in Exhibit 1. On that basis, the reform lifts growth for good."

The reform bill also contains two further measures.
First, it adds public investment equal to 1.5% of GDP a year, split equally between a national fiber-optic and data-center network and a program of new government office buildings and parking structures.
Second, it doubles the tax credit available to companies for qualifying research and development spending.

On 16 April 2026, a trustee asks Natarajan to explain why the government should subsidize private research at all, given that companies already profit from their own innovations.
Natarajan promises a written answer and a revised return estimate by the end of the month.

## Questions

1. Based on Exhibit 1, Tessaly's steady-state growth rate of total real output is closest to: (LOS: los-02-02-i)
   - A. 1.77%.
   - B. 2.17%.
   - C. 3.29%.
2. Using the Grinold-Kroner decomposition and Natarajan's assumptions, the 20-year expected nominal return on Tessalian equities is closest to: (LOS: los-02-02-b)
   - A. 5.07%.
   - B. 5.97%.
   - C. 8.07%.
3. Under the neoclassical model, the growth rate of output per worker immediately after the saving rate rises to 27% is closest to: (LOS: los-02-02-i)
   - A. 2.49%.
   - B. 2.65%.
   - C. 4.09%.
4. Under Osei's specification, the growth rate of output per worker after the reform is closest to: (LOS: los-02-02-i)
   - A. 2.20%.
   - B. 3.48%.
   - C. 3.88%.
5. Which component of the public investment program is most likely to raise Tessaly's long-run rate of economic growth? (LOS: los-02-02-h)
   - A. The fiber-optic and data-center network.
   - B. The government office buildings and parking structures.
   - C. Neither, because both raise the capital stock by the same amount and so represent equal capital deepening.
6. The most appropriate economic rationale Natarajan can give the trustee for the research tax credit is that: (LOS: los-02-02-k)
   - A. knowledge spills over to other firms, so the private return on research falls short of its social return and companies underinvest.
   - B. research is subject to sharply diminishing returns within each firm, so companies abandon it before reaching the socially optimal level.
   - C. patents give innovators monopoly profits, and the credit is needed to restore competitive returns to firms without patents.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Capital's share is $\alpha = 1 - 0.62 = 0.38$, and the steady-state divisor is labor's share $1 - \alpha = 0.62$.
> Per capita growth $= \theta/(1-\alpha) = 1.1\%/0.62 = 1.7742\%$; total output growth $= 1.7742\% + 0.4\% = 2.1742\%$.
> A is wrong because it stops at the per capita rate and omits labor force growth $n$.
> C is wrong because it divides by capital's share: $1.1\%/0.38 + 0.4\% = 2.8947\% + 0.4\% = 3.2947\%$.
> Recent real growth of 1.4% is a decoy; steady-state growth depends only on $\theta$, $\alpha$, and $n$.
> 2. **A.** Both components of the dilution term widen the wedge between economic growth and listed-company earnings growth: net new issuance raises shares outstanding, so the net buyback component is $+0.6\%$, and relative dynamism adds 0.9%, so $\Delta S = 0.6\% + 0.9\% = 1.5\%$.
> $E(R_e) = dy + \Delta(P/E) + i + g - \Delta S = 2.1\% + 0 + 2.3\% + 2.1742\% - 1.5\% = 5.0742\%$.
> B is wrong because it ignores relative dynamism and subtracts only net issuance: $2.1\% + 2.3\% + 2.1742\% - 0.6\% = 5.9742\%$.
> C is wrong because it adds the dilution term instead of subtracting it: $2.1\% + 2.3\% + 2.1742\% + 1.5\% = 8.0742\%$.
> The vendor's 9.5% implies permanent P/E expansion or a rising profit share of GDP, neither of which can persist over long horizons.
> 3. **B.** Required investment rate $= \theta/(1-\alpha) + \delta + n = 0.017742 + 0.08 + 0.004 = 0.1017$.
> $\Psi_{old} = 0.1017/0.22 = 0.4625$ and $\Psi_{new} = 0.1017/0.27 = 0.3768$.
> Because Tessaly starts in steady state, the current output-to-capital ratio equals $\Psi_{old} = 0.4625$, so the gap is $0.4625 - 0.3768 = 0.0856$.
> $\Delta y/y = \theta/(1-\alpha) + \alpha s(y/k - \Psi) = 1.7742\% + (0.38)(0.27)(0.0856) = 1.7742\% + 0.8787\% = 2.6529\%$.
> A is wrong because it uses the old saving rate in the adjustment term: $1.7742\% + (0.38)(0.22)(0.0856) = 2.4902\%$.
> C is wrong because it omits $\alpha$ from the adjustment term: $1.7742\% + (0.27)(0.0856) = 4.0865\%$, which is the growth rate of capital per worker, not of output per worker.
> Growth then decays back to 1.7742% as the output-to-capital ratio falls toward $\Psi_{new}$.
> 4. **B.** $\Delta y_e/y_e = sc - \delta - n = (0.27)(0.44) - 0.08 - 0.004 = 0.1188 - 0.084 = 3.4800\%$, and this rate is permanent because the marginal product of broad capital never diminishes.
> A is wrong because it reports the increase in growth rather than the new rate: before the reform $(0.22)(0.44) - 0.084 = 1.2800\%$, so the change is $3.4800\% - 1.2800\% = 2.2000\%$.
> C is wrong because it omits labor force growth: $0.1188 - 0.08 = 3.8800\%$.
> 5. **A.** ICT investment generates network externalities that raise TFP, so it can raise potential growth for a sustained period, beyond its direct capital deepening effect.
> B is wrong because non-ICT structures are pure capital deepening in a high-income economy already on the flat part of the production function, so their growth effect fades under diminishing returns.
> C is wrong because it treats all capital spending as equivalent capital deepening and ignores the externality channel that separates ICT from non-ICT investment.
> 6. **A.** Endogenous growth theory holds that research creates knowledge externalities; because other firms capture part of the benefit, the private return is below the social return, companies underinvest in R&D, and subsidies correct this market failure.
> B is wrong because it attributes the gap to diminishing returns, whereas the rationale rests on the difference between private and social returns caused by spillovers.
> C is wrong because monopoly profits from patents raise, not lower, private returns and so cannot justify a subsidy.

## Distractor design

1. A omits $n$ (per capita rather than total output); C divides TFP growth by capital's share instead of labor's share.
2. B drops relative dynamism from the dilution term; C gets the sign of the dilution term wrong.
3. A uses the pre-reform saving rate in the transition term; C omits $\alpha$, computing capital-per-worker growth instead of output-per-worker growth.
4. A reports the change in the growth rate instead of the level; C omits labor force growth $n$.
5. B treats non-ICT capital as a source of sustained growth; C ignores ICT network externalities.
6. B substitutes diminishing returns for the externality argument; C inverts the effect of patent rents on private returns.
