# Kestrel Motor Components Plant Site

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions (or 12 for 4). Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Kestrel Motor Components is a listed manufacturer of drivetrain parts headquartered in Aurelia, a high-income economy.
The company is deciding whether to build a $640 million assembly plant in Pelagia, a lower-middle-income economy.
The board commissioned a country report from Brightwater Advisory, and strategy director Hana Kovac must check the report's analysis before it is entered into the board papers.

Brightwater's report states that Pelagia's 2025 growth "owes more to total factor productivity than to capital accumulation."
Kovac decides to verify this claim from the national accounts in Exhibit 1.

**Exhibit 1: Pelagia, national accounts (constant prices)**

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Capital stock, end of 2024 | $820 billion |
| Real GDP, 2025 | $330 billion |
| Gross investment, 2025 | 27% of GDP |
| Depreciation (consumption of fixed capital), 2025 | 11% of GDP |
| Real GDP growth, 2025 | 4.6% |
| Growth in hours worked, 2025 | 1.4% |
| Capital share of national income | 0.42 |
| Population growth, 2025 | 1.9% |

Brightwater also compares Pelagia with Sardova, a neighboring economy, and with Aurelia.

**Exhibit 2: Real GDP per capita at PPP and policy profile**

| Country | 2005 | 2025 | Gross saving, % of GDP | Policy profile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pelagia | $4,200 | $9,800 | 29% | Property rights reform in 2006; export processing zones; average tariff cut to 4% |
| Sardova | $5,100 | $5,900 | 27% | State price controls; import substitution behind an average tariff of 28% |
| Aurelia | $48,000 | $58,500 | 21% | High-income benchmark |

In January 2026, Pelagia and Aurelia signed a free trade agreement that removes all tariffs and capital controls between the two countries by 2028.
Kestrel's plant would serve both markets.

The Brightwater draft, dated 9 February 2026, contains two further statements.

Statement 1: "Pelagia imports all of its crude oil and most of its metals. Its limited natural resources will cap long-run growth, and the plant's volume forecasts beyond 2035 should be discounted accordingly."

Statement 2: "The free trade agreement will benefit Pelagia, but Kestrel should model its effects through the standard neoclassical framework rather than assume a permanent change in the growth rate."

In a note to the board dated 11 February 2026, Kovac adds her own interpretation of Exhibit 2.
She writes: "Pelagia's rapid catch-up since 2005, while Sardova fell further behind despite a similar saving rate, shows that poorer countries converge toward the richest only after they adopt the right institutions and an outward trade orientation."

Kovac's finance team will use the report's conclusions to set the plant's target return on capital and its wage assumptions for the first 15 years of operation.

## Questions

1. Based on Exhibit 1, Pelagia's TFP growth in 2025 is closest to: (LOS: los-02-02-e)
   - A. -0.78%.
   - B. 0.28%.
   - C. 1.08%.
2. Based on Exhibit 1, the contribution of capital deepening to Pelagia's 2025 labor productivity growth is closest to: (LOS: los-02-02-d)
   - A. 2.12%.
   - B. 3.20%.
   - C. 5.04%.
3. The pattern Kovac describes in her note is best characterized as: (LOS: los-02-02-j)
   - A. absolute convergence.
   - B. club convergence.
   - C. conditional convergence.
4. Consistent with Statement 2, the free trade agreement's most likely effect on Pelagia over the next decade is: (LOS: los-02-02-l)
   - A. capital inflows from Aurelia, matched by a Pelagian trade deficit, with growth temporarily above its steady-state rate.
   - B. capital outflows to Aurelia's deeper financial markets, with Pelagia running a trade surplus.
   - C. a permanently higher steady-state growth rate driven by selection, scale, and backwardness effects.
5. Under the neoclassical framework, as capital flows into Pelagia after the agreement, Kestrel's finance team should most likely assume that over time Pelagian real wages and the return on capital in Pelagia will: (LOS: los-02-02-l)
   - A. rise and fall, respectively.
   - B. fall and rise, respectively.
   - C. both rise permanently.
6. Brightwater's Statement 1 is best evaluated as: (LOS: los-02-02-f)
   - A. correct, because a country must own natural resources to sustain long-run growth.
   - B. incorrect, because access to resources through trade substitutes for ownership, and scarcity induces substitution and efficiency gains.
   - C. correct, because natural resources enter the growth accounting equation as a separate input subject to diminishing returns.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **C.** Build the 2025 capital stock with $K_t = K_{t-1} + I - D$: $K_{2025} = 820 + 330(0.27 - 0.11) = 820 + 52.80 = \$872.80$ billion, so capital growth is $872.80/820 - 1 = 6.4390\%$.
> TFP is the residual: $\Delta A/A = \Delta Y/Y - \alpha\,\Delta K/K - (1-\alpha)\,\Delta L/L = 4.6\% - 0.42(6.4390\%) - 0.58(1.4\%) = 4.6\% - 2.7044\% - 0.8120\% = 1.0836\%$.
> A is wrong because it ignores depreciation and adds gross investment: $K = 820 + 330(0.27) = \$909.10$ billion, capital growth $10.8659\%$, and TFP $4.6\% - 0.42(10.8659\%) - 0.58(1.4\%) = -0.7757\%$.
> B is wrong because it swaps the weights: $4.6\% - 0.58(6.4390\%) - 0.42(1.4\%) = 0.2774\%$.
> Population growth (1.9%) is a decoy; labor input is hours worked.
> 2. **A.** Capital per worker grew by $\Delta k/k = \Delta K/K - \Delta L/L = 6.4390\% - 1.4\% = 5.0390\%$, and its contribution to labor productivity growth is $\alpha\,\Delta k/k = 0.42(5.0390\%) = 2.1164\%$.
> Check: labor productivity growth is $4.6\% - 1.4\% = 3.2000\%$, and $3.2000\% - 1.0836\% = 2.1164\%$, so TFP explains about one-third and capital deepening about two-thirds of productivity growth; Brightwater's claim is wrong.
> B is wrong because it reports total labor productivity growth, which also includes TFP.
> C is wrong because it reports unweighted growth in capital per worker and omits the elasticity $\alpha$.
> 3. **B.** Annual per capita growth over 2005-2025 is $(9{,}800/4{,}200)^{1/20} - 1 = 4.3275\%$ for Pelagia, $(5{,}900/5{,}100)^{1/20} - 1 = 0.7312\%$ for Sardova, and $(58{,}500/48{,}000)^{1/20} - 1 = 0.9940\%$ for Aurelia.
> Pelagia is closing the gap while Sardova, which grows more slowly than the benchmark, is falling further behind; convergence depends on joining the club through institutional and trade reform.
> A is wrong because absolute convergence would have all poor countries, Sardova included, catching up regardless of their characteristics.
> C is wrong because conditional convergence conditions on the saving rate, population growth, and production function, and Kovac attributes the divergence to institutions and trade orientation despite similar saving rates.
> 4. **A.** In the neoclassical open-economy model, capital flows from the capital-rich country to the capital-poor country, where its marginal product is higher; Pelagia finances the extra investment by running a trade deficit and grows above its steady-state rate during the transition, with no permanent change in the growth rate.
> B is wrong because it reverses the direction of capital flows predicted by differences in the marginal product of capital.
> C is wrong because a permanent growth-rate increase through selection, scale, and backwardness effects is the endogenous growth prediction, which Statement 2 explicitly sets aside.
> 5. **A.** Capital inflows raise Pelagia's capital-to-labor ratio, which raises the marginal product of labor (real wages) and lowers the marginal product of capital (the return on capital) until returns equalize; the plant's target return should reflect a declining return on capital and its wage assumptions should include real wage growth.
> B is wrong because it reverses the marginal-product effects of rising capital per worker.
> C is wrong because diminishing returns to capital mean the return on capital cannot rise as capital deepens, and neoclassical effects are transitional rather than permanent.
> 6. **B.** Natural resources are neither necessary nor sufficient for growth: countries without them obtain access through trade, and rising resource prices induce substitution, new technology, and more efficient use, so limited resources are unlikely to constrain long-run growth.
> A is wrong because resource-poor economies have grown rapidly by importing resources.
> C is wrong because natural resources are not specified separately in the two-factor growth accounting equation; their contribution is subsumed in the TFP residual.

## Distractor design

1. A omits depreciation (gross instead of net investment) when building the capital stock; B swaps the capital and labor weights.
2. B uses total labor productivity growth, including TFP; C omits the output elasticity $\alpha$ from capital-per-worker growth.
3. A ignores that Sardova is not catching up; C confuses conditioning on institutions (club) with conditioning on saving, population growth, and technology (conditional).
4. B reverses the direction of capital flows; C attributes the endogenous permanent-growth prediction to the neoclassical model.
5. B reverses the marginal-product effects of capital deepening; C treats transitional effects as permanent and ignores diminishing returns.
6. A treats resource ownership as necessary; C misreads the growth accounting equation by adding natural resources as a separate input.
