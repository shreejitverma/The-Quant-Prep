# Brightwater Pension Currency Risk Screen

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions (or 12 for 4). Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Brightwater Municipal Pension Plan holds a sleeve of emerging market local-currency sovereign bonds.
Its investment policy statement requires the plan to move to an underweight in any country that the investment committee formally flags for currency crisis risk, and the compliance officer, Grace Tan, must confirm that every flag is supported by the documented warning signs of a currency crisis.
Macro analyst Daniel Okafor prepares the country screens, and chief investment officer Helena Ruiz presents them to the committee.
This quarter's screen covers two emerging markets, Country A and Country B, together with a developed market, Country D, whose bonds the plan uses as a funding benchmark.

Exhibit 1: Country Screen (two years ago and latest)

| Indicator | Country A | Country B |
|---|---|---|
| FX reserves (USD billions) | 62 to 41 | 38 to 47 |
| Broad money (M2) to FX reserves | 3.1 to 4.6 | 2.8 to 2.5 |
| Real exchange rate versus tranquil-period mean | +18% | -4% |
| Exchange rate regime | Crawling peg | Free float |
| Terms of trade, two-year change | -9% | +3% |
| CPI inflation, latest | 11.2% | 3.9% |
| Current account balance (% of GDP) | -6.1% | +1.2% |
| External debt (% of GDP) | 48% to 57% | 31% to 29% |
| Capital mobility | Low (extensive controls) | High |
| Average daily FX turnover in the currency (USD billions) | 2.1 | 5.5 |

Country D has a floating currency, high capital mobility, FX reserves of USD 40 billion, and average daily turnover in its currency of USD 180 billion.
Its currency trades at 1.2500 units of Country D currency per USD.

Okafor's notes on Country A describe a persistent current account deficit financed largely by foreign purchases of short-term government bills.
He writes that the deficit will correct within a year because import demand will respond immediately to any currency weakness, and that the rise in external debt is irrelevant for the exchange rate as long as foreign investors continue to buy the bills.
Country A's new government has also announced a large subsidy program financed by borrowing, while its central bank has raised its policy rate by 300 basis points to contain inflation.

For Country D, Ruiz asks Okafor to model an unexpected 8% increase in the money supply, noting that goods prices in Country D adjust only slowly while financial markets reprice immediately.

Both Country A and Country D have intervened in their foreign exchange markets this year.
Tan asks Okafor which central bank's intervention is more likely to be effective, and on what basis.
Separately, Country B is receiving a surge of portfolio inflows driven by low interest rates in industrial countries, and its finance ministry has proposed a tax on short-term foreign portfolio inflows.

Finally, Okafor proposes that the committee flag Country B rather than Country A.
His draft states: "Country B should be flagged because its FX reserves have grown by about 24% in two years and its real exchange rate is now 4% below its tranquil-period mean, both of which historically precede currency crises."
Tan must decide whether the proposed flag meets the policy statement's requirement before the committee meets.

## Questions

1. The mechanism by which the rise in Country A's external debt ratio most likely affects its currency is the: (LOS: los-02-01-j)
   - A. debt sustainability channel, through which markets revise the currency's long-run equilibrium value downward as external debt to GDP rises.
   - B. portfolio balance channel, through which Country A's residents accumulate foreign assets and later rebalance toward domestic assets, supporting the currency.
   - C. flow supply and demand channel, through which an immediate response of import demand restores balance with no lasting effect on the currency.
2. Under the Mundell-Fleming model, the pressure that Country A's policy mix places on its currency is most likely: (LOS: los-02-01-k)
   - A. upward.
   - B. downward.
   - C. indeterminate.
3. Given Ruiz's assumptions, the Country D currency per USD rate following the money supply increase will most likely: (LOS: los-02-01-k)
   - A. rise immediately to about 1.3500 and remain there.
   - B. rise above 1.3500, then decline toward 1.3500 as prices adjust.
   - C. fall toward about 1.1574 because the currency appreciates.
4. The best response to Tan's question about intervention is that intervention by: (LOS: los-02-01-l)
   - A. Country A is more likely to be effective, because its reserves cover about 19.5 days of average turnover in its currency versus about 0.2 day for Country D.
   - B. Country D is more likely to be effective, because the depth of a USD 180 billion daily market lets its central bank trade large amounts without moving prices.
   - C. the two countries is about equally likely to be effective, because their reserve holdings of USD 41 billion and USD 40 billion are nearly identical.
5. The objectives of Country B's proposed tax on short-term portfolio inflows most likely include: (LOS: los-02-01-l)
   - A. preventing excessive currency appreciation and preserving an independent monetary policy.
   - B. attracting additional short-term foreign-currency inflows to rebuild FX reserves.
   - C. correcting a real exchange rate that is 4% below its tranquil-period mean by weakening the currency further.
6. Tan's most appropriate conclusion about Okafor's proposed flag on Country B is that it is: (LOS: los-02-01-m)
   - A. supported, because both cited indicators are documented warning signs of a currency crisis.
   - B. partially supported, because rising reserves are a warning sign but a currency below its mean is not.
   - C. not supported, because both cited indicators are the opposite of the patterns that typically precede a currency crisis.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **A.** Country A's external debt ratio rose by $57\% - 48\% = 9$ percentage points (an 18.75% relative increase) because persistent current account deficits are financed by capital inflows.
>    Under the debt sustainability channel, markets revise the long-run equilibrium value of the deficit country's currency downward as its external debt to GDP ratio climbs, so the rise is not irrelevant even while financing continues.
>    B is wrong because it is the surplus country, not the deficit country, that accumulates claims on the other country and may later rebalance away from them.
>    C is wrong because exchange rate pass-through to traded-goods prices is incomplete and import and export demand respond with long lags, so flow adjustment is slow, not immediate.
> 2. **C.** Country A has low capital mobility, so the policy effect runs through the trade balance rather than capital flows.
>    Expansionary fiscal policy raises aggregate demand and imports (downward pressure), while restrictive monetary policy lowers aggregate demand and imports (upward pressure); under low mobility this mixed combination is indeterminate.
>    The crawling peg governs how much of any pressure reaches the quoted rate and does not change the sign of the pressure itself.
>    A is wrong because it applies the high-mobility result, in which restrictive monetary plus expansionary fiscal policy raises real rates, attracts capital, and appreciates the currency.
>    B is wrong because downward pressure under low mobility requires both policies to be expansionary.
> 3. **B.** Under the monetary approach, an 8% money supply increase eventually raises the price level by 8% and, through PPP, depreciates the currency proportionally: $1.2500 \times 1.08 = 1.3500$ units per USD.
>    With sticky goods prices (the Dornbusch model), the easing first lowers domestic interest rates, capital flows out, and the currency depreciates beyond its new long-run level, so the rate rises above 1.3500 before moving back toward it as prices adjust.
>    A is wrong because it describes the flexible-price monetary model, which ignores the price stickiness Ruiz specifies.
>    C is wrong because it divides by 1.08 ($1.2500/1.08 = 1.1574$), treating the easing as an appreciation and reading the quote in the wrong direction.
> 4. **A.** Effectiveness depends on reserves relative to turnover in the currency: Country A $= 41/2.1 = 19.52$ days of turnover; Country D $= 40/180 = 0.2222$ day.
>    Emerging market central banks hold reserves that are sizable relative to turnover and can influence the level and path of the exchange rate, whereas developed market reserves are negligible relative to turnover.
>    B is wrong because deep, high-turnover markets make a given amount of intervention less effective, not more.
>    C is wrong because it compares reserve levels without scaling them by daily turnover.
> 5. **A.** Capital controls and intervention aim to prevent excessive currency appreciation, reduce the volume of inflows, and preserve an independent monetary policy, which fits a country facing a surge of push-driven portfolio inflows.
>    B is wrong because the tax discourages, rather than attracts, short-term inflows, and heavy short-term foreign-currency inflows are themselves a crisis warning sign.
>    C is wrong because the tax is meant to resist appreciation pressure from inflows, not to push an already below-mean currency weaker.
> 6. **C.** Country B's reserves rose by $47/38 - 1 = 23.68\% \approx 24\%$, but FX reserves typically decline precipitously before a crisis, so rising reserves argue against a flag.
>    Before a crisis the real exchange rate is typically substantially above its tranquil-period mean, so a currency 4% below its mean is also the opposite of a warning sign.
>    A is wrong because it inverts both documented patterns.
>    B is wrong because it inverts the reserves pattern; Country A, not Country B, shows falling reserves (down $41/62 - 1 = -33.87\%$), a rising M2 to reserves ratio, and a currency 18% above its mean.

## Distractor design

- Q1: B assigns the surplus country's asset accumulation to the deficit country (wrong channel); C assumes immediate trade adjustment (ignores pass-through and lags).
- Q2: A applies the high-mobility table to a low-mobility country (misread exhibit); B uses the twin-expansion cell (wrong cell).
- Q3: A uses the flexible-price monetary model (wrong model variant); C divides instead of multiplies (quote direction and sign).
- Q4: B reverses the effect of market depth (sign); C compares reserve levels instead of reserves relative to turnover (wrong scaling).
- Q5: B reverses the purpose of an inflow tax (sign); C misreads the valuation row of the exhibit and the direction of the policy goal.
- Q6: A inverts both warning signs; B inverts only the reserves pattern.
