# Corvina Beverages: A Full Picture of Leverage for a Term Loan

> [!info] Timer
> 12 minutes for 4 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Paula Ibarra, CFA, is a senior credit officer at a regional commercial bank in Santiago.
Corvina Beverages SA, a Chilean bottler of soft drinks and juices that reports under IFRS in US dollars, has applied for a five-year USD400 million term loan.
Ibarra's credit committee asks her for a comprehensive picture of Corvina's financial leverage and liquidity before it votes.

Corvina operates 14 bottling plants and pays a dividend every year.
In 2024 and 2025 Corvina issued bonds and used much of the proceeds to transfer part of its defined benefit pension obligation to an insurer through an annuity buyout, which is the main reason employee benefit and other long-term liabilities declined.

Exhibit 1: Corvina Beverages SA, balance sheet data at 31 December (USD millions)

| Item | 2025 | 2024 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash and equivalents | 410 | 380 | 350 |
| Trade receivables | 620 | 580 | 540 |
| Inventories | 610 | 500 | 470 |
| Other current assets | 160 | 140 | 140 |
| Total assets | 6,800 | 6,300 | 5,900 |
| Trade payables | 760 | 690 | 610 |
| Current financial debt | 310 | 240 | 180 |
| Other current liabilities | 280 | 270 | 290 |
| Long-term financial debt | 1,640 | 1,280 | 900 |
| Employee benefit and other long-term liabilities | 390 | 570 | 820 |
| Total equity | 3,420 | 3,250 | 3,100 |

Exhibit 2: Corvina Beverages SA, selected income and cash flow data (USD millions)

| Item | 2025 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 5,480 | 5,100 |
| Cost of sales | 3,290 | 3,080 |
| Operating profit (EBIT) | 690 | 650 |
| Cash generated from operations | 980 | 930 |
| Interest paid | 96 | 70 |
| Income taxes paid | 150 | 140 |
| Capital expenditure | 360 | 340 |
| Expenditure on intangible assets | 60 | 50 |
| Dividends paid | 200 | 190 |

Cash generated from operations is stated before interest and income taxes paid.
Ibarra defines total financial debt as current financial debt plus long-term financial debt, and reinvestment as capital expenditure plus expenditure on intangible assets.
She computes working capital ratios on average balances and a 365-day year, and measures days payables against purchases.

Ibarra's colleague, Mateo Fuentes, has already run a DuPont analysis.
He tells her: "Average total assets over average total equity was 1.92 in 2024 and 1.96 in 2025, and the current ratio held at 1.33, so Corvina's balance sheet risk has barely changed and the new loan adds little risk to the bank."

Ibarra replies that the DuPont leverage ratio says nothing about what kind of obligations finance the assets.
She decides to common-size Corvina's long-term capital, defined as long-term financial debt plus employee benefit and other long-term liabilities plus total equity, and to test how quickly cash flow could retire debt.
She also wants to know whether Corvina's working capital cycle is a source or a use of liquidity.

## Questions

1. Long-term financial debt as a percentage of Corvina's long-term capital at 31 December 2025 is closest to: (LOS: los-03-06-e)
   - A. 24.1%.
   - B. 30.1%.
   - C. 33.9%.
2. The number of years Corvina would need to repay its total financial debt at 31 December 2025 from 2025 cash generated from operations after reinvestment is closest to: (LOS: los-03-06-e)
   - A. 2.93.
   - B. 3.15.
   - C. 3.48.
3. Corvina's 2025 cash conversion cycle is closest to: (LOS: los-03-06-e)
   - A. 21.1 days.
   - B. 23.7 days.
   - C. 27.4 days.
4. Ibarra's most appropriate conclusion for the credit committee about Corvina's financial leverage from 2023 to 2025 is that financial risk: (LOS: los-03-06-a)
   - A. is essentially unchanged, because the DuPont leverage ratio and the current ratio are stable.
   - B. has decreased, because employee benefit and other long-term liabilities fell by more than half.
   - C. has increased, because interest-bearing debt has replaced less onerous long-term obligations and cash flow coverage of debt has weakened.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Long-term capital at 31 December 2025 $= 1{,}640 + 390 + 3{,}420 = 5{,}450$.
> Long-term financial debt share $= 1{,}640 / 5{,}450 = 0.3009 = 30.1\%$.
> The same share was $1{,}280 / 5{,}100 = 25.10\%$ in 2024 and $900 / 4{,}820 = 18.67\%$ in 2023.
> A is wrong because it divides by total assets rather than long-term capital: $1{,}640 / 6{,}800 = 0.2412$.
> C is wrong because it adds current financial debt to both numerator and denominator: $(1{,}640 + 310) / (5{,}450 + 310) = 1{,}950 / 5{,}760 = 0.3385$, although the long-term capital structure excludes current liabilities.
> 2. **C.** Total financial debt $= 310 + 1{,}640 = 1{,}950$; reinvestment $= 360 + 60 = 420$.
> Cash after reinvestment $= 980 - 420 = 560$.
> Years to repay $= 1{,}950 / 560 = 3.4821$, against $1{,}520 / (930 - 390) = 2.8148$ in 2024.
> A is wrong because it omits current financial debt: $1{,}640 / 560 = 2.9286$.
> B is wrong because it deducts capital expenditure but not expenditure on intangible assets: $1{,}950 / (980 - 360) = 1{,}950 / 620 = 3.1452$.
> 3. **B.** Average receivables $= (620 + 580) / 2 = 600$; DSO $= 600 / 5{,}480 \times 365 = 39.9635$ days.
> Average inventory $= (610 + 500) / 2 = 555$; DOH $= 555 / 3{,}290 \times 365 = 61.5729$ days.
> Purchases $= 3{,}290 + 610 - 500 = 3{,}400$; average payables $= (760 + 690) / 2 = 725$; days payables $= 725 / 3{,}400 \times 365 = 77.8309$ days.
> Cash conversion cycle $= 39.9635 + 61.5729 - 77.8309 = 23.7056$ days, up from $21.2679$ days in 2024, so working capital is a modest and growing use of liquidity driven by the inventory build.
> A is wrong because it measures days payables against cost of sales, $725 / 3{,}290 \times 365 = 80.4331$, giving $39.9635 + 61.5729 - 80.4331 = 21.1033$.
> C is wrong because it uses year-end rather than average balances: $41.2956 + 67.6748 - 81.5882 = 27.3822$.
> 4. **C.** The DuPont leverage ratio moved only from $6{,}100 / 3{,}175 = 1.9213$ to $6{,}550 / 3{,}335 = 1.9640$, but long-term financial debt rose from $18.67\%$ to $30.09\%$ of long-term capital while employee benefit and other long-term liabilities fell from $17.01\%$ to $7.16\%$.
> Bond debt carries fixed maturities and interest and is more onerous than the pension obligation it replaced, so the mix of obligations became riskier within an almost unchanged asset-to-equity multiple.
> Cash flow confirms it: cash generated from operations to total debt fell from $930 / 1{,}520 = 61.18\%$ to $980 / 1{,}950 = 50.26\%$, years to repay rose from 2.81 to 3.48, and cash interest coverage fell from $930 / 70 = 13.29$ to $980 / 96 = 10.21$.
> A is wrong because it relies on the single leverage ratio and the current ratio, which cannot show a change in the nature of financing.
> B is wrong because the fall in employee benefit liabilities was funded by issuing bonds, so it is a substitution of a more onerous obligation, not a reduction in leverage.

## Distractor design

1. A: total assets used as the denominator instead of long-term capital (wrong formula variant); C: current financial debt included in long-term capital (misread exhibit).
2. A: current financial debt left out of total debt (misread exhibit); B: expenditure on intangible assets left out of reinvestment (wrong formula variant).
3. A: days payables on cost of sales instead of purchases (wrong formula variant); C: year-end instead of average balances (period error).
4. A: single DuPont leverage ratio read as the whole capital structure; B: fall in pension liabilities read as deleveraging, ignoring that bonds funded it.
