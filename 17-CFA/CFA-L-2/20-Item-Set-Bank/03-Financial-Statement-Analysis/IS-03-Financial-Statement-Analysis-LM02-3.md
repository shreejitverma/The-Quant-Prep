# Velmora Cloud Forecast and Valuation

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions (or 12 for 4). Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Priya Raman, CFA, is a growth equity analyst at Castlegate Partners.
She is building a financial statement model and DCF valuation for Velmora Cloud plc, a UK software-as-a-service company that reports under IFRS.
Velmora pays most of its engineers partly in RSUs, reported a net loss in 2025, and has not yet paid a dividend.

**Exhibit 1: Velmora selected 2025 data (GBP millions unless noted)**

| Item | 2025 |
|---|---|
| Revenue | 1,240.0 |
| Share-based compensation expense | 223.2 |
| Cash flow from operating activities | 310.0 |
| Capital expenditure | 95.0 |
| Net loss | (61.0) |
| Weighted average basic and diluted shares (millions) | 406.5 |
| Shares outstanding at 31 December (millions) | 410.0 |
| Share price at 31 December (GBP) | 62.00 |

The share-based compensation note lists 14.2 million unvested RSUs and 6.8 million options with a weighted average strike of GBP 40.00 as excluded from diluted shares because their effect would be anti-dilutive.

Raman's 2026 forecast assumes revenue growth of 30%.
Because share-based compensation should fall as a share of revenue as Velmora matures, she assumes it will be 15% of revenue in 2026, down from 18% in 2025.
She assumes that all 2026 share-based compensation relates to RSUs that vest and settle in 2026, with a weighted average grant-date fair value of GBP 31.00 per RSU.
She also forecasts 1.5 million shares issued from option exercises and 4.0 million shares repurchased in 2026.

Velmora acquired a smaller UK rival in 2021 and with it a closed defined benefit plan.
At 31 December 2025 the UK plan had an obligation of GBP 620 million and plan assets of GBP 535 million.
A separate Irish plan, also closed, had plan assets exceeding its obligation by GBP 40 million.

**Exhibit 2: Raman's valuation inputs, 31 December 2025 (GBP millions)**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Enterprise value from DCF (share-based compensation deducted from free cash flow) | 27,900 |
| Financial debt | 1,150 |
| Cash and equivalents | 2,380 |

At a review meeting on 12 March 2026, Raman's associate, Tom Whitcombe, makes two comments.

On free cash flow, he says: "Velmora trades at about 118 times reported free cash flow, which looks expensive, but that multiple is the right comparison with our cash-paying peers because share-based compensation is non-cash."

On the share count, he says: "Since you already deducted share-based compensation from free cash flow, we should also raise the share count by the expected future dilution from awards not yet granted, to be conservative."

## Questions

1. Raman's forecast of Velmora's 2026 share-based compensation expense is closest to: (LOS: los-03-02-c)
   - A. GBP 186.0 million.
   - B. GBP 241.8 million.
   - C. GBP 290.2 million.
2. Raman's forecast of Velmora's basic shares outstanding at 31 December 2026 is closest to: (LOS: los-03-02-c)
   - A. 411.4 million.
   - B. 415.3 million.
   - C. 419.3 million.
3. Velmora's 2025 free cash flow margin after deducting share-based compensation is closest to: (LOS: los-03-02-c)
   - A. -0.7%.
   - B. 17.3%.
   - C. 35.3%.
4. Which statement about Velmora's reported 2025 diluted share count is most accurate? (LOS: los-03-02-b)
   - A. It equals basic shares because every award is anti-dilutive when a company reports a net loss, so Raman should add the 21.0 million disclosed awards for valuation.
   - B. It should include the in-the-money options under the treasury stock method, so the reported figure is understated by an accounting error.
   - C. It equals basic shares because the RSUs and options were granted at prices above the current share price.
5. The equity value Raman should derive from Exhibit 2 and the pension information is closest to: (LOS: los-03-02-e)
   - A. GBP 28,510 million.
   - B. GBP 29,045 million.
   - C. GBP 29,085 million.
6. Regarding Whitcombe's two comments, which is most likely correct? (LOS: los-03-02-c)
   - A. The free cash flow comment only.
   - B. The share count comment only.
   - C. Neither comment.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** 2026 revenue $= 1{,}240.0 \times 1.30 = 1{,}612.0$; share-based compensation $= 15\% \times 1{,}612.0 = 241.8$.
> A is wrong because it applies the 15% ratio to 2025 revenue: $0.15 \times 1{,}240.0 = 186.0$.
> C is wrong because it keeps the historical 18% ratio: $0.18 \times 1{,}612.0 = 290.16$, ignoring Raman's maturity-based decline.
> 2. **B.** Shares issued from RSU vesting $=$ expense recognised at vesting $/$ grant-date fair value $= 241.8 / 31.00 = 7.8000$ million.
> Ending basic shares $= 410.0 + 7.8 + 1.5 - 4.0 = 415.3$ million.
> A is wrong because it divides by the GBP 62.00 market price: $241.8 / 62.00 = 3.9000$ million, giving $410.0 + 3.9 + 1.5 - 4.0 = 411.4$.
> C is wrong because it forgets the repurchases: $410.0 + 7.8 + 1.5 = 419.3$.
> 3. **A.** Reported free cash flow $= 310.0 - 95.0 = 215.0$; after deducting share-based compensation $= 215.0 - 223.2 = -8.2$; margin $= -8.2 / 1{,}240.0 = -0.6613\%$.
> B is wrong because it uses reported free cash flow, which excludes share-based compensation: $215.0 / 1{,}240.0 = 17.3387\%$.
> C is wrong because it adds share-based compensation back a second time, even though CFO already includes the non-cash add-back: $(215.0 + 223.2) / 1{,}240.0 = 35.3387\%$.
> 4. **A.** Diluted EPS may not exceed basic EPS, so a loss-making company reports identical basic and diluted shares whatever awards are outstanding.
> For valuation the analyst adds back the disclosed anti-dilutive securities: $14.2 + 6.8 = 21.0$ million.
> B is wrong because the exclusion is required by the anti-dilution rule, not an error; including the options would reduce the reported loss per share.
> C is wrong because the options are in the money (GBP 40.00 strike against a GBP 62.00 price); the net loss, not moneyness, makes them anti-dilutive.
> 5. **B.** UK plan funded status $= 535 - 620 = -85$, a net liability treated as debt; the Irish surplus of 40 is ignored because plan assets cannot be distributed to shareholders.
> Equity value $= 27{,}900 - 1{,}150 + 2{,}380 - 85 = 29{,}045$.
> A is wrong because it deducts the gross UK obligation and ignores the 535 of plan assets dedicated to paying it: $27{,}900 - 1{,}150 + 2{,}380 - 620 = 28{,}510$.
> C is wrong because it nets the Irish surplus against the UK deficit: $27{,}900 - 1{,}150 + 2{,}380 - 85 + 40 = 29{,}085$.
> 6. **C.** The free cash flow comment is wrong: share-based compensation transfers value from shareholders to employees and is often offset by buybacks, so reported free cash flow multiples flatter heavy users; comparing Velmora's multiple with those of cash-paying peers without deducting share-based compensation is misleading.
> The share count comment is wrong: deducting share-based compensation from free cash flow and inflating the share count for expected dilution are alternative treatments that should give the same answer, so doing both double counts the cost.
> A and B are wrong because each endorses one of the two incorrect comments.

## Distractor design

1. A applies the forecast ratio to the wrong period's revenue (period); C extrapolates the historical ratio (wrong assumption).
2. A divides by the market price instead of grant-date fair value; C omits repurchases from the roll-forward.
3. B uses reported free cash flow that excludes share-based compensation; C double counts the non-cash add-back (sign).
4. B treats the anti-dilution rule as an error; C attributes anti-dilution to moneyness instead of the net loss.
5. A deducts the gross obligation rather than funded status; C nets a surplus against a deficit.
6. A accepts reported free cash flow multiples as comparable; B double counts share-based compensation in both cash flow and share count.
