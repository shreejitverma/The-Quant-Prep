# Carrow Freight Lines Pension Quality Review

> [!info] Timer
> 12 minutes for 4 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Diane Tran, CFA, works in the accounting quality group of Harrow Street Capital, which holds the shares and bonds of Carrow Freight Lines Inc., a US trucking and rail logistics company.
Carrow reports under US GAAP and sponsors a single defined benefit pension plan for its unionised drivers and yard workers.
Tran's portfolio manager has asked her to check how much of Carrow's reported pension cost is driven by management assumptions and to recommend the pension adjustments for the firm's valuation model.

Effective 1 January 2025, Carrow amended the plan benefit formula for past service as part of a new labour agreement.
The amendment increased the obligation by USD 240 million.
The opening obligation in Exhibit 1 is measured immediately after the amendment.
The average remaining service life of the employees affected by the amendment is 12 years.

**Exhibit 1: Carrow pension plan data, 2025 (USD millions unless noted)**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Projected benefit obligation, 1 January 2025 | 6,200 |
| Fair value of plan assets, 1 January 2025 | 5,400 |
| Current service cost | 145 |
| Benefits paid | 330 |
| Employer contributions | 250 |
| Actual return on plan assets | 210 |
| Actuarial loss from lower discount rate at year end | 380 |
| Cumulative unrecognised net actuarial loss in AOCI, 1 January 2025 | 1,020 |
| Average remaining working life of active employees | 14 years |

**Exhibit 2: Carrow pension assumptions**

| Assumption | 2025 | 2026 guidance |
|---|---|---|
| Discount rate (start of year) | 4.5% | 4.1% |
| Expected long-term return on plan assets | 7.0% | 7.5% |
| Rate of compensation increase | 3.0% | 3.0% |

Carrow amortises actuarial gains and losses using the corridor method and past service cost straight-line over the remaining service life of affected employees.
Carrow's market capitalisation is about USD 9.2 billion.

Tran notes that Carrow's chief financial officer told investors on the fourth-quarter call that "raising our expected return assumption to 7.5% for 2026 will reduce pension expense and strengthen the balance sheet."

Tran then drafts her valuation recommendation.

Tran writes: "In our FCFF forecast we should keep 2026 service cost as an operating expense, exclude the interest cost, expected return, and amortisation components, and deduct the year-end net pension liability from enterprise value as debt."

Her colleague, Rafael Mehta, adds: "We should also reduce the net pension liability we deduct from enterprise value to reflect the higher expected return, because the plan assets will now be expected to earn more."

## Questions

1. Carrow's 2025 pension expense recognised in profit or loss under US GAAP is closest to: (LOS: los-03-02-d)
   - A. USD 66.0 million.
   - B. USD 94.6 million.
   - C. USD 138.9 million.
2. If Carrow reported under IFRS instead, its 2025 pension amount recognised in profit or loss would be closest to: (LOS: los-03-02-d)
   - A. USD 181.0 million.
   - B. USD 286.0 million.
   - C. USD 421.0 million.
3. Carrow's net pension liability at 31 December 2025 and its 2025 operating cash outflow for the plan are closest to: (LOS: los-03-02-d)
   - A. USD 1,144 million and USD 250 million.
   - B. USD 1,144 million and USD 330 million.
   - C. USD 976 million and USD 250 million.
4. Regarding the statements by Tran and Mehta, which is most likely correct? (LOS: los-03-02-e)
   - A. Tran's statement only.
   - B. Mehta's statement only.
   - C. Both statements.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Interest cost $= 4.5\% \times 6{,}200 = 279.0$; expected return $= 7.0\% \times 5{,}400 = 378.0$.
> Past service cost amortisation $= 240 / 12 = 20.0$.
> Corridor $= 10\% \times \max(6{,}200, 5{,}400) = 620.0$; excess $= 1{,}020 - 620 = 400.0$; amortisation $= 400.0 / 14 = 28.5714$.
> Expense $= 145.0 + 279.0 - 378.0 + 20.0 + 28.5714 = 94.5714$, or 94.6.
> A is wrong because it omits the corridor amortisation of the net loss: $94.5714 - 28.5714 = 66.0$.
> C is wrong because it amortises the whole unrecognised loss instead of the excess over the corridor: $1{,}020 / 14 = 72.8571$, giving $145.0 + 279.0 - 378.0 + 20.0 + 72.8571 = 138.8571$.
> 2. **C.** Under IFRS, past service cost is recognised immediately in profit or loss as part of service cost, and net interest applies the discount rate to the net liability; there is no expected return and no amortisation.
> Service cost $= 145.0 + 240.0 = 385.0$; net interest $= 4.5\% \times (6{,}200 - 5{,}400) = 36.0$; total $= 421.0$.
> A is wrong because it omits the past service cost, as if it went to OCI first under US GAAP: $145.0 + 36.0 = 181.0$.
> B is wrong because it keeps the US GAAP gross interest cost and expected return: $385.0 + 279.0 - 378.0 = 286.0$.
> 3. **A.** Closing obligation $= 6{,}200 + 145 + 279 - 330 + 380 = 6{,}674$; closing plan assets $= 5{,}400 + 210 + 250 - 330 = 5{,}530$.
> Funded status $= 5{,}530 - 6{,}674 = -1{,}144$, a net pension liability of 1,144.
> The only cash item for the sponsor is the employer contribution of 250, in operating activities.
> B is wrong because benefits paid of 330 come out of plan assets and never appear in the sponsor's cash flow statement.
> C is wrong because it rolls plan assets forward with the expected return instead of the actual return: $5{,}400 + 378 + 250 - 330 = 5{,}698$, giving a funded status of $5{,}698 - 6{,}674 = -976$.
> Cross-check over the period Exhibit 1 covers, which begins immediately after the amendment: total periodic cost $= 250 - (-1{,}144 - (-800)) = 594 = 145 + 279 - 210 + 380$.
> The 240 of past service cost is not in that 594 because it was charged to accumulated other comprehensive income at the 1 January amendment date, before the opening balances in Exhibit 1, and reaches profit or loss only through the 20.0 of annual amortisation in Question 1.
> 4. **A.** Tran's recommendation matches the curriculum: service cost is compensation in lieu of salary and stays in FCFF, the other components relate to the unwinding and financing of a present value that is captured by deducting the net liability, and an underfunded plan is treated as debt.
> Mehta is wrong because the expected return is a US GAAP expense assumption only; it does not enter the obligation or the fair value of plan assets, so it does not change funded status.
> Raising it from 7.0% to 7.5% lowers reported 2026 expense by about $0.5\% \times 5{,}530 = 27.65$ with no economic change, which is an aggressive earnings lever; it does not strengthen the balance sheet as the chief financial officer claimed.
> B and C are wrong because each accepts that an expected return assumption changes the deficit.

## Distractor design

1. A drops the corridor amortisation; C amortises the full unrecognised loss rather than the excess over 10% of the larger of obligation and assets (wrong formula variant).
2. A applies US GAAP past service cost timing under IFRS; B keeps US GAAP gross interest and expected return under IFRS (IFRS versus US GAAP).
3. B treats benefits paid by the plan as sponsor cash (misread exhibit); C uses expected instead of actual return in the asset roll-forward.
4. B and C let the US GAAP expected return assumption move the obligation or funded status.
