# Aurelian Financial Holdings: A Life Insurer and Its Bank

> [!info] Timer
> 12 minutes for 4 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Daniel Ferreira is a portfolio manager at a multi-family office in Lisbon.
A client has asked whether to add shares of Aurelian Financial Holdings, a listed group with two main subsidiaries: Aurelian Life, a life and health (L&H) insurer, and Aurelian Bank, a mid-sized commercial bank.
The group reports in € millions with December year ends.
Ferreira reviews each subsidiary separately before forming a view on the group.

Exhibit 1: Aurelian Life investment portfolio (€ millions)

| Asset class | 31 Dec 2024 | 31 Dec 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Loans and deposits | 4,800 | 5,100 |
| Debt securities | 62,400 | 66,300 |
| Equity securities | 11,000 | 12,500 |
| Total investments | 78,200 | 83,900 |

For 2025, Aurelian Life reported interest income of €3,150 million, dividend income of €385 million, gains on debt securities of €94 million, and gains on equity securities of €410 million.
Following the method his firm uses for insurers, Ferreira measures the fixed-income return using fixed-income income and average fixed-income assets.

Ferreira's junior analyst, Marta Sousa, drafts three observations about Aurelian Life.

Observation 1: "Because life claims track stable mortality experience, Aurelian Life can operate with relatively lower capital requirements than a P&C insurer and can seek higher returns on its investments, but its risk-based capital calculation must incorporate interest rate risk."

Observation 2: "Aurelian Life's liquidity is best assessed with the current ratio, comparing current assets with current policy liabilities."

Observation 3: "The fair value hierarchy is irrelevant to Aurelian Life's liquidity, because all of its investments are carried at fair value."

Exhibit 2: Aurelian Bank investment securities, 31 December 2025 (€ millions)

| Portfolio | Amortised cost | Fair value | Gross unrealised losses | Of which: outstanding 12 months or longer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Held to maturity | 18,200 | 17,640 | 690 | 552 |
| Available for sale | 41,500 | 41,120 | 1,120 | 280 |

Aurelian Bank's CET1 ratio was 13.4% and its liquidity coverage ratio was 142% at year end 2025.
Ferreira also collects the following disclosures from the bank's notes.

Disclosure 1: Aurelian Bank sponsors unconsolidated securitisation vehicles holding €4,200 million of assets, to which it has provided committed liquidity lines.

Disclosure 2: The net obligation of the bank's defined benefit pension plan declined in each of the last three years.

Disclosure 3: The bank manages €38,000 million of client assets in funds that are not on its balance sheet, and fee income from these funds has grown steadily.

## Questions

1. Aurelian Life's 2025 return on average fixed-income assets is closest to: (LOS: los-03-04-f)
   - A. 4.54%.
   - B. 4.68%.
   - C. 5.27%.
2. Which of Sousa's observations about Aurelian Life is most accurate? (LOS: los-03-04-f)
   - A. Observation 1.
   - B. Observation 2.
   - C. Observation 3.
3. Based on Exhibit 2, the proportion of Aurelian Bank's held-to-maturity unrealised losses outstanding for 12 months or longer, and the most appropriate interpretation, is: (LOS: los-03-04-d)
   - A. 46.0%, and the concern is limited because most aged losses sit in available-for-sale securities already carried at fair value.
   - B. 80.0%, and because held-to-maturity securities are carried at amortised cost, the aged losses may signal impairment not yet recognised.
   - C. 80.0%, but the concern is limited because held-to-maturity unrealised losses are already reflected in the carrying amount.
4. Which of the bank disclosures, a factor not captured by the CAMELS components, most likely warrants further investigation as a potential source of loss? (LOS: los-03-04-e)
   - A. Disclosure 1.
   - B. Disclosure 2.
   - C. Disclosure 3.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Fixed-income income $= 3{,}150 + 94 = 3{,}244$, using only gains on debt securities.
> Average fixed-income assets $= [(4{,}800 + 62{,}400) + (5{,}100 + 66{,}300)] / 2 = (67{,}200 + 71{,}400) / 2 = 69{,}300$.
> Return $= 3{,}244 / 69{,}300 = 0.0468 = 4.68\%$.
> A is wrong because it divides by the ending fixed-income balance: $3{,}244 / 71{,}400 = 4.54\%$.
> C is wrong because it adds the gains on equity securities to fixed-income income: $(3{,}150 + 94 + 410) / 69{,}300 = 3{,}654 / 69{,}300 = 5.27\%$.
> 2. **A.** L&H claims are more predictable than P&C claims, so L&H insurers can hold lower capital and seek higher-return investments; because L&H products are long-dated and create material interest rate exposure, L&H risk-based capital incorporates interest rate risk.
> B is wrong because the current ratio is not a relevant liquidity measure for an L&H insurer; its liquidity is judged from the liquidity of its invested assets, for which the fair value hierarchy is a proxy.
> C is wrong because the fair value hierarchy serves as a liquidity proxy: Level 1 assets are the most liquid and Level 3 the least, whatever the measurement basis.
> 3. **B.** HTM losses outstanding 12 months or longer $= 552 / 690 = 0.8000 = 80.0\%$.
> Losses that have persisted beyond a year are less likely to reverse, and HTM securities are carried at amortised cost, so the €560 million shortfall of fair value below cost ($18{,}200 - 17{,}640$) is not in the carrying amount and may hide impairment not yet recognised.
> A is wrong because it pools both portfolios, $(552 + 280) / (690 + 1{,}120) = 832 / 1{,}810 = 46.0\%$, and most aged losses are in fact in HTM (552 against 280).
> C is wrong because HTM securities are carried at amortised cost, so their unrealised losses are not reflected in the carrying amount.
> 4. **A.** Unconsolidated securitisation vehicles are off-balance-sheet items that CAMELS does not capture, and committed liquidity lines mean the bank could be required to fund or support €4,200 million of assets under stress.
> B is wrong because a steadily declining net benefit plan obligation reduces the risk of unplanned cash contributions and is favourable.
> C is wrong because client assets under management are off-balance-sheet but here generate a growing, sustainable fee stream, so they are not the most likely source of loss.

## Distractor design

1. A: ending instead of average fixed-income assets (period); C: equity gains included in fixed-income income (misread exhibit).
2. B: current ratio applied to an L&H insurer (wrong metric); C: fair value hierarchy dismissed as a liquidity proxy (measurement basis confused with liquidity).
3. A: HTM and AFS losses pooled (wrong denominator and numerator); C: amortised cost treated as fair value (accounting category error).
4. B: a favourable off-balance-sheet trend read as unfavourable (direction error); C: fee-generating assets under management read as a loss exposure (off-balance-sheet items treated as uniformly risky).
