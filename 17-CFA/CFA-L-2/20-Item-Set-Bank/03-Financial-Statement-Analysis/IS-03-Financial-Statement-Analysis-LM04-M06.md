# Rotating the Financials Sleeve: Saltmarsh General and Corrib Commercial

> [!info] Timer
> 12 minutes for 4 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Chiara Ventimiglia, CFA, manages the financials sleeve of an equity income fund in London.
She is deciding whether to reduce the fund's holding in Saltmarsh General Insurance, a property and casualty (P&C) insurer, and add to Corrib Commercial Bank.
Both companies report in £ millions and have December year ends.

Saltmarsh's statutory filing gives the underwriting data below, and Ventimiglia computes losses on the statutory basis.

Exhibit 1: Saltmarsh General Insurance, 2025 (£ millions)

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Claims paid during the year | 3,420 |
| Loss reserves, beginning of year | 6,850 |
| Loss reserves, end of year | 7,090 |
| Loss adjustment expenses | 410 |
| Underwriting expenses | 1,490 |
| Net premiums earned | 5,300 |
| Net premiums written | 5,520 |
| Net investment income | 610 |
| Dividends paid to shareholders | 280 |

Exhibit 2: Industry-wide combined ratio for the domestic P&C market

| Year | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Combined ratio | 106.2% | 101.5% | 96.8% | 92.4% | 90.9% |

Ventimiglia's colleague Owen Pryce comments on Exhibit 2.
He says that several new insurers and specialist capital providers have obtained licences over the past year to write commercial property and motor cover.

Ventimiglia next reviews Corrib's market-risk disclosures.
Corrib's trading desk makes markets in government bonds and interest rate swaps.

Exhibit 3: Corrib Commercial Bank trading data (£ millions)

| Item | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Annual trading revenue | 412 | 468 |
| Average daily trading VaR (99%, one day) | 5.2 | 4.8 |
| Year-end trading VaR (99%, one day) | 4.9 | 5.6 |

A broker report states that a peer bank earned annual trading revenue of 110 times its average daily trading VaR in 2025.

Pryce, who is new to bank analysis, has drafted three statements about the CAMELS approach for the fund's research file.

Statement 1: "Because capital adequacy is the first letter in CAMELS, it carries the greatest weight in a bank's composite rating."

Statement 2: "Two examiners who assign a bank identical ratings on all six components could still reach different composite ratings."

Statement 3: "A liquidity component rating of 5 indicates the strongest possible liquidity position."

Ventimiglia notes that CAMELS ratings run from 1 to 5 for each component and for the composite.

## Questions

1. Saltmarsh's 2025 combined ratio, and its underwriting result, is closest to: (LOS: los-03-04-f)
   - A. 99.3%, an underwriting profit.
   - B. 103.8%, an underwriting loss.
   - C. 104.9%, an underwriting loss.
2. Based on Exhibit 2 and Pryce's comment, over the next few years the domestic P&C market is most likely to experience: (LOS: los-03-04-f)
   - A. rising combined ratios, as new entrants attracted by underwriting profits compete on price.
   - B. further declines in combined ratios, as unprofitable insurers exit the market.
   - C. rising premium rates, as a shortage of underwriting capacity develops.
3. Based on Exhibit 3, Corrib's trading revenue per unit of risk in 2025 most likely indicates: (LOS: los-03-04-d)
   - A. a ratio of 83.6 times, a slight deterioration from 2024.
   - B. a ratio of 97.5 times, weaker risk-adjusted trading performance than the peer bank.
   - C. a ratio of 97.5 times, an improvement from 79.2 times in 2024.
4. Which of Pryce's statements about the CAMELS approach is correct? (LOS: los-03-04-c)
   - A. Statement 1.
   - B. Statement 3.
   - C. Statement 2.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Statutory losses $= \text{claims paid} + (\text{ending reserves} - \text{beginning reserves}) = 3{,}420 + (7{,}090 - 6{,}850) = 3{,}660$.
> Loss and LAE ratio $= (3{,}660 + 410) / 5{,}300 = 4{,}070 / 5{,}300 = 0.7679 = 76.79\%$, over net premiums earned.
> Underwriting expense ratio $= 1{,}490 / 5{,}520 = 0.2699 = 26.99\%$, over net premiums written.
> Combined ratio $= 76.79\% + 26.99\% = 103.79\%$, above 100%, so an underwriting loss.
> A is wrong because it uses claims paid instead of accrual losses, ignoring the 240 increase in reserves: $(3{,}420 + 410) / 5{,}300 = 72.26\%$, and $72.26\% + 26.99\% = 99.25\%$.
> C is wrong because it divides underwriting expenses by net premiums earned: $1{,}490 / 5{,}300 = 28.11\%$, and $76.79\% + 28.11\% = 104.91\%$.
> Net investment income and dividends to shareholders are not part of the combined ratio.
> 2. **A.** The industry combined ratio has fallen from 106.2% to 90.9%, so the market is in a hard phase with underwriting profits.
> In the P&C cycle, such profits attract new entrants, as Pryce observes, and the added capacity and price competition lower premium rates, so combined ratios rise towards a soft market.
> B is wrong because exits follow a soft market with high combined ratios, not a period of strong underwriting profits.
> C is wrong because new capacity is entering, not leaving; rising rates from scarce capacity describe the move from a soft market into a hard one.
> 3. **C.** Reward to risk $= \text{annual trading revenue} / \text{average daily VaR}$.
> 2025: $468 / 4.8 = 97.50$ times; 2024: $412 / 5.2 = 79.23$ times, an improvement of $97.50 / 79.23 - 1 = 23.1\%$.
> A is wrong because it uses year-end VaR instead of average daily VaR: $468 / 5.6 = 83.57$ times against $412 / 4.9 = 84.08$ times.
> B is wrong because VaR is useful for tracking one bank's trend but not for comparing banks, whose VaR assumptions differ, so the peer's 110 times does not show that Corrib's performance is weaker.
> 4. **C.** Statement 2 is correct: the composite rating is not a simple average; each examiner weights the components by judgment, so identical component ratings can produce different composites unless all six ratings are the same.
> A is wrong because the order of the CAMELS letters does not rank importance; under Basel III, capital and liquidity carry equal weight.
> B is wrong because ratings run from 1 (best) to 5 (worst), so a 5 is the weakest liquidity position.

## Distractor design

1. A: claims paid used instead of statutory losses, omitting the reserve increase (cash versus accrual); C: net premiums earned used as the expense-ratio denominator (earned versus written).
2. B: exit phase of the cycle placed after a profitable hard market (cycle order reversed); C: capacity shortage assumed when capacity is entering (cycle direction reversed).
3. A: year-end VaR used instead of average daily VaR (misread exhibit); B: VaR-based ratio compared across banks (improper cross-company use).
4. A: letter order read as a weighting scheme (framework misconception); B: rating scale inverted (1 is best, 5 is worst).
