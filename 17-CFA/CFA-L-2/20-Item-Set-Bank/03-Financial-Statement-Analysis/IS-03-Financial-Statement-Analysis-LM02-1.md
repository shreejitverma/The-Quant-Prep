# Lindqvist Maskin Pension Note Review

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions (or 12 for 4). Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Mateo Ferreira, CFA, is an equity analyst at Nordkap Kapital covering Lindqvist Maskin AB, a Swedish maker of industrial machinery that reports under IFRS.
Ferreira is updating his model after the release of the FY2025 annual report and begins with the employee compensation notes.

Lindqvist has four compensation arrangements that Ferreira wants to classify correctly:

- Salaried staff in Sweden hired after 2012 are covered by an arrangement under which Lindqvist pays 4.5% of salary into individual accounts managed by an insurer; Lindqvist has no obligation beyond the contribution.
- A legacy Swedish plan (Plan S) and a German plan (Plan G) promise a pension based on final salary and years of service; Plan S was closed to new members in 2012.
- German retirees receive medical coverage for life; Lindqvist pays claims as they arise and holds no assets for this purpose.
- In November 2025 Lindqvist announced a voluntary separation programme at its Gothenburg plant, offering lump sums totalling SEK 95 million to employees who accept redundancy by 31 March 2026.

Exhibit 1 combines Plan S and Plan G.
The retiree medical plan is excluded from Exhibit 1 and shown separately in Exhibit 2.

**Exhibit 1: Defined benefit plans S and G combined, FY2025 (SEK millions)**

| Obligation | Amount | Plan assets | Amount |

| Current service cost | 410.0 | Actual return on plan assets | 1,120.0 |
| Past service cost (plan amendment, Plan G) | 90.0 | Employer contributions | 480.0 |

Ferreira's valuation uses a DCF on free cash flow to the firm.
His enterprise value estimate is SEK 64,500 million, and Lindqvist has SEK 9,800 million of financial debt and SEK 3,100 million of cash.

At a model review on 18 February 2026, Ferreira and his associate, Elin Sandberg, discuss the treatment of the plans.

Ferreira states: "In the free cash flow forecast I will keep service cost as an operating expense and exclude net interest on the defined benefit plans, because the deficit is already deducted in the bridge from enterprise value to equity value."

Sandberg replies: "Agreed, but Lindqvist owns the Plan S surplus of SEK 1,050 million, so that surplus should be added to equity value alongside cash."

## Questions

1. Which of Lindqvist's arrangements, other than Plans S and G, is most likely accounted for as a defined benefit plan with its obligation measured at present value? (LOS: los-03-02-a)
   - A. The 4.5% salary contribution arrangement for Swedish staff.
   - B. The German retiree medical coverage.
   - C. The Gothenburg voluntary separation programme.
2. The amount Lindqvist recognises in operating expenses for Plans S and G in FY2025 is closest to: (LOS: los-03-02-d)
   - A. SEK 410.0 million.
   - B. SEK 500.0 million.
   - C. SEK 554.0 million.
3. The remeasurement of Plans S and G recognised in other comprehensive income for FY2025 is closest to: (LOS: los-03-02-d)
   - A. a gain of SEK 161.6 million.
   - B. a loss of SEK 159.5 million.
   - C. a gain of SEK 770.0 million.
4. Total periodic pension cost (profit or loss plus OCI) for Plans S and G in FY2025 is closest to: (LOS: los-03-02-d)
   - A. SEK 392.4 million.
   - B. SEK 554.0 million.
   - C. SEK 567.6 million.
5. In bridging from enterprise value to equity value, the total post-employment amount Ferreira should deduct is closest to: (LOS: los-03-02-e)
   - A. SEK 1,722.4 million.
   - B. SEK 2,462.4 million.
   - C. SEK 2,772.4 million.
6. Regarding the statements made at the model review, which is most likely correct? (LOS: los-03-02-e)
   - A. Ferreira's statement only.
   - B. Sandberg's statement only.
   - C. Both statements.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Any post-employment arrangement that is not explicitly defined contribution is accounted for as defined benefit, including other post-employment benefits such as retiree medical care, which are usually unfunded pay-as-you-go plans.
> The obligation is measured at the present value of expected future claims, and the full amount is a liability because there are no plan assets.
> A is wrong because the employer's obligation ends with the contribution; the employee bears the investment and actuarial risk, so this is a defined contribution plan expensed as the contribution accrues.
> C is wrong because the separation lump sums are termination benefits, often reported as a discrete restructuring line, not a post-employment plan.
> 2. **B.** Under IFRS both current and past service cost are operating expenses: $410.0 + 90.0 = 500.0$.
> Net interest is presented below operating income with financing costs, and remeasurements go to OCI.
> A is wrong because it omits the past service cost, which is the US GAAP treatment (past service cost goes to OCI first and is amortised).
> C is wrong because it adds net interest of $3.6\% \times (18{,}400 - 16{,}900) = 54.0$, giving the full profit or loss amount $500.0 + 54.0 = 554.0$ instead of the operating component.
> 3. **A.** Interest income on plan assets at the discount rate is $3.6\% \times 16{,}900 = 608.4$.
> Net return on plan assets $= 1{,}120.0 - 608.4 = 511.6$ (gain); less the actuarial loss of 350.0 gives a remeasurement gain of $511.6 - 350.0 = 161.6$.
> B is wrong because it deducts the expected return $5.5\% \times 16{,}900 = 929.5$, a US GAAP concept that does not exist under IFRS: $1{,}120.0 - 929.5 - 350.0 = -159.5$.
> C is wrong because it forgets to strip out the interest income already included in net interest: $1{,}120.0 - 350.0 = 770.0$.
> 4. **A.** Opening funded status $= 16{,}900.0 - 18{,}400.0 = -1{,}500.0$; closing $= 17{,}780.0 - 19{,}192.4 = -1{,}412.4$; change $= +87.6$.
> Total periodic cost $=$ contributions $-$ change in funded status $= 480.0 - 87.6 = 392.4$.
> Cross-check: profit or loss $554.0$ less the OCI gain $161.6$ gives $392.4$.
> B is wrong because it stops at the profit or loss amount and ignores the OCI remeasurement.
> C is wrong because it adds the change in funded status to contributions ($480.0 + 87.6 = 567.6$), a sign error.
> 5. **C.** Underfunded plans are treated as debt and deducted in full; surpluses are ignored and plans are never netted.
> Deduction $= 2{,}462.4 + 310.0 = 2{,}772.4$.
> A is wrong because it nets the Plan S surplus against the deficits ($2{,}772.4 - 1{,}050.0 = 1{,}722.4$), which effectively credits shareholders with plan assets they cannot receive.
> B is wrong because it deducts only Plan G and omits the unfunded retiree medical liability, which is a post-employment obligation the company must pay.
> 6. **A.** Ferreira is correct: service cost is compensation in lieu of salary and stays in free cash flow even though it is non-cash, while net interest is the unwinding of a present value that the bridge already captures by deducting the net liability, so including it double counts.
> Sandberg is incorrect: plan assets belong to the plan, are legally isolated from the sponsor, and cannot be paid out to shareholders, so a surplus is ignored in valuation.
> B and C are wrong because each accepts Sandberg's claim that the surplus adds to equity value.

## Distractor design

1. A confuses a retirement-purpose arrangement with defined benefit risk-bearing; C confuses a termination benefit with a post-employment benefit.
2. A applies the US GAAP past service cost treatment (OCI first) to an IFRS reporter; C counts net interest, a financing item, as operating.
3. B uses the expected return instead of the discount rate (US GAAP versus IFRS); C omits the interest income on assets already in net interest.
4. B reports profit or loss only and drops OCI; C adds rather than subtracts the change in funded status (sign).
5. A nets a surplus against deficits (no-netting rule); B omits the unfunded OPEB liability (misread exhibit).
6. B and C treat an irrecoverable plan surplus as a shareholder asset.
