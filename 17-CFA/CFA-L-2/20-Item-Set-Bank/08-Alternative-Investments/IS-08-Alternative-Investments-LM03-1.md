# Harrowgate Logistics REIT: A Pension Plan NAV Review

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Priya Raman, CFA, is a real estate analyst at Northcote Teachers' Pension Plan, a Canadian defined benefit plan that holds warehouse properties directly.
The plan's trustees have asked her whether some of the direct holdings should be replaced with shares of listed equity REITs.
Raman begins with Harrowgate Logistics REIT, a self-managed REIT that owns distribution warehouses near three Canadian ports.
Harrowgate reports under IFRS and has elected the cost model for its investment property.

Harrowgate completed the acquisition of a warehouse portfolio at the midpoint of its most recent fiscal year.
The acquired portfolio generates cash NOI of CAD 14,400 thousand per full year, and only the post-acquisition half is included in last-12-month NOI.
Raman expects cash NOI to grow 2.5% over the next 12 months, and recent comparable warehouse transactions support a capitalization rate of 5.75%.
She estimates Harrowgate's cost of equity at 7.6% using a risk-free rate of 3.4%, a beta of 0.84, and a market risk premium of 5.0%.
Harrowgate's land held for development is carried at cost, which Raman judges to approximate current market value.
Harrowgate shares trade at CAD 31.25.

Exhibit 1: Harrowgate selected data (CAD thousands, except per share)

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Last-12-month net operating income | 412,600 |
| Non-cash (straight-line) rent included in NOI | 9,850 |
| Cash and equivalents | 58,300 |
| Land held for development | 96,000 |
| Accounts receivable | 31,700 |
| Prepaid expenses and other tangible assets | 12,400 |
| Goodwill | 44,000 |
| Deferred financing costs | 6,100 |
| Total debt (market value) | 2,915,000 |
| Accounts payable and other liabilities | 148,500 |
| Deferred tax liability (taxable subsidiary) | 62,000 |
| Shares outstanding (thousands) | 118,400 |

Raman's colleague, Martin Dube, reviews her approach and makes three statements.

Statement 1: "Because Harrowgate uses the cost model, its book value per share most likely understates the value of warehouses it has owned for many years."

Statement 2: "An advantage of NAVPS is that it captures the value Harrowgate's management adds through its development pipeline and capital allocation."

Statement 3: "NAVPS estimates become more reliable when transaction volume in the warehouse market dries up, because appraisers then rely on stable historical values."

Raman also screens Beacon Harbour Trust, a listed company that originates first-lien loans secured by office and industrial buildings and holds them to maturity.
Beacon owns no buildings, distributes more than 90% of its taxable income, and pays no corporate income tax on distributed income.

At the trustee meeting, the chair, Elaine Fortier, summarizes the plan's thinking.
"We like the daily liquidity and the professional management of listed REITs, and we would not need to raise minimum deal sizes to buy them.
Before we sell any warehouses, however, I want to understand what we would be giving up by switching."

## Questions

1. Based on Exhibit 1 and Raman's assumptions, Harrowgate's NAVPS is closest to: (LOS: los-08-03-b)
   - A. CAD 37.52.
   - B. CAD 37.92.
   - C. CAD 38.61.
2. Which of Dube's statements is most likely correct? (LOS: los-08-03-b)
   - A. Statement 1
   - B. Statement 2
   - C. Statement 3
3. Using Raman's forward cash NOI, the capitalization rate implied by Harrowgate's current share price is closest to: (LOS: los-08-03-d)
   - A. 6.21%.
   - B. 6.40%.
   - C. 11.36%.
4. Relative to Raman's NAVPS estimate, Harrowgate's share price and its most appropriate interpretation are best described as: (LOS: los-08-03-d)
   - A. a 16.7% discount; the shares are potentially undervalued, and issuing new equity at this price would dilute NAVPS.
   - B. a 16.7% discount; the shares are potentially overvalued, because the market anticipates declining NOI.
   - C. a 20.1% discount; the shares are potentially undervalued, and issuing new equity at this price would dilute NAVPS.
5. Beacon Harbour Trust is best classified as: (LOS: los-08-03-a)
   - A. an equity REIT.
   - B. a mortgage REIT.
   - C. a real estate operating company.
6. Relative to the plan's direct warehouse holdings, the most likely disadvantage of switching to listed REIT shares that Fortier is asking about is: (LOS: los-08-03-e)
   - A. higher minimum investment requirements.
   - B. lower transparency of financial information.
   - C. loss of control over property-level decisions.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **A.** Cash NOI removes non-cash rent and adds the missing half-year of the acquisition: $412{,}600 - 9{,}850 + 14{,}400/2 = 409{,}950$.
> Forward cash NOI $= 409{,}950 \times 1.025 = 420{,}198.75$.
> Operating real estate $= 420{,}198.75 / 0.0575 = 7{,}307{,}804.3478$.
> Add tangible non-real-estate assets: $7{,}307{,}804.3478 + 58{,}300 + 96{,}000 + 31{,}700 + 12{,}400 = 7{,}506{,}204.3478$; goodwill and deferred financing costs are excluded as soft assets.
> Subtract hard liabilities: $7{,}506{,}204.3478 - 2{,}915{,}000 - 148{,}500 = 4{,}442{,}704.3478$; the deferred tax liability is excluded because it is an accounting provision, not an economic claim.
> NAVPS $= 4{,}442{,}704.3478 / 118{,}400 = 37.5228$, or CAD 37.52.
> B is wrong because it capitalizes unadjusted last-12-month NOI grown one year ($412{,}600 \times 1.025 / 0.0575 = 7{,}355{,}043.4783$), leaving the non-cash rent in and omitting the acquisition adjustment, which gives 37.9218.
> C is wrong because it adds the full-year acquisition NOI of 14,400 instead of the missing half ($417{,}150 \times 1.025 / 0.0575$), double counting the half already in last-12-month NOI and giving 38.6069.
> 2. **A.** Under the cost model, buildings are carried at historical cost less accumulated depreciation, so long-held appreciating properties are understated and book value per share is a poor value measure.
> B is wrong because NAV treats the REIT as a static pool of assets and does not capture management's ability to add or destroy value, which is one reason shares can trade at premiums to NAV.
> C is wrong because NAV becomes more subjective, not more reliable, when few transactions are available to establish cap rates.
> 3. **B.** Run the NAV model backward from the price.
> Equity market capitalization $= 31.25 \times 118{,}400 = 3{,}700{,}000$.
> Implied value of operating real estate $= 3{,}700{,}000 + 2{,}915{,}000 + 148{,}500 - (58{,}300 + 96{,}000 + 31{,}700 + 12{,}400) = 6{,}565{,}100$.
> Implied cap rate $= 420{,}198.75 / 6{,}565{,}100 = 0.064005$, or 6.40%.
> A is wrong because it does not strip out the non-real-estate assets from the denominator ($420{,}198.75 / 6{,}763{,}500 = 6.2127\%$), so part of the price attributed to real estate is really paying for cash, land, and receivables.
> C is wrong because it divides NOI by equity market capitalization alone ($420{,}198.75 / 3{,}700{,}000 = 11.3567\%$), ignoring the debt and other liabilities that also claim the property value.
> 4. **A.** Premium or discount $= 31.25 / 37.5228 - 1 = -0.1672$, a 16.7% discount to NAVPS.
> A discount to NAVPS signals potential undervaluation, and selling new shares below NAVPS would be dilutive, which impedes non-dilutive equity raising for growth.
> B is wrong because it reverses the signal: a discount to NAVPS indicates potential undervaluation, not overvaluation.
> C is wrong because it measures the gap on the wrong base ($37.5228 / 31.25 - 1 = 20.07\%$), expressing NAV's excess over price rather than price relative to NAV.
> 5. **B.** Beacon invests in loans secured by real estate and owns no buildings, and it enjoys REIT tax treatment on distributed income, so it is a mortgage REIT.
> A is wrong because equity REITs own and operate income-producing property.
> C is wrong because a REOC is an ordinary taxable company, and Beacon pays no corporate tax on distributed income.
> 6. **C.** Direct ownership gives the plan control over leasing, capital spending, and disposition decisions, which REIT shareholders delegate to management.
> A is wrong because low minimum investment is an advantage of listed shares, as Fortier herself notes.
> B is wrong because listed REITs are regulated and publish financial statements, so transparency is higher, not lower, than for private holdings.

## Distractor design

1. B: unadjusted LTM NOI capitalized (non-cash rent and acquisition adjustment skipped); C: full-year acquisition NOI added instead of the missing half-year.
2. B: NAV credited with management value it ignores (static asset pool); C: reliability direction reversed for illiquid markets.
3. A: non-real-estate assets not stripped from the implied property value; C: equity market cap alone used as the denominator, debt ignored.
4. B: discount read as overvaluation (sign of the signal reversed); C: gap computed as NAV over price instead of price over NAV (wrong base).
5. A: owner of property confused with lender against property; C: tax status misread (REOC is taxable).
6. A: advantage of public vehicles presented as a disadvantage (minimums); B: transparency direction reversed between public and private.
