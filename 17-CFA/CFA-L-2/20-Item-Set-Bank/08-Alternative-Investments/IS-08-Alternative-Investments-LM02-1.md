# Aldergrove Capital: Underwriting Birchmount Medical Pavilion

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Nadia Okafor, CFA, is a senior analyst at Aldergrove Capital, a taxable single-family office based in Vancouver.
The family's principal, Graham Whitlow, has asked her to underwrite the direct purchase of Birchmount Medical Pavilion, a multi-tenant medical office building in suburban Toronto with 9,000 square metres of rentable space.
All space is leased at an average base rent of CAD48.00 per square metre per year.
Under the standard Birchmount lease, tenants reimburse property taxes and insurance in full, and the owner may recover servicing and repair costs from tenants up to a cap of CAD9.00 per square metre per year.
Property management fees are borne by the owner and are not recoverable.
Because the building has many tenants with staggered expiries, Okafor budgets a vacancy and collection allowance equal to 5% of gross rent, applied to gross rent only.

Exhibit 1: Birchmount first-year operating budget (CAD)

| Line item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Servicing and repairs | 96,000 |
| Property tax | 95,000 |
| Insurance | 35,000 |
| Property management fee | 20,000 |
| Property maintenance allowance | 60,000 |

Exhibit 2: Transaction, financing, and market data

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Asking price | CAD4,150,000 |
| Appraised land value | CAD1,030,000 |
| Building useful life (straight line) | 40 years |
| Aldergrove marginal tax rate | 26.5% |
| Lender maximum loan-to-value | 75% of price |
| Lender minimum debt service coverage | 1.60x |
| Interest-only mortgage rate | 6.5% |
| Required return on similar properties | 9.5% |
| Expected constant NOI growth | 2.0% |
| Replacement cost of the building (broker estimate) | CAD3,600,000 |
| Average cap rate on recent Toronto medical office sales | 7.1% |

Whitlow reviews the budget and asks Okafor a follow-up question.
"The city is reassessing the building, and my contractor thinks repairs will run over budget," he says.
"What happens to first-year NOI if the property tax bill rises by CAD12,000 and servicing and repair costs come in 15% above the budget?"

Okafor then turns to financing.
Aldergrove intends to borrow CAD3,000,000 on an interest-only basis at the rate in Exhibit 2, and Whitlow wants to know how much headroom the lender's covenants leave above that amount.
For the valuation, Okafor capitalizes the budgeted first-year NOI, which she treats as the expected NOI for the coming year, using the required return and growth rate in Exhibit 2.

Finally, Okafor summarizes the rent clauses of the three largest tenants.
The dental group's lease raises base rent by a fixed 2.5% each year.
The imaging clinic's lease resets rent each year by the change in the Canadian consumer price index.
The pharmacy pays base rent plus 6% of annual sales above CAD2,400,000; its sales were CAD2,100,000 last year, so Okafor has budgeted no additional rent from that clause.
Whitlow says his main worry over a ten-year hold is a sustained, unexpected rise in inflation eroding the real value of the rent roll.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **A.** Gross rent: $48.00 \times 9{,}000 = 432{,}000$.
> Servicing recovery is capped at $9.00 \times 9{,}000 = 81{,}000$, below the 96,000 actual cost, so the recovery is 81,000.
> Pass-throughs: $95{,}000 + 35{,}000 = 130{,}000$.
> Vacancy and collection: $0.05 \times 432{,}000 = 21{,}600$.
> Effective gross income: $432{,}000 + 81{,}000 + 130{,}000 - 21{,}600 = 621{,}400$.
> Operating expenses: $96{,}000 + 95{,}000 + 35{,}000 + 20{,}000 = 246{,}000$.
> NOI: $621{,}400 - 246{,}000 - 60{,}000 = 315{,}400$.
> B is wrong because it computes NOI before vacancy, 337,000, and multiplies by 0.95, giving 320,150; that shrinks the recoveries and pass-throughs as well as rent, and scales the expenses too.
> C is wrong because it recovers the full 96,000 of servicing cost, ignoring the CAD9.00 per square metre cap, which overstates NOI by 15,000.
> 2. **B.** Property tax is fully reimbursed, so the CAD12,000 increase appears in both recoveries and expenses and leaves NOI unchanged.
> Servicing rises to $96{,}000 \times 1.15 = 110{,}400$, an increase of 14,400, but the recovery was already at its 81,000 cap, so the owner absorbs all of it.
> NOI: $315{,}400 - 14{,}400 = 301{,}000$.
> A is wrong because it charges the tax increase to the owner as well, $315{,}400 - 14{,}400 - 12{,}000 = 289{,}000$.
> C is wrong because it assumes the extra servicing cost is recovered from tenants; the cap was binding before the overrun.
> 3. **B.** LTV constraint: $0.75 \times 4{,}150{,}000 = 3{,}112{,}500$.
> DSC constraint: maximum debt service $315{,}400 / 1.60 = 197{,}125$, so the maximum interest-only loan is $197{,}125 / 0.065 = 3{,}032{,}692$.
> The binding limit is the smaller loan, 3,032,692; the LTV-based loan would give DSC of $315{,}400 / (3{,}112{,}500 \times 0.065) = 315{,}400 / 202{,}312.50 = 1.5590$, below the 1.60 floor.
> The planned 3,000,000 loan therefore leaves only 32,692 of headroom.
> A is wrong because it stacks the constraints, $0.75 \times 3{,}032{,}692 = 2{,}274{,}519$, instead of taking the minimum.
> C is wrong because it takes the LTV-based loan, which breaches the DSC covenant.
> 4. **B.** Debt service: $3{,}000{,}000 \times 0.065 = 195{,}000$, all interest.
> Pre-tax cash flow: $315{,}400 - 195{,}000 = 120{,}400$.
> Depreciation: $(4{,}150{,}000 - 1{,}030{,}000) / 40 = 3{,}120{,}000 / 40 = 78{,}000$.
> Taxes: $0.265 \times (315{,}400 - 195{,}000 - 78{,}000) = 0.265 \times 42{,}400 = 11{,}236$.
> After-tax cash flow: $120{,}400 - 11{,}236 = 109{,}164$.
> A is wrong because it applies the tax rate to pre-tax cash flow, $120{,}400 \times 0.735 = 88{,}494$, losing the depreciation shield.
> C is wrong because it depreciates the full price including land, $4{,}150{,}000 / 40 = 103{,}750$, so taxes are $0.265 \times 16{,}650 = 4{,}412.25$ and after-tax cash flow is 115,988; land has an infinite life and is not depreciable.
> 5. **C.** Cap rate: $0.095 - 0.020 = 0.075$.
> Value: $315{,}400 / 0.075 = 4{,}205{,}333$.
> A is wrong because it divides by the required return alone, $315{,}400 / 0.095 = 3{,}320{,}000$, ignoring growth.
> B is wrong because it treats the budgeted NOI as trailing and deflates it by one year of growth, $315{,}400 / 1.02 / 0.075 = 4{,}122{,}876$; the vignette states the budget is the expected NOI for the coming year, which is the correct numerator.
> The 7.1% comparable cap rate and the replacement cost estimate are not needed for this calculation.
> 6. **A.** An indexed clause ties rent to an observed variable, here CPI, so rent rises automatically with realized inflation, including inflation nobody expected at signing.
> B is wrong because a step-up clause pre-specifies increases (2.5% a year) that do not respond if inflation turns out higher than expected.
> C is wrong because overage rent depends on the pharmacy's sales exceeding CAD2,400,000; sales are currently below that breakeven, and sales do not track inflation one for one.

## Distractor design

1. B: vacancy applied to NOI instead of gross rent (wrong base); C: recovery cap ignored (misread lease term).
2. A: fully passed-through tax charged to the owner (pass-through vs absorbed); C: servicing overrun assumed recovered although the cap already binds (misread lease term).
3. A: constraints multiplied together instead of taking the smaller loan (wrong formula variant); C: LTV-only loan that breaches the DSC floor (one constraint only).
4. A: tax rate applied to pre-tax cash flow (before-tax vs after-tax base); C: land included in the depreciable base (wrong depreciable base).
5. A: NOI divided by r instead of r minus g (wrong formula variant); B: expected NOI treated as trailing and deflated (period error).
6. B: fixed step-up confused with inflation protection (clause misread); C: sales-contingent overage confused with an inflation link (clause misread).
