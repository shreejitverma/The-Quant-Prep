# Quillhaven Medical Properties REIT: An Endowment Switch Review

> [!info] Timer
> 12 minutes for 4 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Renata Szabo, CFA, is a senior investment analyst at the Brinmoor College endowment in the United States.
The endowment holds its real estate allocation through units of a private open-end core fund whose values are based on quarterly appraisals.
The investment committee is considering replacing part of that holding with shares of Quillhaven Medical Properties REIT (Quillhaven), a self-managed equity REIT that owns medical office buildings leased to hospital systems and physician groups.
Most of Quillhaven's leases run 10 to 15 years and contain fixed annual rent step-ups.

During the most recent year, Quillhaven sold three older medical office buildings below their carrying amounts.
Szabo collects the data in Exhibits 1 and 2 from Quillhaven's annual filing and from a sell-side sector report.

Exhibit 1: Quillhaven selected annual data (USD thousands, except per share)

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Net income | 118,600 |
| Depreciation and amortization of real estate | 121,400 |
| Loss on sale of medical office buildings | 9,800 |
| Non-cash (straight-line) rent | 14,700 |
| Recurring capital expenditures, tenant improvements, and leasing commissions | 31,900 |
| Weighted average shares outstanding (thousands) | 72,500 |
| Dividends per share | USD 2.40 |

Exhibit 2: Quillhaven balance sheet and market data (USD thousands, except where noted)

| Item | Quillhaven | Health care REIT peers |
|---|---|---|
| Next-12-month cash NOI forecast | 348,000 | |
| Cash and equivalents | 42,000 | |
| Accounts receivable | 36,500 | |
| Land held for development (market value) | 58,000 | |
| Goodwill | 27,000 | |
| Total debt (market value) | 1,650,000 | |
| Other liabilities | 96,000 | |
| Deferred tax liability | 22,000 | |
| Share price | USD 52.60 | |
| Sector P/FFO | | 14.9x |
| Sector P/AFFO | | 17.4x |
| Net debt to EBITDA | 4.9x | 6.6x |
| Expected AFFO growth, next 3 years | 2.5% per year | 3.8% per year |

Recent transactions in comparable medical office buildings support a capitalization rate of 6.00%.
Szabo is concerned that long-term interest rates have been rising.
She asks how much Quillhaven's NAVPS would fall if buyers of medical office buildings demanded a capitalization rate of 6.50%, holding the NOI forecast and all other balance sheet items constant.

Szabo presents her findings to two committee members, Kwame Adjei and Mei-Ling Hartwell.
Adjei says, "Quillhaven trades at a premium to its sector on P/AFFO, and I want to know whether the exhibits give us a reason to accept that premium."

Adjei and Hartwell then make three statements about the proposed switch.

Statement 1 (Adjei): "Because Quillhaven shares trade every day, their price will reflect a change in medical office values sooner than the appraisal-based values our private fund reports."

Statement 2 (Adjei): "Moving part of the allocation into Quillhaven will lower the correlation of our real estate holdings with our public equity portfolio over the next few quarters."

Statement 3 (Hartwell): "Like our private fund, Quillhaven can retain most of its earnings to finance its development pipeline, because the endowment is a tax-exempt shareholder."

Szabo must tell the committee which statement she agrees with before the committee votes.

## Questions

1. Quillhaven's AFFO per share is closest to: (LOS: los-08-03-c)
   - A. USD 2.53.
   - B. USD 2.80.
   - C. USD 3.21.
2. Quillhaven's P/AFFO relative to the sector P/AFFO, and the Exhibit 2 factor that best supports Adjei accepting it, are best described as: (LOS: los-08-03-d)
   - A. a 12.3% discount; Quillhaven is potentially undervalued relative to peers.
   - B. a 7.9% premium; Quillhaven's expected AFFO growth is higher than peers'.
   - C. a 7.9% premium; Quillhaven's leverage is lower than peers'.
3. If the capitalization rate rises from 6.00% to 6.50%, the percentage decline in Quillhaven's NAVPS is closest to: (LOS: los-08-03-b)
   - A. 7.7%.
   - B. 10.6%.
   - C. 11.9%.
4. Which statement about the proposed switch is most likely correct? (LOS: los-08-03-e)
   - A. Statement 1
   - B. Statement 2
   - C. Statement 3

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** FFO $= 118{,}600 + 121{,}400 + 9{,}800 = 249{,}800$: add back real estate depreciation and amortization, and add back the loss on sale because it is not sustainable operating income.
> AFFO $= 249{,}800 - 14{,}700 - 31{,}900 = 203{,}200$: subtract non-cash straight-line rent and recurring capital expenditures, tenant improvements, and leasing commissions.
> AFFO per share $= 203{,}200 / 72{,}500 = 2.8028$, or USD 2.80.
> A is wrong because it subtracts the loss on sale instead of adding it back, treating it like a gain ($118{,}600 + 121{,}400 - 9{,}800 - 14{,}700 - 31{,}900 = 183{,}600$; $183{,}600 / 72{,}500 = 2.5324$).
> C is wrong because it adds the non-cash rent instead of subtracting it ($249{,}800 + 14{,}700 - 31{,}900 = 232{,}600$; $232{,}600 / 72{,}500 = 3.2083$), keeping straight-line revenue that was never collected in cash.
> 2. **C.** P/AFFO $= 52.60 / 2.8028 = 18.7672$, and $18.7672 / 17.4 - 1 = 0.0786$, a 7.9% premium to the sector.
> FFO and AFFO are levered income, so lower leverage lowers the required return and supports a higher multiple; Quillhaven's net debt to EBITDA of 4.9x against 6.6x for peers supports the premium.
> A is wrong because it divides price by FFO per share ($52.60 / 3.4455 = 15.2662$) and compares that with the sector P/AFFO ($15.2662 / 17.4 - 1 = -12.26\%$), mixing two different measures.
> B is wrong because it misreads Exhibit 2: Quillhaven's expected AFFO growth of 2.5% is below the peer figure of 3.8%, so growth would argue for a lower multiple, not a higher one.
> 3. **B.** At 6.00%: operating real estate $= 348{,}000 / 0.06 = 5{,}800{,}000$; NAV $= 5{,}800{,}000 + 42{,}000 + 36{,}500 + 58{,}000 - 1{,}650{,}000 - 96{,}000 = 4{,}190{,}500$; NAVPS $= 4{,}190{,}500 / 72{,}500 = 57.8000$.
> Goodwill and the deferred tax liability are excluded as soft items, not economic assets or claims.
> At 6.50%: operating real estate $= 348{,}000 / 0.065 = 5{,}353{,}846.1538$; NAV $= 3{,}744{,}346.1538$; NAVPS $= 51.6462$.
> Change $= 51.6462 / 57.8000 - 1 = -0.1065$, a decline of 10.6%.
> The fall in NAVPS is larger than the fall in property value because debt and other liabilities stay fixed while asset value falls, so leverage magnifies the effect on equity.
> A is wrong because it measures only the change in operating real estate value ($5{,}353{,}846.1538 / 5{,}800{,}000 - 1 = -7.69\%$), ignoring the leverage that magnifies the change in NAV.
> C is wrong because it divides the decline by the post-shock NAVPS instead of the starting NAVPS ($(57.8000 - 51.6462) / 51.6462 = 11.92\%$), using the wrong base.
> 4. **A.** Statement 1 is correct: public real estate prices are set in a liquid, forward-looking market, while private values are appraisal based and lag changes in market conditions.
> B is wrong because listed REIT returns are highly correlated with the stock market in the short run, so the switch would raise, not lower, the short-run correlation with the endowment's public equity portfolio; public real estate tracks property fundamentals over the long term.
> C is wrong because a REIT must distribute most of its taxable income to keep its tax status, whatever the tax position of its shareholders, so lack of retained earnings is a disadvantage of REITs relative to private vehicles, and development must be financed largely with new external capital.

## Distractor design

1. A: loss on sale subtracted as if it were a gain (sign error); C: non-cash rent added instead of subtracted (wrong direction of the AFFO adjustment).
2. A: P/FFO computed and compared with the sector P/AFFO (measures crossed); B: growth direction misread in Exhibit 2 (Quillhaven growth is below peers).
3. A: change in property value only, leverage ignored (asset versus equity change); C: decline divided by the post-shock NAVPS (wrong base).
4. B: short-run correlation direction reversed between public and private real estate; C: REIT payout requirement confused with private vehicle flexibility (shareholder tax status wrongly thought to relax it).
