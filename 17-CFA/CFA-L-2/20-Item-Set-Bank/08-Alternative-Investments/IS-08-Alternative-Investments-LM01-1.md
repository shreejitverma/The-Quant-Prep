# Harrowgate University Endowment: Building a Commodity Sleeve

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Priya Raman, CFA, is investment director of the Harrowgate University Endowment, a USD 3.2 billion fund with a perpetual horizon.
The investment committee has approved a strategic allocation of 4% of the endowment to commodities, and Raman must recommend how to implement it.
She is assisted by Tomas Lindqvist, an external consultant who specializes in real assets.

At the first committee meeting, trustee Harold Beck comments: "We should value the commodity sleeve the way we value our bond portfolio, by forecasting the income each position will generate and discounting it at an appropriate rate."

Lindqvist presents a short list of external commodity managers.
One of them, Pellham Cross Partners, has no storage facilities and does not trade physical commodities.
Pellham systematically buys grain futures in the weeks before harvest, when growers sell forward heavily, and sells energy futures when utilities and airlines buy protection ahead of winter, earning an expected but not guaranteed premium for absorbing that price risk.

Lindqvist also presents market data collected on 2 March, shown in Exhibit 1.

Exhibit 1: Selected spot and futures prices, 2 March

| Commodity | Unit | Spot | Near futures (May) | Far futures (December) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brent crude oil | USD per barrel | 84.10 | 83.25 | 81.40 |
| Arabica coffee | USD per pound | 1.8420 | 1.8710 | 1.9380 |
| Gold | USD per troy ounce | 2,315.0 | 2,331.8 | 2,368.4 |

Lindqvist notes that coffee roasters have been locking in purchase prices far more actively than growers have been selling forward this season.
Raman and Lindqvist then discuss the coffee curve.

Raman: "Under the hedging pressure hypothesis, the shape of the coffee curve is consistent with roasters' demand for price protection exceeding growers' demand."

Lindqvist: "Under the insurance theory, the shape of the coffee curve is the expected equilibrium, because speculators must be paid to take the long side of producers' hedges."

To obtain exposure efficiently, Raman proposes a long total return swap on the Meridian Diversified Commodity Index, with a notional amount equal to the full commodity allocation and monthly resets.
The dealer will hedge its short exposure in the futures market.
Exhibit 2 shows the index levels over the first two months of the swap.

Exhibit 2: Meridian Diversified Commodity Index levels

| Date | Index level |
|---|---|
| Initiation | 412.60 |
| First monthly reset | 421.26 |
| Second monthly reset | 409.05 |

Finally, the committee must choose a benchmark for the sleeve.
Lindqvist compares two candidate indexes with identical commodity coverage.
Index P weights commodities by the value of world production and rebalances annually.
Index F assigns fixed, equal weights and rebalances monthly.
Lindqvist expects commodity prices to move in strong, persistent trends over the next three years, driven by supply constraints in energy and industrial metals.
Beck asks whether the rebalancing choice matters at all, since both indexes hold the same commodities.

## Questions

1. The most appropriate response to Beck's comment on valuation is that commodities are valued: (LOS: los-08-01-c)
   - A. by discounting forecast future prices driven by supply, demand, and expected price volatility, because physically held commodities generate no income and instead incur storage and transportation costs.
   - B. by discounting the convenience yield as the commodity's periodic income, because convenience yield plays the role that coupons play for a bond.
   - C. in the same way as bonds, but at a higher discount rate that compensates the holder for storage costs.
2. Pellham Cross Partners is best described as: (LOS: los-08-01-d)
   - A. an arbitrageur.
   - B. an informed investor.
   - C. a liquidity provider.
3. Based on Exhibit 1, the calendar spread for arabica coffee and the market condition it indicates are: (LOS: los-08-01-e)
   - A. -0.0670 per pound, contango.
   - B. +0.0670 per pound, backwardation.
   - C. -0.0290 per pound, contango.
4. Which of the statements made by Raman and Lindqvist about the coffee curve is correct? (LOS: los-08-01-f)
   - A. Raman's statement only.
   - B. Lindqvist's statement only.
   - C. Both statements.
5. Based on Exhibit 2, at the second monthly reset the endowment will: (LOS: los-08-01-i)
   - A. pay USD 3,710,012.82.
   - B. receive USD 3,710,012.82.
   - C. pay USD 1,101,308.77.
6. Given Lindqvist's market expectations, the best response to Beck's question about rebalancing is that: (LOS: los-08-01-j)
   - A. Index F is likely to outperform, because monthly rebalancing adds to positions that are trending upward.
   - B. Index P is likely to outperform, because production-value weights drift with prices and annual rebalancing avoids repeatedly selling the commodities that are trending up.
   - C. rebalancing frequency does not affect index returns when the two indexes hold the same commodities.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **A.** Stocks and bonds are valued as the present value of expected future cash flows, but a physical commodity pays no income; its value comes from its use, so it is valued as a discounted forecast of future prices shaped by supply, demand, and expected volatility.
> Owning the physical good costs money rather than paying it: storage, insurance, and transportation.
> B is wrong because convenience yield is a non-cash benefit of holding inventory that enters the theory of storage, not a cash flow that can be discounted like a coupon.
> C is wrong because it keeps the cash flow framework that does not apply to commodities; storage costs are carrying costs that are reflected in futures prices, not a premium added to a discount rate.
> 2. **C.** Liquidity providers act as insurers for hedgers: they buy when producers must sell and sell when consumers must buy, for an expected but not guaranteed profit, which is exactly Pellham's strategy.
> A is wrong because arbitrageurs need the ability to store physical commodities to exploit mispricing between the physical good plus storage and financing costs and the futures price, and Pellham has no storage facilities.
> B is wrong because informed investors trade on an information advantage about price direction; Pellham's return comes from taking the other side of hedging flows, not from a view.
> 3. **A.** Calendar spread $= F_{\text{near}} - F_{\text{far}} = 1.8710 - 1.9380 = -0.0670$ per pound.
> A negative calendar spread means the near contract trades below the far contract, which is contango (the spot price of 1.8420 is also below the futures prices).
> B is wrong because it subtracts in the wrong order ($1.9380 - 1.8710 = +0.0670$) and then reads the positive number as backwardation.
> C is wrong because it computes the basis, spot minus near futures ($1.8420 - 1.8710 = -0.0290$), instead of the calendar spread.
> 4. **A.** Raman's statement is correct: the coffee curve is in contango, and under the hedging pressure hypothesis contango arises when consumers' (roasters') hedging demand exceeds producers' (growers'), so consumers bid futures prices up until speculators will sell them protection; this matches Lindqvist's own observation about roasters.
> Lindqvist's statement is incorrect: the insurance theory has producers selling forward at a discount to pay speculators a risk premium, so it predicts backwardation ("normal backwardation") and cannot explain a contango curve.
> B is wrong because it accepts the theory that predicts the opposite curve shape.
> C is wrong because the insurance theory never predicts contango.
> 5. **A.** Notional $= 0.04 \times 3{,}200{,}000{,}000 = 128{,}000{,}000$.
> The second reset is measured from the first reset level: $(409.05 - 421.26)/421.26 = -2.8984\%$.
> The index fell, so the long pays the dealer $128{,}000{,}000 \times 0.028984 = 3{,}710{,}012.82$.
> B is wrong because the swap buyer receives only when the index rises; on a decline the buyer owes the dealer.
> C is wrong because it measures the change from the initiation level ($(409.05 - 412.60)/412.60 = -0.8604\%$, giving $1{,}101{,}308.77$) instead of the previous reset.
> 6. **B.** Frequent rebalancing to fixed weights sells commodities that have risen and buys those that have fallen, which adds value in mean-reverting markets but loses value in trending markets.
> Production-value weights drift with prices, so post-rebalance weights stay close to current weights and the index sells fewer winners; in the persistent trends Lindqvist expects, Index P should outperform.
> A is wrong because it reverses the effect: monthly rebalancing trims, rather than adds to, positions that have trended up.
> C is wrong because rebalancing methodology and frequency are among the construction choices that drive index returns even when coverage is identical.

## Distractor design

1. B: convenience yield treated as a cash flow (theory of storage confused with DCF); C: DCF framework retained with storage costs misplaced into the discount rate.
2. A: arbitrageur chosen without the physical storage requirement; B: trade motive misread (information advantage instead of insurance provision).
3. B: subtraction order reversed (far minus near) and sign read as backwardation; C: basis (spot minus near) computed instead of the calendar spread.
4. B: insurance theory credited with contango (curve prediction reversed); C: both theories accepted without checking which curve each predicts.
5. B: payment direction reversed on a falling index; C: wrong reset base (initiation level instead of previous reset).
6. A: rebalancing effect in trending markets reversed; C: construction choice (rebalancing frequency) treated as irrelevant.
