# Wexcombe Wealth Partners: A Real Estate Suitability Review

> [!info] Timer
> 12 minutes for 4 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Hannah Pryce, CFA, is the compliance officer at Wexcombe Wealth Partners, a UK private wealth manager.
She is reviewing real estate recommendations made last quarter by adviser Oliver Grant before they are sent to clients.

The first file concerns Verdant Property Holdings plc, which Grant recommended to a client seeking growth.
Verdant is listed in London and pays corporation tax on its profits.
It owns a small portfolio of offices but earns most of its profit from developing residential apartment blocks for sale and from third-party property management and estate agency fees.
Verdant retains nearly all of its earnings to fund new development sites.

The second file is a suitability letter for Margaret Ellison, a retired higher-rate taxpayer who owns two let residential flats directly.
Grant proposes that she sell the flats and buy a diversified portfolio of listed REIT shares.
His draft letter contains four statements.

Statement 1: "Listed REITs let you invest small amounts and sell on any trading day, whereas your flats could take months to sell."

Statement 2: "REIT dividends are paid out of income that is not taxed at the company level, so you avoid the double taxation that applies to ordinary company dividends."

Statement 3: "Because a REIT portfolio holds many properties, your overall investment portfolio will be as well diversified as if you held the same buildings privately."

Statement 4: "You will give up the control you now have over tenant selection and refurbishment decisions."

The third file is a fact sheet Grant prepared on Solent Office REIT, which he describes as "attractively priced against its funds from operations."
The fact sheet shows Solent's AFFO but not its FFO, and Pryce asks a junior analyst, Tom Ashdown, to reconstruct the FFO multiple.

Exhibit 1: Solent Office REIT data (GBP thousands, except per share)

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| AFFO | 52,800 |
| AFFO per share | GBP 2.40 |
| Non-cash (straight-line) rent | 3,960 |
| Depreciation and amortization of real estate | 27,500 |
| Recurring capital expenditures and leasing commissions | 11,220 |
| Share price | GBP 41.00 |
| Office REIT sector P/FFO | 14.5x |

The fact sheet also quotes a NAVPS of GBP 46.80, which Ashdown traces to a spreadsheet by a former colleague.
Ashdown finds two errors in that spreadsheet.
First, it deducted Solent's deferred tax liability of GBP 18,700 thousand as if it were debt.
Second, it carried a development site at its historical cost of GBP 9,000 thousand, although a recent independent valuation, which Ashdown considers reliable, puts the site's current market value at GBP 27,000 thousand.
The spreadsheet's cap rate and all other inputs are appropriate.

Pryce notes that Grant's letter to Ellison must be accurate in every statement before it can be sent, and that any fact sheet figure must be reproducible from company data.

## Questions

1. Verdant Property Holdings is best classified as: (LOS: los-08-03-a)
   - A. an equity REIT, because it owns income-producing offices.
   - B. a real estate operating company, because its activities and income would not satisfy REIT qualification tests.
   - C. a mortgage REIT, because development for sale is a form of real estate financing.
2. Which statement in Grant's draft letter to Ellison is least likely accurate? (LOS: los-08-03-e)
   - A. Statement 2
   - B. Statement 3
   - C. Statement 4
3. Based on Exhibit 1, Solent's P/FFO multiple is closest to: (LOS: los-08-03-c)
   - A. 11.23x.
   - B. 13.27x.
   - C. 23.98x.
4. After Ashdown corrects both errors, Solent's NAVPS is closest to: (LOS: los-08-03-b)
   - A. GBP 46.77.
   - B. GBP 47.65.
   - C. GBP 48.47.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Verdant pays corporation tax, earns most of its profit from for-sale development and non-qualifying services such as property management and estate agency fees, and retains its earnings.
> These are the activities and choices that lead a property company to organize as a REOC rather than a REIT.
> A is wrong because owning some income-producing offices does not make it a REIT when its income and payout fail the qualification tests and it is taxed at the company level.
> C is wrong because a mortgage REIT invests in loans secured by real estate; developing property for sale is not lending.
> 2. **B.** Statement 3 is inaccurate: because listed REIT prices co-move with the stock market, particularly in the short run, a REIT holding dilutes diversification at the overall portfolio level relative to holding the same real estate privately.
> A is not the answer because Statement 2 is accurate: dividends paid are deductible for a REIT, effectively exempting it from corporate tax, so income is taxed once, at the shareholder level.
> C is not the answer because Statement 4 is accurate: moving from direct ownership to REIT shares gives up property-level control to REIT management.
> Statement 1 is also accurate, since liquidity and low minimum investment are advantages of listed vehicles.
> 3. **B.** Shares $= 52{,}800 / 2.40 = 22{,}000$ thousand.
> FFO reverses the two AFFO deductions: $52{,}800 + 3{,}960 + 11{,}220 = 67{,}980$; depreciation belongs only to the bridge from net income to FFO and is not added here.
> FFO per share $= 67{,}980 / 22{,}000 = 3.0900$, and P/FFO $= 41.00 / 3.09 = 13.2686$, or 13.27x, slightly below the 14.5x sector multiple.
> A is wrong because it adds depreciation to AFFO instead of the two AFFO adjustments ($80{,}300 / 22{,}000 = 3.65$; $41.00 / 3.65 = 11.2329$).
> C is wrong because it subtracts the adjustments rather than adding them back ($37{,}620 / 22{,}000 = 1.71$; $41.00 / 1.71 = 23.9766$), running the AFFO bridge in the wrong direction.
> 4. **C.** Shares $= 22{,}000$ thousand, from question 3.
> Deferred tax liabilities are excluded from NAV as accounting provisions rather than economic claims, so the wrongly deducted amount is added back: $18{,}700 / 22{,}000 = 0.8500$ per share.
> NAV uses current market values, so the site is marked up from 9,000 to 27,000: $(27{,}000 - 9{,}000) / 22{,}000 = 0.8182$ per share.
> Corrected NAVPS $= 46.80 + 0.8500 + 0.8182 = 48.4682$, or GBP 48.47, a 15.4% discount at the GBP 41.00 price.
> A is wrong because it deducts the deferred tax liability a second time instead of reversing it ($46.80 - 0.8500 + 0.8182 = 46.7682$).
> B is wrong because it corrects only the deferred tax error and leaves the site at historical cost ($46.80 + 0.8500 = 47.6500$).

## Distractor design

1. A: ownership of some rental property mistaken for REIT status; C: development confused with lending (equity versus mortgage).
2. A: accurate single-taxation statement flagged (REIT versus REOC taxation confused); C: accurate loss-of-control trade-off flagged.
3. A: depreciation added into the AFFO-to-FFO bridge; C: bridge run backward (adjustments subtracted from AFFO).
4. A: deferred tax correction applied with the wrong sign; B: land left at historical cost (book versus market value).
