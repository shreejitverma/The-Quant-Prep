# Solent Core-Plus Fund: Revaluing Ashworth Retail Park

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Oliver Brandt, CFA, is a valuation analyst at Solent Core-Plus Fund, an open-end UK property fund that reports net asset value to its unitholders each quarter.
Brandt is preparing the year-end valuation of Ashworth Retail Park, a grocery-anchored retail centre near Manchester that the fund bought three years ago.

The anchor supermarket pays base rent of GBP600,000 per year plus overage rent of 4% of annual sales above GBP18,000,000.
Its sales are forecast at GBP21,500,000 next year.
The supermarket has proposed an amended lease that cuts base rent by 12% and raises the overage rate to 5%, with the breakeven unchanged at GBP18,000,000.
Brandt's colleague asks him at what level of annual sales the fund would be indifferent between the current lease and the proposal.

A mid-size unit is being re-let with a rent-free period, so Year 1 NOI is temporarily depressed.
Brandt projects NOI in Exhibit 1, with NOI growing at a constant 2.5% a year from Year 3 onward.
He values the park over a four-year hold, discounting at a 9.0% required return and capitalizing the year after the sale at a 6.5% terminal cap rate.
The fund's research team notes that recent sales of comparable retail parks imply a 6.25% cap rate.

Exhibit 1: Ashworth Retail Park projected NOI (GBP)

| Year | NOI |
|---|---|
| 1 | 2,150,000 |
| 2 | 2,480,000 |
| 3 | 2,600,000 |
| 4 | 2,665,000 |
| 5 | 2,731,625 |

As a cross-check, Brandt prepares a cost approach estimate.
The 8-year-old buildings have a 50-year economic life, and he depreciates the full replacement cost of the buildings on a straight-line basis.

Exhibit 2: Cost approach inputs (GBP)

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Hard construction costs | 30,000,000 |
| Soft costs (design, legal, permits, finance) | 3,200,000 |
| Developer profit | 4,100,000 |
| Land value | 8,500,000 |
| Current insurance rebuild value | 34,000,000 |

The fund's investment committee is also weighing three possible acquisitions: a city-centre business hotel in Leeds, a last-mile logistics warehouse outside Birmingham, and a suburban office building in Reading.
Two committee members comment.

Priya Nandakumar: "The warehouse is the safest of the three in a downturn because its purpose-built design makes it the easiest to convert to another use if logistics demand fades."

Callum Reid: "The hotel's NOI is the most sensitive of the three to a recession because it effectively re-leases its space every night and carries a largely fixed cost base."

## Questions

1. The level of annual anchor sales at which the fund is indifferent between the current lease and the proposed amendment is closest to: (LOS: los-08-02-b)
   - A. GBP7,200,000.
   - B. GBP19,440,000.
   - C. GBP25,200,000.
2. Brandt's terminal value for Ashworth at the end of Year 4 is closest to: (LOS: los-08-02-d)
   - A. GBP41,000,000.
   - B. GBP42,025,000.
   - C. GBP43,706,000.
3. Brandt's DCF value for Ashworth is closest to: (LOS: los-08-02-d)
   - A. GBP35,268,840.
   - B. GBP37,727,043.
   - C. GBP41,096,271.
4. Which statement about Ashworth's going-in cap rate implied by Brandt's DCF value is most accurate? (LOS: los-08-02-d)
   - A. It is about 5.70%, below the terminal cap rate because the rent-free period depresses Year 1 NOI.
   - B. It is about 5.70%, below the terminal cap rate because the park carries less risk in its early years.
   - C. It is about 6.50%, equal to the terminal cap rate because both equal the required return minus growth.
5. Brandt's cost approach value for Ashworth is closest to: (LOS: los-08-02-d)
   - A. GBP36,388,000.
   - B. GBP38,472,000.
   - C. GBP39,832,000.
6. Regarding the committee members' comments on property types, which is most accurate? (LOS: los-08-02-c)
   - A. Only Nandakumar's comment is correct.
   - B. Only Reid's comment is correct.
   - C. Both comments are correct.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **C.** Set the two rents equal for sales $S$ above the breakeven: $600{,}000 + 0.04(S - 18{,}000{,}000) = 528{,}000 + 0.05(S - 18{,}000{,}000)$, where $528{,}000 = 600{,}000 \times 0.88$.
> The 72,000 base rent cut must be recovered by the extra 1 percentage point of overage: $S - 18{,}000{,}000 = 72{,}000 / 0.01 = 7{,}200{,}000$, so $S = 25{,}200{,}000$.
> At the forecast 21,500,000 the current lease pays $600{,}000 + 0.04 \times 3{,}500{,}000 = 740{,}000$ against $528{,}000 + 0.05 \times 3{,}500{,}000 = 703{,}000$ under the proposal, so the landlord prefers the current lease unless sales exceed 25,200,000.
> A is wrong because it reports only the excess of sales over the breakeven, 7,200,000, and forgets to add back the 18,000,000 breakeven.
> B is wrong because it divides the base rent cut by the new overage rate instead of by the difference in rates, $18{,}000{,}000 + 72{,}000 / 0.05 = 19{,}440{,}000$.
> 2. **B.** The terminal value capitalizes the Year 5 NOI (the year after the sale) at the terminal cap rate: $2{,}731{,}625 / 0.065 = 42{,}025{,}000$.
> Consistency check: $2{,}731{,}625 / 2{,}665{,}000 = 1.0250$, and $0.09 - 0.025 = 0.065$, so the terminal cap rate equals r minus g in the constant-growth phase.
> A is wrong because it capitalizes the Year 4 NOI, $2{,}665{,}000 / 0.065 = 41{,}000{,}000$.
> C is wrong because it uses the 6.25% comparable (going-in) cap rate, $2{,}731{,}625 / 0.0625 = 43{,}706{,}000$.
> 3. **B.** Present values at 9%: $2{,}150{,}000/1.09 = 1{,}972{,}477.06$; $2{,}480{,}000/1.09^2 = 2{,}087{,}366.38$; $2{,}600{,}000/1.09^3 = 2{,}007{,}677.05$; $2{,}665{,}000/1.09^4 = 1{,}887{,}953.19$; sum 7,955,473.68.
> Terminal value: $42{,}025{,}000 / 1.09^4 = 29{,}771{,}569.50$.
> Value: $7{,}955{,}473.68 + 29{,}771{,}569.50 = 37{,}727{,}043$.
> A is wrong because it discounts the terminal value for five years instead of four, $7{,}955{,}473.68 + 42{,}025{,}000/1.09^5 = 35{,}268{,}840$.
> C is wrong because it discounts the NOIs and the terminal value at the 6.5% terminal cap rate instead of the 9.0% discount rate, giving 41,096,271; cap rates capitalize income, they never discount it.
> 4. **A.** Going-in cap rate: $2{,}150{,}000 / 37{,}727{,}043 = 5.6988\%$, about 5.70%, below the 6.5% terminal cap rate.
> The difference reflects a temporarily depressed Year 1 NOI from the rent-free period, not lower risk: every cash flow is discounted at the same 9.0% rate.
> B is wrong because it attributes the gap to lower early-year risk, which contradicts the single 9.0% discount rate applied to all years.
> C is wrong because the identity cap rate equals r minus g holds only in the constant-growth phase; Year 1 to Year 3 growth is far above 2.5%, so the going-in rate need not equal 6.5%.
> 5. **C.** Replacement cost of the buildings: $30{,}000{,}000 + 3{,}200{,}000 + 4{,}100{,}000 = 37{,}300{,}000$.
> Depreciation: $37{,}300{,}000 / 50 \times 8 = 5{,}968{,}000$.
> Value: $37{,}300{,}000 - 5{,}968{,}000 + 8{,}500{,}000 = 39{,}832{,}000$.
> A is wrong because it omits developer profit from replacement cost: $33{,}200{,}000 - 33{,}200{,}000/50 \times 8 + 8{,}500{,}000 = 36{,}388{,}000$.
> B is wrong because it depreciates the land along with the buildings: $45{,}800{,}000 - 45{,}800{,}000/50 \times 8 = 38{,}472{,}000$.
> The 34,000,000 insurance rebuild value is a decoy; it is not the replacement cost the approach requires.
> A cost value above the DCF value is consistent with the oversupply phase, when replacement cost tends to exceed market value.
> 6. **B.** Reid is correct: hospitality is the most cyclical and management-intensive property type, with nightly repricing of space and high fixed operating costs, so recessions hit its NOI hardest.
> Nandakumar is wrong: industrial and warehouse properties are purpose-built and are the least likely of the property types to convert to alternative uses; office and retail are the segments actively being converted.
> A reverses the two comments, and C accepts Nandakumar's incorrect claim about convertibility.

## Distractor design

1. A: excess over breakeven reported as the sales level (breakeven not added back); B: base rent cut divided by the new overage rate instead of the rate difference (wrong formula variant).
2. A: Year n NOI capitalized instead of Year n+1 (period error); C: going-in cap rate from comparables used instead of the terminal cap rate (wrong rate).
3. A: terminal value discounted over n+1 periods (period error); C: cash flows discounted at the terminal cap rate instead of the discount rate (cap rate vs discount rate).
4. B: depressed first-year NOI misread as lower risk (scenario vs risk); C: r minus g identity applied outside the constant-growth phase (wrong formula scope).
5. A: developer profit excluded from replacement cost (component omitted); B: land depreciated (wrong depreciable base); decoy: insurance rebuild value.
6. A and C: warehouse convertibility misstated, since purpose-built industrial space is the hardest type to convert (property type characteristics swapped).
