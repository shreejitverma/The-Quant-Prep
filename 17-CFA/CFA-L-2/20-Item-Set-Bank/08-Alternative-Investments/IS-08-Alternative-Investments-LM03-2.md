# Kestrel Residential Trust: Reconciling Reported Earnings

> [!info] Timer
> 18 minutes for 6 questions. Record confidence 1-5 before opening the answer key.

## Vignette

Daniel Okoro, CFA, covers US apartment REITs for Larchmont Securities, a sell-side research firm.
He is preparing an initiation report on Kestrel Residential Trust, an equity REIT that owns garden-style and mid-rise apartment communities in the southeastern United States.
Kestrel's management reports only GAAP net income in its press release, so Okoro must build FFO and AFFO himself from the annual filing.

During the year, Kestrel sold four older communities and recognized a gain on the sales, which management presents within other income.
It also wrote down the carrying amount of one community damaged by flooding.
Kestrel is developing two new towers, and management describes the related spending as growth capital that will not recur once the towers are leased.
The annual filing does not state the weighted average share count, but Okoro notes that total dividends paid to common shareholders were USD 176,000 thousand, equal to USD 2.20 per share.

Exhibit 1: Kestrel selected annual data (USD thousands)

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Net income | 186,420 |
| Depreciation and amortization of real estate | 142,310 |
| Gain on sale of apartment communities (in other income) | 38,650 |
| Impairment of real estate | 12,900 |
| Non-cash (straight-line) rent | 4,280 |
| Recurring capital expenditures and turnover and leasing costs | 36,900 |
| Development capital expenditures (two new towers) | 118,000 |
| Common dividends paid | 176,000 |

Exhibit 2: Valuation inputs

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Kestrel share price | USD 64.50 |
| Apartment REIT sector P/FFO | 18.2x |
| Apartment REIT sector P/AFFO | 21.0x |
| Risk-free rate | 4.0% |
| Kestrel beta | 0.80 |
| Market risk premium | 5.5% |
| Expected dividend per share, Year 1 | USD 2.30 |
| Dividend growth, Years 2 and 3 | 6.0% per year |
| Dividend growth, Year 4 onward | 4.0% per year |
| Kestrel net debt to EBITDA | 7.4x |
| Peer median net debt to EBITDA | 5.9x |
| Kestrel average portfolio age | 9 years |
| Peer median portfolio age | 17 years |

Okoro's supervisor, Rachel Lindqvist, reviews his draft.
She says, "You treat the flood write-down like depreciation, but a one-off loss has no place in a sustainable earnings measure, so leave it in net income."
Okoro replies that his treatment follows the standard industry definition.

Lindqvist adds that institutional clients will ask how well Kestrel's dividend is covered.
"Give them the payout ratio on whichever measure best reflects Kestrel's sustainable dividend-paying capacity, not the one that flatters the number."

Finally, Lindqvist observes that Kestrel's portfolio is much younger than its peers' and asks Okoro to explain in the report why the shares nonetheless trade at a lower P/FFO multiple than the sector.

## Questions

1. Kestrel's FFO per share is closest to: (LOS: los-08-03-c)
   - A. USD 3.63.
   - B. USD 3.79.

2. Kestrel's AFFO per share is closest to: (LOS: los-08-03-c)
   - A. USD 1.80.
   - B. USD 3.27.
   - C. USD 3.33.
3. Using the appropriate sector multiple from Exhibit 2, the value of a Kestrel share based on AFFO is closest to: (LOS: los-08-03-d)
   - A. USD 59.56.
   - B. USD 68.72.
   - C. USD 79.53.
4. Using a two-step dividend discount model with a CAPM discount rate, the intrinsic value of a Kestrel share is closest to: (LOS: los-08-03-d)
   - A. USD 50.46.
   - B. USD 52.34.
   - C. USD 54.18.
5. The payout ratio Okoro should report in response to Lindqvist's request is closest to: (LOS: los-08-03-c)
   - A. 58.1%.
   - B. 67.2%.
   - C. 94.4%.
6. Kestrel's current P/FFO multiple, and the factor in Exhibit 2 that best explains why it differs from the sector multiple, are closest to: (LOS: los-08-03-d)
   - A. 17.0x; higher financial leverage than peers.
   - B. 17.0x; younger portfolio than peers.
   - C. 19.7x; higher financial leverage than peers.

## Answer key

> [!success]- Answers and reasoning
> 1. **B.** Shares are not given, so derive them from the dividend data: $176{,}000 / 2.20 = 80{,}000$ thousand shares.
> FFO $= 186{,}420 + 142{,}310 - 38{,}650 + 12{,}900 = 302{,}980$: add back real estate depreciation and amortization, subtract the gain on sale hidden in other income, and add back the real estate impairment.
> FFO per share $= 302{,}980 / 80{,}000 = 3.7872$, or USD 3.79.
> Okoro is right and Lindqvist is wrong: the full definition of FFO adds back real estate impairments and write-downs unrelated to depreciation, just as it strips gains and losses on property sales.
> A is wrong because it follows Lindqvist and leaves the impairment in net income ($290{,}080 / 80{,}000 = 3.6260$).
> C is wrong because it adds the gain on sale instead of subtracting it ($380{,}280 / 80{,}000 = 4.7535$).
> 2. **B.** AFFO $= 302{,}980 - 4{,}280 - 36{,}900 = 261{,}800$, subtracting non-cash rent and recurring maintenance-type capital expenditures and leasing costs.
> AFFO per share $= 261{,}800 / 80{,}000 = 3.2725$, or USD 3.27.
> Development capital for new towers is growth spending, not the maintenance-type spending needed to sustain existing income, so it is not deducted.
> A is wrong because it also deducts the 118,000 of development capital ($143{,}800 / 80{,}000 = 1.7975$), treating one-off growth spending as recurring.
> C is wrong because it omits the non-cash rent deduction ($266{,}080 / 80{,}000 = 3.3260$), leaving straight-line revenue that was never received in cash.
> 3. **B.** Pair the P/AFFO multiple with AFFO per share: $3.2725 \times 21.0 = 68.7225$, or USD 68.72.
> A is wrong because it applies the P/FFO multiple to AFFO per share ($3.2725 \times 18.2 = 59.5595$).
> C is wrong because it applies the P/AFFO multiple to FFO per share ($3.7872 \times 21.0 = 79.5322$).
> 4. **C.** Required return $r = 4.0\% + 0.80 \times 5.5\% = 8.4\%$.
> Dividends: $D_1 = 2.30$, $D_2 = 2.30 \times 1.06 = 2.4380$, $D_3 = 2.4380 \times 1.06 = 2.5843$, $D_4 = 2.5843 \times 1.04 = 2.6877$.
> Terminal value at the end of Year 3 $= 2.6877 / (0.084 - 0.040) = 61.0830$.
> $V_0 = 2.30/1.084 + 2.4380/1.084^2 + (2.5843 + 61.0830)/1.084^3 = 54.1803$, or USD 54.18.
> A is wrong because it discounts the terminal value four periods instead of three (50.4642), dating it one year too late.
> B is wrong because it uses $D_3$ rather than $D_4$ in the Gordon numerator (52.3358), so the terminal value omits one year of growth.
> 5. **B.** AFFO best reflects sustainable dividend-paying capacity because it charges non-cash rent and recurring capital spending.
> Payout $= 2.20 / 3.2725 = 0.6723$, or 67.2%.
> A is wrong because it uses FFO per share ($2.20 / 3.7872 = 58.09\%$), which ignores the recurring spending needed to keep the apartments competitive and flatters coverage.
> C is wrong because it uses EPS ($186{,}420 / 80{,}000 = 2.3302$; $2.20 / 2.3302 = 94.41\%$), and net income is distorted by non-economic depreciation and the one-off gain and impairment.
> 6. **A.** P/FFO $= 64.50 / 3.7872 = 17.03$, below the sector's 18.2x.
> FFO and AFFO are levered income, so higher leverage raises the required return and compresses the multiple; Kestrel's net debt to EBITDA of 7.4x against a peer median of 5.9x explains the lower multiple.
> B is wrong because a younger, better-maintained portfolio needs less capital spending to sustain rent growth and supports a higher multiple, not a lower one.
> C is wrong because 19.7x is price over AFFO per share ($64.50 / 3.2725 = 19.71$), mislabelled as P/FFO.

## Distractor design

1. A: impairment left in net income (supervisor's non-standard FFO definition); C: gain on sale added instead of subtracted (sign error).
2. A: development (growth) capex deducted as if recurring maintenance capex; C: non-cash straight-line rent not removed.
3. A: P/FFO multiple crossed onto AFFO; C: P/AFFO multiple crossed onto FFO.
4. A: terminal value discounted one period too many; B: $D_3$ instead of $D_4$ in the Gordon numerator.
5. A: FFO used as the coverage base (measure that flatters); C: GAAP EPS used as the coverage base.
6. B: portfolio age direction reversed (younger portfolios earn premiums); C: P/AFFO computed and labelled as P/FFO.
