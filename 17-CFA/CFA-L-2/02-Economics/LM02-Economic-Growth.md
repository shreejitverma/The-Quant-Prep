## Core intuition

The module studies the long-run trend in aggregate output, potential GDP, rather than business-cycle fluctuations (V2 p.82).
Potential GDP is the maximum output the economy can sustainably produce without pushing up inflation, so its growth rate is the upper limit on sustainable growth (V2 p.82, p.92).
Small growth-rate differences compound into enormous income differences: Venezuela and Argentina were richer than Japan, Singapore, and South Korea in 1950 and vastly poorer per capita by 2018 (V2 p.89, p.90).
Cross-country comparisons must use purchasing power parity exchange rates, not volatile market rates that ignore cheap non-traded goods in developing countries and so understate their living standards (V2 p.83, p.85, p.86).

Why does an investor care?
For equities, the long-run cap is brutal: aggregate earnings cannot permanently outgrow GDP, because the profit share of GDP cannot rise forever, and the P/E ratio cannot expand forever either (V2 p.92).
The Grinold-Kroner decomposition makes this precise: expected equity return is dividend yield plus repricing plus inflation plus real growth minus the change in shares outstanding (V2 p.92).
Over long horizons the repricing term and the dilution term must average near zero, so nominal GDP growth plus the dividend yield is the anchor for long-run equity returns (V2 p.92, p.96).
The dilution term hides two effects, net buybacks and the relative dynamism of the economy (growth created in firms not listed on the exchange), which is why China could grow 8.2% a year and deliver only a 0.7% real equity return from 1997 to 2017 (V2 p.93, p.94).
For fixed income, actual growth above potential signals inflation pressure and central bank tightening, potential growth feeds sovereign credit ratings, output gaps drive monetary policy, and structural deficits are judged against potential GDP (V2 p.95).
Faster potential growth also means higher real interest rates, because savers must be induced to fund the capital accumulation that faster growth requires (V2 p.95).

> [!abstract] Idea 1: the production function and its two productivity concepts
> Everything runs through Y = AF(K, L), and in Cobb-Douglas form output per worker is y = Ak^alpha (V2 p.97, p.98).
> Labor productivity (y = Y/L) is output per worker; TFP (A) is a scale factor capturing technology and organization, measured only as a residual (V2 p.98, p.99).
> Alpha is capital's share of income because competitive factors are paid their marginal products, so the exponents are simultaneously factor shares and output elasticities, which is what makes growth accounting possible from national accounts data (V2 p.98, p.99).

Two properties of Cobb-Douglas drive every conclusion in the module (V2 p.98).
Constant returns to scale means scaling both inputs scales output proportionally, which lets us work per worker.
Diminishing marginal productivity of each individual input means each extra unit of capital adds less output than the last, and the smaller alpha is, the faster those returns die away (V2 p.98, p.99).

> [!abstract] Idea 2: capital deepening moves you along the curve, TFP shifts the curve
> Capital deepening, a rise in the capital-to-labor ratio, is a movement along the per capita production function and peters out as the marginal product of capital falls toward its marginal cost (V2 p.101).
> Technological progress shifts the whole function upward proportionally, raises the marginal product of capital, and is the only source of sustained growth in per capita output (V2 p.101, p.102).
> Empirically, capital deepening's contribution equals labor productivity growth minus TFP growth, a subtraction the exam asks for directly (V2 p.114, p.115).

The developing-versus-developed contrast follows immediately.
A country with little capital per worker (India at $6,270 per worker in 2000 versus the United States at $148,091) sits low on the curve where diminishing returns barely bite, so investment powerfully raises growth (V2 p.112).
Rich countries sit where the curve is flat, so their growth must come from TFP, which is why they spend heavily on R&D and post-secondary education while developing countries can grow by imitation and should emphasize primary and secondary education (V2 p.88, p.114).
Growth preconditions beyond capital are institutional: savings and investment, developed financial markets that screen and monitor borrowers, rule of law and property rights, education and health, tax and regulatory systems that encourage entry, and openness to trade and capital flows (V2 p.86, p.87, p.88, p.89).
Foreign investment, direct or indirect, can break the vicious cycle of low income, low savings, and low investment (V2 p.87, p.88).

Labor input is hours available for work: labor force times average hours.
Its growth depends on population growth, participation, net migration, and average hours worked (V2 p.105).
Population growth raises aggregate growth but not per capita growth; rising participation can raise per capita growth but only as a transition to a new level; immigration is a possible fix for aging developed countries; average hours trend down as countries get richer (V2 p.105, p.106, p.107, p.110).
Human capital raises productivity directly and through spillovers into innovation, and health investment matters especially in developing countries (V2 p.110, p.111).
Natural resources are the one input a country need not own, since trade provides access; abundance can even hurt, through weak institutions or Dutch disease, where resource-driven currency appreciation makes manufacturing uncompetitive (V2 p.103, p.104).

> [!abstract] Idea 3: the Solow steady state ties growth to TFP and nothing else
> In the neoclassical model the steady state has a constant output-to-capital ratio, and per capita output grows at theta/(1 - alpha) forever, with total output growing at theta/(1 - alpha) + n (V2 p.124, p.125).
> Saving more raises the level of per capita income and the capital-to-labor ratio, with a temporary growth burst during the transition, but the growth rate returns to theta/(1 - alpha), so capital accumulation affects levels, not long-run growth (V2 p.127, p.130, p.131, p.132).
> Endogenous growth theory deletes the diminishing returns on aggregate capital (broadened to include knowledge), so the production function is a straight line y_e = c k_e and a higher saving rate raises growth permanently through sc - delta - n (V2 p.136, p.137).

The classical (Malthusian) model is the historical baseline: population accelerates whenever income rises above subsistence, diminishing returns to labor against fixed land drag productivity back down, and technology produces a larger but not richer population (V2 p.123).
It failed because population growth slowed as incomes rose and technology outran diminishing returns (V2 p.123).
The neoclassical critique cuts the other way: Solow and Denison found TFP explained 70% to 80% of US growth, yet the model leaves TFP unexplained, exogenous, a residual (V2 p.135).
Endogenous growth theory answers by making R&D and knowledge capital paid factors of production whose externalities keep the aggregate marginal product of capital constant, private returns fall short of social returns, companies under-invest, and government subsidies to R&D correct the market failure (V2 p.136, p.137).

Convergence is where theories are tested.
The neoclassical model predicts capital-poor countries grow faster (higher marginal product of capital), so per capita incomes should converge, absolutely if characteristics do not matter, conditionally if countries share the same saving rate, population growth, and production function (V2 p.139).
The data show club convergence: members that adopt the right institutions converge toward the richest, while non-members fall behind, and traps (unreformed labor markets, import substitution kept too long) keep countries out of the club (V2 p.139).
Endogenous growth theory predicts no convergence at all, because rich countries with large knowledge externalities can grow as fast or faster indefinitely (V2 p.140).
Open trade accelerates neoclassical convergence, capital flows to the capital-poor country, which runs a trade deficit and grows above steady state temporarily, but only endogenous models predict a permanently higher growth rate, through selection, scale, and backwardness effects (V2 p.142, p.143).

## Formulas and derivations

**Grinold-Kroner decomposition of expected equity return** (V2 p.92)
$$
E(R_e) = dy + \Delta(P/E) + i + g - \Delta S
$$
^f-02-02-1

- $dy$: dividend yield; $\Delta(P/E)$: expected repricing (change in the market P/E); $i$: inflation; $g$: real economic growth; $\Delta S$: change in shares outstanding (dilution).
- Return is dividend yield plus capital gain; the capital gain splits into repricing plus per-share earnings growth, and per-share earnings growth splits into inflation plus real growth minus dilution (V2 p.92).
- Over long horizons $\Delta(P/E)$ and the earnings share of GDP cannot trend, so nominal GDP growth plus the dividend yield anchors the long-run return (V2 p.92, p.96).

**Dilution effect decomposition** (V2 p.93)
$$
\Delta S = nbb + rd
$$
^f-02-02-2

- $nbb$: net buybacks (buybacks net of new issuance, at the national market level); $rd$: relative dynamism, growth accruing to firms not publicly traded.
- A negative $nbb$ (net issuance) or a positive $rd$ widens the wedge between economic growth and listed-company earnings growth (V2 p.93).
- This wedge is why China's 8.2% growth coexisted with a 0.7% real equity return over 1997-2017 (V2 p.93, p.94).

**Two-factor aggregate production function** (V2 p.97)
$$
Y = A\,F(K, L)
$$
^f-02-02-3

- $Y$: aggregate output; $K$: capital services; $L$: labor (workers or hours); $A$: total factor productivity, a multiplicative scale factor.
- A rise in $A$ raises output proportionally for any input mix; a change in relative factor productivity would instead change $F(\cdot)$ itself (V2 p.97).
- TFP captures technology, management methods, and organization, and is estimated only as a residual (V2 p.97, p.99).

**Cobb-Douglas production function** (V2 p.97)
$$
F(K, L) = K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha}
$$
^f-02-02-4

- $\alpha \in (0,1)$: capital's share of income and the output elasticity of capital; $1-\alpha$: labor's share and elasticity.
- Exhibits constant returns to scale (exponents sum to 1) and diminishing marginal productivity in each input separately (V2 p.98, p.99).
- A small $\alpha$ means diminishing returns to capital bite quickly; an $\alpha$ near 1 means they are mild (V2 p.99).

**Marginal product of capital and the capital share** (V2 p.98)
$$
MPK = \alpha A K^{\alpha-1} L^{1-\alpha} = \frac{\alpha Y}{K}, \qquad \frac{\alpha Y}{K} = r
$$
^f-02-02-5

- $r$: rental price of capital; profit maximization sets $MPK = r$, so $\alpha = rK/Y$, capital income over GDP.
- Derivation: differentiate $Y = AK^{\alpha}L^{1-\alpha}$ with respect to $K$ to get $\alpha Y/K$ (V2 p.98).
- This is why $\alpha$ can be read off the national income accounts (roughly 0.3 for the United States, labor share 0.7) (V2 p.98, p.99).

**Per capita production function** (V2 p.98)
$$
y = \frac{Y}{L} = A\left(\frac{K}{L}\right)^{\alpha} = A k^{\alpha}
$$
^f-02-02-6

- $y$: output per worker (labor productivity); $k = K/L$: capital-to-labor ratio.
- Divide the constant-returns production function by $L$; labor productivity depends on capital per worker, TFP, and $\alpha$ (V2 p.98).
- Capital deepening raises $k$ along this curve; TFP growth shifts the whole curve up (V2 p.101).

**Growth accounting equation (Solow)** (V2 p.99)
$$
\frac{\Delta Y}{Y} = \frac{\Delta A}{A} + \alpha \frac{\Delta K}{K} + (1-\alpha)\frac{\Delta L}{L}
$$
^f-02-02-7

- Growth of output equals TFP growth plus capital growth weighted by $\alpha$ plus labor growth weighted by $1-\alpha$.
- Take logs of $Y = AK^{\alpha}L^{1-\alpha}$ and differentiate; the elasticities become the weights (V2 p.99).
- TFP growth is the residual: measure output, capital, labor, and the shares, and solve for $\Delta A/A$; unspecified inputs (natural resources) are subsumed into it (V2 p.99).
- With US shares, a 1% rise in labor growth adds roughly double what a 1% rise in capital growth adds (0.7 versus 0.3) (V2 p.99).

> [!tip] Calculator
> Spain 2006-2015: `0.413 x 0.04 = STO 1`, `0.587 x 0.0127 +/- = STO 2`, `RCL 1 + RCL 2 - 0.0085 =` gives 0.0006, or 0.06% (V2 p.148).

**Labor productivity growth accounting equation** (V2 p.100)
$$
\text{Potential GDP growth} = \text{Long-term labor force growth} + \text{Long-term labor productivity growth}
$$
^f-02-02-8

- Simpler than Solow accounting: no capital stock or TFP estimate needed; 1% labor force growth plus 2% productivity growth gives 3% potential growth (V2 p.100).
- The cost is that the productivity term mixes capital deepening and TFP in a way that is hard to analyze or project (V2 p.100, p.149).
- Capital deepening's contribution equals labor productivity growth minus TFP growth (V2 p.114, p.115).

**Per-worker growth decomposition** (V2 p.124)
$$
\frac{\Delta y}{y} = \frac{\Delta A}{A} + \alpha \frac{\Delta k}{k}, \qquad \frac{\Delta k}{k} = \frac{\Delta K}{K} - \frac{\Delta L}{L}
$$
^f-02-02-9

- Per-worker version of growth accounting, from $y = Ak^{\alpha}$ in logs; exact only in continuous time (V2 p.124).
- Growth in output per worker comes from TFP growth plus $\alpha$ times growth in capital per worker.

**Capital-to-labor ratio dynamics** (V2 p.124)
$$
\frac{\Delta k}{k} = \frac{sY}{K} - \delta - n
$$
^f-02-02-10

- $s$: saving rate (closed economy, so investment $I = sY$); $\delta$: depreciation rate; $n$: labor force growth $\Delta L/L$.
- Derivation: $\Delta K = sY - \delta K$, so $\Delta K/K = sY/K - \delta$; subtract labor growth $n$ to get per-worker capital growth (V2 p.124).
- Capital per worker grows when saving outruns the investment needed to cover depreciation and equip new workers.

**Steady-state (sustainable) growth rates** (V2 p.125)
$$
\frac{\Delta y}{y} = \frac{\theta}{1-\alpha}, \qquad \frac{\Delta Y}{Y} = \frac{\theta}{1-\alpha} + n
$$
^f-02-02-11

- $\theta = \Delta A/A$: TFP growth; $\theta/(1-\alpha)$ is also the steady-state growth of labor productivity and of capital per worker.
- Derivation: in the steady state $\Delta k/k = \Delta y/y$; substitute into $\Delta y/y = \theta + \alpha\,\Delta k/k$ and solve to get $\Delta y/y = \theta/(1-\alpha)$ (V2 p.124, p.125).
- Note the divisor is labor's share $1-\alpha$, not capital's; saving, depreciation, and $n$ do not appear, which is the model's central claim (V2 p.125, p.131).

> [!tip] Calculator
> Example 11 parameters: `0.02 / 0.65 =` gives 0.0308, or 3.08% per capita; `+ 0.01 =` gives 4.08% for total output (V2 p.133).

**Equilibrium output-to-capital ratio** (V2 p.125, p.126)
$$
\frac{Y}{K} = \frac{1}{s}\left[\frac{\theta}{1-\alpha} + \delta + n\right] \equiv \Psi
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
s\,y = \left[\frac{\theta}{1-\alpha} + \delta + n\right] k
$$
^f-02-02-12

- Substitute the steady-state growth rate $\theta/(1-\alpha)$ into formula 10 and rearrange; $\Psi$ is constant on the balanced path (V2 p.125).
- The savings/investment form says steady state occurs where actual saving per worker $sf(k)$ just equips new workers ($n$), replaces depreciation ($\delta$), and deepens capital at $\theta/(1-\alpha)$ (V2 p.126, p.127).
- On this path the marginal product of capital $\alpha(Y/K)$ is constant and equals the real interest rate; capital deepening continues but no longer affects growth or the MPK (V2 p.125).
- Comparative statics: higher $s$ raises $k$ and $y$ levels only; higher $n$ or $\delta$ lowers them; higher $\theta$ lowers the current level but steepens the path (V2 p.127, p.128).

**Transition dynamics away from the steady state** (V2 p.129)
$$
\frac{\Delta y}{y} = \frac{\theta}{1-\alpha} + \alpha s\left(\frac{y}{k} - \Psi\right), \qquad
\frac{\Delta k}{k} = \frac{\theta}{1-\alpha} + s\left(\frac{y}{k} - \Psi\right)
$$
^f-02-02-13

- $y/k = Y/K$: the current output-to-capital ratio; the gap $(y/k - \Psi)$ measures distance from equilibrium.
- If $y/k > \Psi$ (capital scarce), actual investment exceeds required investment and both ratios grow above the steady-state rate; because $\alpha < 1$, capital grows faster than output, $Y/K$ falls, and growth decays back to $\theta/(1-\alpha)$ (V2 p.129, p.130).
- A saving-rate increase works through this channel: it lowers $\Psi$, makes the gap positive, and produces a temporary growth burst (V2 p.131).

**Level impact of a parameter change (comparative statics ratios)** (V2 p.132)
$$
\frac{k_{new}}{k_{old}} = \left[\frac{(Y/K)_{new}}{(Y/K)_{old}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}, \qquad
\frac{y_{new}}{y_{old}} = \left(\frac{k_{new}}{k_{old}}\right)^{\alpha}
$$
^f-02-02-14

- Derived from $y = Ak^{\alpha}$ with TFP cancelled, so the ratios isolate the saving-rate effect from TFP growth (V2 p.132, p.134).
- The exponent $1/(\alpha - 1)$ is negative: a lower output-to-capital ratio means a higher capital-to-labor ratio.
- In the new steady state after a saving rise, $k$ and $y$ sit permanently higher but grow at the unchanged rate $\theta/(1-\alpha)$ (V2 p.134).

**Endogenous growth production function** (V2 p.137)
$$
y_e = f(k_e) = c\,k_e
$$
^f-02-02-15

- $c$: the constant marginal product of capital in the aggregate economy; capital is defined broadly to include human and knowledge capital and R&D (V2 p.136, p.137).
- R&D externalities keep the social marginal product of capital from diminishing, so the per capita production function is a straight line rather than a flattening curve (V2 p.137).
- Constant returns hold privately (no monopoly advantage to size) while increasing returns hold economy-wide through spillovers (V2 p.136).

**Endogenous growth rate of per capita output** (V2 p.137)
$$
\frac{\Delta y_e}{y_e} = \frac{\Delta k_e}{k_e} = s c - \delta - n
$$
^f-02-02-16

- Substitute $y_e = ck_e$ into formula 10: $Y/K = c$ is fixed, so output per worker always grows with capital per worker.
- Every term is constant, so this is both the short-run and the long-run growth rate: a higher saving rate raises growth permanently, the key contrast with Solow (V2 p.137).
- No convergence is predicted: rich countries with strong knowledge externalities can grow as fast as or faster than poor ones (V2 p.137, p.140).

> [!tip] Calculator
> Example 12: `0.235 x 0.704 = - 0.10 - 0.01 =` gives 0.0554, or 5.54% (V2 p.137).

**Physical capital stock accumulation** (V2 p.147)
$$
K_t = K_{t-1} + I - D
$$
^f-02-02-17

- $I$: gross investment (gross capital spending); $D$: depreciation (consumption of fixed capital); both taken as percentages of GDP times GDP in Example 15.
- The stock grows only while net investment $I - D$ is positive (V2 p.111, p.147).
- Spain 2006: $K = 1{,}808 + 1{,}390(0.313 - 0.149) = 2{,}036$ billion (V2 p.147).

## Worked examples

**Example 1: why growth rates matter** (V2 p.89, p.90, p.91)
Per capita GDP in 1950: Venezuela $8,104, Argentina $6,164, Singapore $4,299, Japan $3,048, South Korea $1,185; by 2018: $9,487, $18,255, $66,189, $39,313, $36,756.
Q1: annual growth over 68 years is $(V_{2018}/V_{1950})^{1/68} - 1$.
Argentina $(18{,}255/6{,}164)^{1/68} - 1 = 1.6\%$; Venezuela 0.2%; Japan 3.8%; Singapore 4.1%; South Korea 5.2% (Python: 1.61%, 0.23%, 3.83%, 4.10%, 5.18%).
Q2: sustained growth differences compound into reversed income rankings; the Asian countries joined the developed ranks while Argentina and Venezuela slipped to developing status.
Q3: Argentina at Japan's 3.8% rate: $6{,}164 \times 1.038^{68} = 6{,}164 \times 12.63 = \$77{,}854$ versus the actual $18,255 (Python: multiplier 12.6305, level $77,854).
Q4: Venezuela's plan (infrastructure, education, health) improves two preconditions but leaves property rights, open trade and capital flows, and financial markets untouched, so a significant growth improvement is unlikely. ^ex-02-02-1

**Example 2: impact on equity and fixed income investors** (V2 p.95, p.96)
A vendor projects 13.5% long-run equity returns (11% appreciation plus 2.5% dividend yield); a macro service forecasts 3.25% real growth and 3.75% inflation.
Q1: macro forecasts deserve more confidence because potential growth and inflation evolve slowly, while equity returns are volatile and extrapolation ignores valuation change.
Q2: nominal GDP growth is $3.25\% + 3.75\% = 7\%$ (Python: 7.0%), so 11% appreciation implies a permanent $11\% - 7\% = 4\%$ annual contribution from P/E expansion and dilution, which cannot hold.
Q3: a reasonable long-run return is nominal growth plus dividend yield: $7\% + 2.5\% = 9.5\%$ (Python: 9.5%).
Q4: with actual growth (1.5%) far below potential (3.25%), the analyst assumes a widening output gap, downward inflation pressure, central bank easing, and rising bond prices. ^ex-02-02-2

**Example 3: capital deepening vs. technological progress** (V2 p.102, p.103)
Country A has $100,000 of capital per worker; Country B has $5,000.
Q1: more business investment raises $k$ in both, but only B, low on the production function where diminishing returns are small, gets a meaningful boost to potential growth; A sits where the curve is flat.
Q2: university research raises TFP and shifts the production function up in both countries, offsetting diminishing returns; developed countries depend on this channel.
Q3: removing B's restrictions on foreign investment works exactly like more domestic investment, a movement along the curve that boosts B's potential growth given its low $k$. ^ex-02-02-3

**Example 4: impact of natural resources** (V2 p.104, p.105)
The 1990 share of world proved oil reserves is uncorrelated with 1990-2018 per capita growth (Saudi Arabia 25.75% of reserves, 0.4% growth; China 2.40%, 9.1%; Ethiopia 0.00%, 4.6%).
Access to resources through trade matters, but ownership is not necessary: countries lacking oil trade for it, substitute other energy sources, shift toward services, and adopt energy-efficient methods.
Resources are important but neither necessary nor sufficient for growth. ^ex-02-02-4

**Example 5: impact of the age distribution on growth, Mexico vs. Germany** (V2 p.107)
Germany's population was flat over 2000-2018 while Mexico's grew 1.35% a year; 26.5% of Mexico's population is under 15 versus 13.5% for Germany, and only 7.2% of Mexico's is over 65 versus 21.5% for Germany.
Mexico gets a demographic boost from young workers entering the workforce; Germany faces a demographic burden and must rely on productivity growth, higher participation, or immigration to raise near-term potential growth. ^ex-02-02-5

**Example 6: potential growth in Spain, labor input** (V2 p.108, p.109)
Hours worked grew 1.2% a year over 2000-2010, but immigration added nearly 5 million of the 5.8 million population increase; without it population growth was about 0.2% a year, not 1.35%.
Q1: labor input growth should slow: immigration at that pace is unsustainable, the male participation rate (80.4%) is already high, female participation (66.1%) is mid-range, and only 15% of the population is under 15 (versus 26.5% in Mexico).
Q2: cutting the 20.1% unemployment rate would raise utilization of the existing labor supply, a transition to a higher employment level that can lift growth for a substantial period without raising the permanent potential growth rate. ^ex-02-02-6

**Example 7: why the sluggish growth in the Japanese economy?** (V2 p.116, p.117)
Japan grew about 1% a year since 1990 versus 4.2% over 1971-1990 because labor input is flat, TFP growth is near zero (0.4% for 1995-2005, negative for 2005-2018), and diminishing returns make its capital-deepening-only productivity growth unsustainable.
Q1: with formula 8, zero labor force growth plus 1.0% labor productivity growth (Exhibit 10, 2005-2018) gives potential GDP growth of about $0\% + 1\% = 1\%$ (Python: 1.0%). ^ex-02-02-7

**Example 8: the Irish economy** (V2 p.118, p.119, p.120)
Q1: growth accounting for Ireland: 1995-2005 growth of 7.3% came from labor 2.3% (quantity 2.0%, quality 0.3%), capital 3.3% (non-ICT 2.6%, ICT 0.7%), and TFP 1.7%; for 2005-2018, growth of 3.3% came from labor 0.3%, capital 2.8%, and TFP 0.1% (V2 p.119).

Q2: forecast labor force growth of 0.3% (immigration-driven population growth is not repeating), assume no further capital deepening, and assume TFP reverts to its 1.7% 1995-2005 average with labor productivity growing at the TFP rate.

**Example 9: investment outlook for China and India** (V2 p.120, p.121, p.122)

**Example 10: steady-state rate of growth for China, Japan, and Ireland** (V2 p.125, p.126)

Ireland: $0.9\%/0.574 + 0.3\% = 1.87\%$ (Python: 1.87%).

**Example 11: comparative statics and transitional growth in the neoclassical model** (V2 p.132, p.133, p.134)
The saving rate jumps from 20% to 30% with $\theta = 0.02$, $\alpha = 0.35$, $\delta = 0.10$, $n = 0.01$.
Q1: the steady-state per capita growth rate is unchanged at $\theta/(1-\alpha) = 0.02/0.65 = 3.08\%$ (Python: 3.077%).

**Example 12: neoclassical vs. endogenous growth models** (V2 p.137, p.138)

**Example 13: the entry of China and India into the global economy** (V2 p.143, p.144)

## Item-set patterns

> [!warning] Trap 1: earnings growth above GDP growth forever
> The vignette shows a market where the profit share of GDP and the P/E are above historical averages and a vendor extrapolates double-digit appreciation.
> Correct: over long horizons real earnings growth cannot exceed potential GDP growth, and the growth rates of the earnings-to-GDP share and the P/E must be approximately zero, so long-run appreciation anchors on nominal GDP growth and the profit share should revert (V2 p.92, p.96, p.163).
> Distractors let the P/E or profit-share trend continue ("remain near current level" or "increase") or pick short-horizon drivers (repricing) for a long-horizon question.

> [!warning] Trap 2: growth accounting weight swap
> A table gives capital growth, labor growth, TFP growth, and factor shares, and asks for potential GDP growth.
> Correct: weight capital growth by $\alpha$ (capital's income share) and labor growth by $1-\alpha$, then add TFP growth; for the United States $\alpha \approx 0.3$, so labor changes matter about twice as much (V2 p.99).
> Distractors swap the weights, weight TFP, or add unweighted input growth rates.

> [!warning] Trap 3: capital deepening measured wrong
> Productivity data give labor productivity growth and TFP growth and ask how important capital deepening is.
> Correct: capital deepening contribution = labor productivity growth minus TFP growth, and the larger the difference, the more the economy leans on deepening (V2 p.114, p.115, p.162).
> Distractors use TFP growth alone, use labor productivity growth alone, or compare hours-worked growth, and a paired conceptual distractor calls deepening "an upward shift in the production function" when it is a movement along it (V2 p.101).

> [!warning] Trap 4: level versus growth rate in the Solow model
> The vignette raises the saving rate (or opens capital inflows) and asks about long-run growth.
> Correct: in the neoclassical model a higher saving rate raises growth only during the transition and permanently raises only the levels of $k$, $y$, and labor productivity; steady-state growth stays at $\theta/(1-\alpha) + n$ (V2 p.131, p.132).
> Distractors claim a permanently higher growth rate (true only in the endogenous model) or no effect at all (wrong, the level and transition effects are real).

> [!warning] Trap 5: population versus participation versus hours
> Demographic data mix population growth, age shares, participation rates, and unemployment.
> Correct: population growth raises aggregate but not per capita growth; rising participation or falling unemployment can raise per capita growth but only as a transition to a new level, not a permanent growth-rate change (V2 p.105, p.106, p.109, p.163).
> Distractors credit population growth with per capita gains or extrapolate participation trends indefinitely.

> [!warning] Trap 6: the steady-state divisor
> A calculation question gives TFP growth, the labor share, and labor force growth.
> Correct: per capita growth is TFP growth divided by the labor share $1-\alpha$ (add $n$ only for total output growth) (V2 p.125).
> Distractors divide by $\alpha$, forget to divide at all, or add $n$ to the per capita figure; watch whether the labor share is given directly (0.561) or as $\alpha$.

> [!warning] Trap 7: convergence taxonomy
> A statement describes convergence with a qualifier and asks which hypothesis it is.
> Correct: absolute means poor countries catch up regardless of characteristics; conditional requires the same saving rate, population growth, and production function; club requires the right institutions, and non-members fall behind (V2 p.139, p.163, p.165).
> Distractors are simply the other two labels, and a second-layer trap asks which model rejects convergence (endogenous, because externalities keep rich countries ahead) (V2 p.140).

> [!warning] Trap 8: resource curse and Dutch disease
> A resource-rich country with good institutions grows slowly and the question asks the least likely explanation.
> Correct: Dutch disease is resource-export-driven currency appreciation crushing manufacturing competitiveness, so strong manufacturing exports argue against it; weak institutions are the other curse channel; ownership of resources is not required for growth since trade provides access (V2 p.103, p.104, p.164).
> Distractors treat resource abundance as automatically growth-positive or resource scarcity as binding, ignoring substitution and rising resource prices inducing efficiency (V2 p.104).

> [!warning] Trap 9: what trade opening does in each model
> Statements attribute permanent growth effects to openness.
> Correct: in the neoclassical model openness speeds convergence (capital flows to the capital-poor country, temporary above-steady-state growth, trade deficit) with no permanent growth change; endogenous models predict a permanently higher rate via selection, scale, and backwardness effects (V2 p.142, p.143, p.165).
> Distractors assign the permanent effect to the neoclassical model, or claim a low exchange rate policy is as powerful as capital deepening or technology investment (V2 p.164).

## Flashcards

#flashcards/cfa-l2/02-Economics/lm02

Potential GDP::The maximum output an economy can sustainably produce without raising the inflation rate; its growth rate caps sustainable growth (V2 p.92).
Why compare GDP across countries at PPP rather than market exchange rates?::Market rates are volatile and ignore cheap non-traded goods in developing countries, understating their living standards (V2 p.85, p.86).
Preconditions for growth (list)::Savings and investment, developed financial markets, rule of law and property rights, political stability, education and health, pro-entrepreneurship tax and regulation, open trade and capital flows (V2 p.86 to p.89, p.91).
Three ways financial intermediaries promote growth::Screen and monitor borrowers toward the best projects, create instruments for risk transfer and liquidity that encourage saving, and pool savings to ease credit constraints (V2 p.87).
Which education spending matters where?::Countries at the technology frontier need post-secondary spending for innovation; imitating countries gain most from primary and secondary education (V2 p.88).
Grinold-Kroner decomposition::Expected equity return equals dividend yield plus repricing plus inflation plus real growth minus change in shares outstanding (V2 p.92).
Which Grinold-Kroner terms must be about zero in the long run?::The repricing term and the earnings-to-GDP trend; neither the P/E nor the profit share can grow forever (V2 p.92).
Dilution effect components::{{Net buybacks (nbb)}} plus {{relative dynamism (rd)}}, growth accruing to firms not listed on the market (V2 p.93).
Why did China's 8.2% growth deliver only 0.7% real equity returns (1997-2017)?::Massive dilution from privatization of state-owned enterprises; the index went from 28 to 152 companies (V2 p.93, p.94).
Three fixed-income uses of potential GDP::Sovereign credit quality (slower potential growth raises risk), output-gap-driven monetary policy calls, and structural (cyclically adjusted) deficit assessment (V2 p.95).
Actual growth above potential implies what for bonds?::Upward inflation pressure, upward pressure on nominal rates, downward pressure on bond prices (V2 p.95).
Faster potential GDP growth does what to real interest rates?::Raises them; higher real rates are needed to induce the savings that fund capital accumulation (V2 p.95).
Production function::Y equals A times F(K, L); A is total factor productivity, a scale factor for the general level of technology (V2 p.97).
Cobb-Douglas production function::F(K, L) equals K to the alpha times L to the (1 minus alpha); constant returns to scale, diminishing marginal productivity per input (V2 p.97, p.98).
Why does alpha equal capital's income share?::Factors are paid marginal products; MPK equals alpha Y over K, set equal to the rental price r, so alpha equals rK over Y (V2 p.98).
Marginal product of capital in Cobb-Douglas::{{alpha Y / K}}, constant along the steady-state path and equal to the real interest rate (V2 p.98, p.125).
Per capita production function::y equals A times k to the alpha, output per worker as a function of TFP and capital per worker (V2 p.98).
Growth accounting equation::Output growth equals TFP growth plus alpha times capital growth plus (1 minus alpha) times labor growth (V2 p.99).
How is TFP growth measured?::As the Solow residual, output growth minus the share-weighted growth of capital and labor (V2 p.99).
US factor shares::Labor about 0.7, capital about 0.3, so labor growth moves output about twice as much as equal capital growth (V2 p.99).
Labor productivity growth accounting equation::Potential GDP growth equals long-term labor force growth plus long-term labor productivity growth (V2 p.100).
Trade-off of the labor productivity method::Simpler, no capital or TFP estimate needed, but its productivity term mixes capital deepening with TFP and is hard to project (V2 p.100, p.149).
Capital deepening::A rise in the capital-to-labor ratio, a movement along the per capita production function; happens while capital growth exceeds labor growth (V2 p.101).
When does capital deepening stop adding to growth?::Once the marginal product of capital equals its marginal cost, producers stop raising k faster than needed; in steady state deepening no longer moves growth (V2 p.101).
Technological (TFP) progress on the production function::A proportional upward shift of the entire curve, raising output per worker at every k and raising the MPK (V2 p.101, p.102).
Measure capital deepening from productivity data::Labor productivity growth minus TFP growth; the bigger the gap, the more growth relies on deepening (V2 p.114, p.115).
Resource curse channels::Failure to develop growth institutions, and Dutch disease (V2 p.104).
Dutch disease::Resource-export demand appreciates the currency, making manufacturing globally uncompetitive, so the country misses manufacturing-led TFP progress (V2 p.104).
Is owning natural resources necessary for growth?::No; access via trade suffices (South Korea, Singapore), and abundant owners (Venezuela, Nigeria) can stagnate (V2 p.103, p.104).
Four determinants of labor input growth::Population growth, labor force participation, net migration, average hours worked (V2 p.105).
Population growth affects which growth rate?::Aggregate GDP growth only; it does not change per capita GDP growth (V2 p.105).
Why treat participation-rate trends cautiously?::Rising or falling participation is a transition to a new level, not a permanent growth-rate change; do not extrapolate indefinitely (V2 p.106).
Human capital::Accumulated knowledge and skills from education, training, and experience; raises productivity directly and spills over into innovation (V2 p.110).
ICT versus non-ICT capital::ICT investment can raise potential growth through network externalities (captured mostly in TFP); non-ICT spending eventually becomes pure capital deepening (V2 p.112, p.113).
Public capital's role::Infrastructure has few substitutes, complements private production, and boosts the productivity of private investment beyond the direct project benefits (V2 p.117).
Classical (Malthusian) growth model::Population accelerates above subsistence income; diminishing returns to labor on fixed land pull per capita income back to subsistence, so technology yields a larger, not richer, population (V2 p.123).
Why did the classical prediction fail?::Population growth slowed as incomes rose, and technological progress outpaced diminishing returns (V2 p.123).
Neoclassical steady state definition::Balanced growth with a constant output-to-capital ratio; capital per worker and output per worker grow at the same rate (V2 p.124).
Steady-state growth of output per capita::{{theta / (1 minus alpha)}}, TFP growth scaled by labor's share; add n for total output growth (V2 p.125).
Equilibrium output-to-capital ratio::Psi equals (1 over s) times [theta/(1 minus alpha) plus delta plus n] (V2 p.125).
Steady-state saving must cover which three uses?::Equipping new workers (n), replacing depreciation (delta), and deepening capital at theta/(1 minus alpha) (V2 p.126).
Effect of a higher saving rate in the Solow model::Higher steady-state k and y levels and a temporary growth burst, but no change in the steady-state growth rate (V2 p.127, p.131, p.132).
Effect of faster labor force growth on the steady state::Lower capital-to-labor ratio and lower output per worker; the required investment line steepens (V2 p.128).
Effect of faster TFP growth on the steady state::Lower current k and y levels but a steeper growth path; output per worker grows faster from a lower base (V2 p.128).
What happens when the output-to-capital ratio exceeds Psi?::Actual investment exceeds required investment, growth runs above steady state, capital grows faster than output, and Y/K falls back toward Psi (V2 p.129, p.130).

Endogenous growth production function::y_e equals c times k_e, a straight line; broadened capital faces no diminishing aggregate returns because of knowledge externalities (V2 p.137).
Endogenous growth rate of per capita output::sc minus delta minus n, both short-run and long-run, so a higher saving rate raises growth permanently (V2 p.137).
Why do governments subsidize R&D under endogenous growth theory?::Knowledge spillovers make social returns exceed private returns, so companies under-invest; subsidies and direct spending correct the market failure (V2 p.137).
Absolute vs. conditional vs. club convergence::Absolute is catch-up regardless of characteristics; conditional requires the same saving rate, population growth, and production function; club requires the right institutions, and non-members fall behind (V2 p.139).
Which convergence does the neoclassical model actually imply?::Convergence of per capita growth rates for all (same technology), level convergence only conditionally; not absolute level convergence (V2 p.139).
Two mechanisms of convergence for developing countries::Capital deepening (high MPK at low k) and importing or imitating advanced technology, which requires spending to absorb it (V2 p.140).
Does the endogenous growth model predict convergence?::No; externalities let high-income countries grow as fast or faster indefinitely (V2 p.140).
Open-economy Solow adjustment sequence::Capital flows to the capital-poor country, it runs a trade deficit, grows above steady state temporarily, then returns falls, flows slow, and growth reverts to steady state with no permanent change (V2 p.142, p.143).

## Common mistakes

1. Comparing GDP across countries at market exchange rates instead of PPP, understating developing-country living standards through cheap non-traded goods (V2 p.85, p.86).
2. Letting the earnings-to-GDP share or the P/E ratio trend forever in a long-run forecast; both growth rates must be approximately zero over long horizons (V2 p.92, p.162).
3. Swapping the growth accounting weights: alpha (capital's share, about 0.3 in the US) multiplies capital growth, and 1 minus alpha multiplies labor growth (V2 p.99).
4. Measuring capital deepening with TFP growth or labor productivity growth alone instead of their difference (V2 p.114, p.162).
5. Calling capital deepening an upward shift of the production function; it is a movement along the curve, and only TFP progress shifts the curve (V2 p.101).
6. Claiming a higher saving rate permanently raises the neoclassical growth rate; it raises levels and gives only a transition burst, while the permanent-growth claim belongs to the endogenous model (V2 p.131, p.137).
7. Dividing TFP growth by capital's share instead of labor's share in the steady-state formula, or adding n to the per capita figure (V2 p.125).
8. Crediting population growth with per capita GDP growth; it raises only aggregate growth, while participation gains raise per capita output but only as level transitions (V2 p.105, p.106, p.163).
9. Treating resource ownership as necessary or automatically beneficial for growth, missing the institutional curse and Dutch disease channels and the trade-access point (V2 p.103, p.104).
10. Confusing the three convergence hypotheses, especially conditional (same saving rate, population growth, production function) with club (institutional membership) (V2 p.139, p.163, p.164).
11. Assigning a permanent trade-openness growth effect to the neoclassical model; it predicts faster convergence and a temporary boost only (V2 p.143, p.165).
12. Forgetting the 1/s factor in the equilibrium output-to-capital ratio, or the alpha factor in the transition-growth adjustment term (V2 p.125, p.129).
