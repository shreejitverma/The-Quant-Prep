# LM3: Application of the Code and Standards: Level II

Difficulty 3: no formulas and only two LOS, but the module is seven long integrative cases where several Standards fire at once, and the exam grades the precise sub-standard, the precise person, and the precise reason a near-miss is not a violation.

## LOS map

- **evaluate** practices, policies, and conduct relative to the CFA Institute Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct (V10 p.223) ^los-10-03-a
    - Examiner asks: given a vignette action, is it a violation at all, and if so exactly which sub-standard (I(A) through VII(B)) it breaches and by whom.
- **explain** how the practices, policies, and conduct do or do not violate the CFA Institute Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct (V10 p.223) ^los-10-03-b
    - Examiner asks: why the tempting "no violation" rationale fails (or why an accusation fails), what corrective action the person should take next, and what firm policy would have prevented the conduct.

## Core intuition

> [!abstract] The framework comes first, the Standard second
> The module opens with the ethical decision-making framework: Identify (facts, stakeholders and duties owed, ethical principles, conflicts of interest), Consider (situational influences, additional guidance, alternative actions), Decide and act, Reflect (V10 p.223).
> Every case is a chain of small decisions where somebody skipped a step, usually Identify (whose duty is owed to whom) or Consider (what a disinterested observer would think of the arrangement).
> The last two cases (Syyark and Agarway) go one step further and ask you to prescribe corrective actions and a preventive firm policy, not just to spot the breach (V10 p.224).

This module contains no new rules.
Everything is the LM2 guidance applied under time pressure to messy fact patterns, so the study skill is mapping actions to sub-standards fast and resisting the vignette's own self-justifications.
Almost every wrong answer choice in the knowledge checks is a rationalization spoken by a character in the case, which is exactly how the real exam builds distractors.

The first recurring mechanism is fair dealing versus differentiated service.
Standard III(B) does not require equal treatment; it requires that different service levels be disclosed, be available to everyone willing to pay, and never disadvantage other clients (V10 p.227).
Bashar's premium subscription violates III(B) only because she offers it selectively to clients she privately judges able to afford it, not because a premium tier exists (V10 p.227).
The mirror images are the no-violation findings: calling the two largest clients after a recommendation is publicly posted on the firm website is permitted extra service (V10 p.241, p.242), inviting them to IPO roadshows is fine when other clients are not disadvantaged in the allocation itself (V10 p.242), a discounted fee for referred clients is fine because fair dealing is about recommendations and investment action, not fee schedules (V10 p.250, p.251), and using a quant model only for institutional clients is fine because suitability differs across client types (V10 p.245).
The violation reappears the moment information or access is selective before public dissemination: Olatunji tipping Bashar on the SWNB strong buy before all clients hear it, and buying it into her account early, is III(B) at its core (V10 p.233, p.234), and Syyark selling his own tokens only to his largest clients is III(B) again because the limited float puts everyone else at an economic disadvantage (V10 p.264, p.265).

The second mechanism is the most-restrictive-law rule of I(A).
Members who practice in multiple jurisdictions follow applicable law when it is stricter than the Code and Standards and follow the Code and Standards when they are stricter than the law (V10 p.239).
Banco Libertad turns this into a grid: applicable law is where the client resides, so Sanchez may not post investment information on social media for Panaguay clients, both managers may not execute trades over instant messaging for Urutina clients, and neither may hand confidential client files to law enforcement from Chiladour or Panaguay where disclosure is prohibited, however disgusted they feel (V10 p.239, p.240).
The same rule cuts the other way for referral fees: no country in the case requires disclosure, but the Code does, and the Code is stricter, so the undisclosed lawyer arrangements violate VI(C) and I(A) together (V10 p.241).
I(A) also carries the participation rule: you are responsible for violations in which you knowingly participate or assist, and the dissociation ladder runs from confronting the actor, to escalating to a supervisor or compliance or senior management, to stepping away, with leaving the firm as the extreme end and contacting clients directly never an intermediate step (V10 p.234, p.246).

> [!abstract] Disclosure has a clock and an addressee
> Most conflict questions are not about whether something is allowed but about when and to whom it must be disclosed.
> Referral benefits under VI(C) must be disclosed to employer, clients, and prospective clients before the client signs, so JRA's disclosure in supplemental policies sent after the investment management agreement is too late (V10 p.251).
> Additional compensation under IV(B) needs written consent from all parties including the employer, so a beach house or hotel week for beating the benchmark that is documented only in the client's IPS still violates IV(B) (V10 p.242, p.243).
> Conflicts under VI(A) must be avoided or disclosed prominently, in plain language, before clients rely on the research, which is why the Gbeho report without mention of Bashar's family ownership is a violation even though the researchers themselves were unaware (V10 p.227, p.228).

The third mechanism is the supervisor chain of IV(C).
An employee's breach almost always drags in the supervisor who made no reasonable effort to prevent it: Maduro approved the social media practice that was illegal for Panaguay clients (V10 p.239, p.240), Omondi co-located the proprietary and client desks so client order flow leaked despite a paper policy that said the right words (V10 p.258, p.259), and Olatunji, who ordered his traders to wash trade, violated IV(C) toward his own traders on top of II(B) himself (V10 p.235).
A policy that exists but does not work does not protect the supervisor; Omondi's need-to-know policy was ineffective by construction, and his assurance to clients that information was confidential became a I(C) misrepresentation (V10 p.258, p.259).

The fourth mechanism is the boundary of each duty, which is where the trickiest no-violation answers live.
Kariuki photographing the discarded OFS client list is I(D) Misconduct, not II(A), because a client contact list is confidential but not material nonpublic information about a security (V10 p.231, p.232).
Grant, not Bashar, violates III(A) in their phone call, because the duty to seek best price and best execution sits with the asset manager directing the trades, not with the research firm suggesting a broker (V10 p.229, p.230).
Othan's USD500 per transferred client is neither a referral fee (it is employer-to-employee compensation, not payment for recommending services) nor additional compensation creating a conflict, so nothing requires disclosure to clients (V10 p.254).
Olatunji escapes VI(B) Priority of Transactions on the wash trades because he was not a beneficial owner in those transactions; the violations are II(B) and IV(C) (V10 p.235).
And under II(A), information need not be definite to be material: a CFO telling Kirabo about a board offer for a subsidiary is material because of its source, substance, and specificity, and running the tip through an analyst's model does not launder it (V10 p.261).

Finally, the constructed-response cases rehearse the full prescription.
Syyark stacks I(E) Competence (recommending cryptocurrency while still learning), III(B) (selling his own tokens only to large clients), III(C) Suitability (a uniform 3% position for all clients, and testing the idea on his smallest clients first), IV(B) (mining income competing with his bank employer without written consent), V(A) (two weekends of research as a reasonable basis), and VI(A) (owning and mining the thinly traded asset he recommends) (V10 p.264 to p.267).
Agarway stacks I(A) (a marketing strategy aimed at preteens that may be illegal), I(B) (founders pressuring the due diligence process and acceptance rate), II(B) (paying influencers to post unfounded acquisition and product rumors), and VI(A) (personal holdings in potential competitors of platform companies) (V10 p.270 to p.272).
For each, the graded answer is a triple: name the standard, state the corrective action, and write a one-paragraph firm policy that would have prevented it.

^f-10-03-1

Three decision rules compress the table.
First, when jurisdictions differ, follow the strictest of applicable law and the Code and Standards, with applicable law defined in this module by client residence (V10 p.239).
Second, differentiated service is legal when disclosed, available to all, and harmless to other clients; selective information, selective allocation, or selective access to a limited asset is a III(B) violation (V10 p.227, p.264).
Third, a conflict or benefit is cured only by disclosure that is prior, prominent, and addressed to everyone owed the duty (employer, clients, prospects); after-the-fact, buried, or partial disclosure fails (V10 p.242, p.251).

## Worked examples

There are no calculations in this module.
Each worked example walks one case's full knowledge check with the curriculum's solution reasoning.

**Example 1: Serengeti Advisory Services, Questions 1 to 12** (V10 p.227 to p.236)

Q1, premium subscription: violation, A.
Offering the premium tier only to clients Bashar believes can afford it breaches III(B); tiers must be disclosed and available to everyone, and charging more for more service is otherwise fine (V10 p.227).
Q2, Gbeho report: violation, C.
Publishing without disclosing the family's ownership breaches VI(A); the researchers' ignorance of the conflict does not erase it, and the interest is significant, so "not significant" and "research was independent" both fail (V10 p.227, p.228).
Q3, third-party research procedures: Procedure 2 violates, B.
Removing qualifying statements from others' forecasts breaches I(C); naming quoted experts (Procedure 1) and citing chart sources (Procedure 3) are required anti-plagiarism practices, not violations (V10 p.228).
Q4, referral arrangement: no violation, A.
Bashar disclosed the arrangement, no quid pro quo benefit flows, and the asset manager clients make the final routing decision; best execution duties belong to the managers, not to her (V10 p.229).
Q5, the phone call: Grant violates, A.
Grant routes every client trade on Bashar's suggestion without seeking best price and best execution, breaching III(A); Bashar merely encouraged use of disclosed research partners (V10 p.229, p.230).
Q6, forum invitation: Option 3, C.
Serengeti must pay its own air travel and hotel to protect I(B); accepting the forum's meals and entertainment is acceptable under the guidance's best-practice rule on commercial transportation (V10 p.230, p.231).
Q7, the recycling-bin list: violation of I(D), B.
Using misappropriated confidential information is dishonesty under I(D); it is not II(A) because a client contact and volume list would not move a security's price, and the careless discloser separately implicates III(E) (V10 p.231, p.232).
Q8, the model portfolio: no violation, C.
Bashar may open a discretionary account at OFS and stay silent to her clients, and Olatunji trades her account alongside other discretionary clients with service any client could request, so neither I(B), VI(A), nor III(B) is breached (V10 p.232, p.233).
Q9, the SWNB tip to Bashar: Fair Dealing, A.
Telling one client about a strong buy before dissemination denies other clients a fair opportunity to act, breaching III(B); confidentiality and client communication standards are not the issue (V10 p.233).
Q10, pre-report accumulation: both violate, C.
Olatunji breaches III(B) by buying before dissemination; Bashar breaches I(A) because she knowingly participates, and she should reject the trade, require unwinding, and report the violations (V10 p.234).
Q11, the trading instructions: the exception is VI(B), B.
The simultaneous buy-and-sell orders are wash trading under II(B), and as the traders' supervisor Olatunji breaches IV(C); VI(B) is not violated because he is not a beneficial owner of the traded shares (V10 p.235).
Q12, tipping Grant: the exception is III(E), C.
Bashar breaches III(B) (one client told first) and VI(B) (her own position established before clients could act), but no client confidence was disclosed, so III(E) survives (V10 p.235, p.236).
Note the curriculum misprint in the Q12 stem: option B reads "Standard V(B) Priority of Transactions", but Priority of Transactions is VI(B), and the solution text cites VI(B) (V10 p.236). ^ex-10-03-1

**Example 2: Banco Libertad, Questions 1 to 7** (V10 p.239 to p.243)

Q1, social media: only Sanchez violates, B.
Applicable law is the client's residence; Panaguay prohibits posting investment information on social media and is stricter than the Code, so Sanchez breaches I(A), while Ortiz's jurisdictions allow it (V10 p.239).
Q2, the supervisor: Maduro violates through Sanchez, B.
By praising and permitting the practice she failed IV(C); she must ensure through education and training that Sanchez complies with Panaguayan law (V10 p.239, p.240).
Q3, IM and police disclosures: both are violations, A.
Urutina prohibits IM trade executions and both managers have Urutina clients, and Chiladour and Panaguay prohibit disclosing client information to law enforcement, so each act breaches applicable stricter law and therefore I(A) (V10 p.240, p.241).
Q4, lawyer referrals: violation, A.
Tickets and dinners are benefits paid for recommendations and must be disclosed to employer, clients, and prospects under VI(C); local law requiring no disclosure is irrelevant because the Code is stricter, and telling Maduro after the fact is too late (V10 p.241).
Q5, calling the two largest clients: no violation, A.
The recommendation changes are already publicly posted on the firm website, so the calls are permissible differentiated service under III(B) (V10 p.241, p.242).
Q6, roadshow invitations: no violation, A.
Attendance gives Fabre and Zeissl no allocation priority, and all suitable clients keep access to the IPOs, so no one is disadvantaged (V10 p.242).
Q7, performance perks: violation, A.
The beach house and hotel weeks are additional compensation contingent on account performance; IV(B) demands written consent from all parties, and the clients' IPS documentation does not substitute for the employer's written consent (V10 p.242, p.243). ^ex-10-03-2

**Example 3: QuantHouse, Questions 1 to 7** (V10 p.245 to p.248)

Q1, institutional-only ATM: no violation, A.
Different models for different client types reflects suitability; III(B) governs dissemination and investment action, not product menus (V10 p.245).
Q2, update cadence: violation, A.
Updating the Alpha and Risk Models quarterly while reviewing the Optimizer and the whole model annually fails V(A); every component and their interactions need the quarterly review (V10 p.245, p.246).
Q3, next step: senior management, C.
The I(A) dissociation ladder runs supervisor, then compliance or senior management, then stepping away; contacting clients is not an intermediate step and leaving the firm is the extreme end (V10 p.246).
Q4, delaying the fix: violation, A.
Leaving a known error until quarter-end, with risk factors disabled, is disloyal to clients under III(A); the supervisor's order does not excuse it (V10 p.246, p.247).
Q5, the cover story: violation, A.
Attributing underperformance to volatility and the (disabled) risk factors is a knowing misrepresentation under I(C) (V10 p.247).
Q6, StockStar: no violation, A.
Built on personal time, used only for personal and family accounts, and uncompensated, the model raises no IV(A) issue (V10 p.247, p.248).
Q7, the performance table: no violation, A.
Actual and backtested returns are separately labeled with the past-performance caveat, which satisfies III(D); showing backtests is allowed with clear disclosure (V10 p.248). ^ex-10-03-3

**Example 4: Jacobs, Riccio, and Associates, Questions 1 to 9** (V10 p.250 to p.255)

Q1, discounted fees: no violation, A.
Fee discounts for referred clients are neither misrepresentation (the brochure fee is genuinely charged to some and is the maximum) nor unfair dealing (no discrimination in recommendations or investment action) (V10 p.250, p.251).
Q2, disclosure timing: violation, A.
VI(C) requires disclosure before entry into the formal agreement; supplemental policies mailed after signing are too late, and the BP lawyers' own disclosures do not discharge JRA's duty (V10 p.251).
Q3, information sharing with E&O: violation, A.
Swapping tax returns and account statements without advance client approval breaches III(E); separate confidentiality agreements with each firm are not consent to share between firms (V10 p.251, p.252).
Q4, Frontline arrangement: violation, A.
The referral-for-directed-trading arrangement must be disclosed to referred clients under VI(C) even though best price and best execution continue (V10 p.252, p.253).
Q5, charity donations: violation of I(B), B.
The donations incentivize the pension consultant gatekeeper and at least appear to compromise his objectivity; they are not IV(B) additional compensation because they are not compensation to Carroll himself (V10 p.253).
Q6, contacting old clients: no violation, A.
Sack permitted personal social media connections, and Othan contacted clients only after resigning, so IV(A) is satisfied (V10 p.253, p.254).
Q7, the USD500 per client: no violation, A.
It is employer-to-employee compensation for services, not a referral fee paid for recommending products or services and not conflict-creating additional compensation, so no client disclosure is required (V10 p.254).
Q8, the chatroom: Comment 2 violates, B.
Naming the contango-backwardation question and the derivatives topic discloses confidential exam content under VII(A); calling ethics hard or graders lenient discloses nothing confidential (V10 p.254, p.255).
Q9, "newest CFA": violation, A.
Ode has passed Level III but lacks the required work experience, so she is not a charterholder; Othan misrepresents her under VII(B) and Ode must correct him (V10 p.255). ^ex-10-03-4

**Example 5: Magadi Asset Management, Questions 1 to 8** (V10 p.258 to p.262)

Q1, the trading floor: violation, A.
Main-desk traders let proprietary traders see and hear client order information, breaching III(E); internal sharing beyond need-to-know is still a breach, and whether the proprietary desk acted is irrelevant (V10 p.258).
Q2, the supervisor: violation, A.
Omondi's co-location and ineffective policy fail IV(C), and his confidentiality assurance to clients becomes a I(C) misrepresentation (V10 p.258, p.259).
Q3, the sovereign wealth fund win: both acts violate, C.
Charitable donations to the fund's top managers and the sham subadviser with undocumented deal fees breach I(B), and the arrangement also implicates I(A) (anti-bribery law), I(C) (mislabeled expenses), V(A) (no reasonable basis), and V(C) (no records) (V10 p.259, p.260).
Q4, disclosure of changes: Change 2 is exempt, B.
Raising the cash cap from 10% to 15% and concentrating all decisions in Kirabo are material mandate and process changes requiring V(B) disclosure; going from 11 to 13 countries stays within the mandate (V10 p.260).
Q5, the track record: violation, B.
Prior-firm performance may be shown only with disclosure of where it was earned and the manager's role; omitting the firm name breaches III(D), and taking the record without written permission would add IV(A) (V10 p.260, p.261).
Q6, whom to tell: current and prospective clients, C (V10 p.261, p.262).
Q7, Kirabo's purchases: violation, A.
The CFO's tip about the board's BWP500 million subsidiary offer is material despite being indefinite, because of its source, substance, and specificity, and the analyst's revised model was built on it, so buying breaches II(A) (V10 p.261).
Q8, Omondi's purchases: violation, A.
Omondi bought for personal and family accounts on the same material nonpublic information, breaching II(A); neither indefiniteness nor the pending board vote saves him (V10 p.261, p.262). ^ex-10-03-5

**Example 6: Syyark, constructed response** (V10 p.264 to p.268)

The graded answer names six violations, each with a corrective action and a firm policy.
I(E) Competence: he recommended Meerine while still learning; he must achieve competence first through training, conferences, or certifications, and the firm should require an investment-committee competence review before any new-product recommendation (V10 p.264).
III(B) Fair Dealing: he offered direct token sales only to his largest clients in a limited-liquidity market; he must offer all clients pro rata allocation or sell to none, and the firm should mandate block execution with pro rata allocation at one price (V10 p.264, p.265).
III(C) Suitability: a uniform 3% position for every client, and an initial 1% test on his smallest clients chosen to limit his own risk rather than by their objectives, ignore client-specific circumstances; each recommendation must be fitted to risk tolerance, objectives, and constraints (V10 p.265).
IV(B) Additional Compensation Arrangements: mining income competes with his bank employer's transaction business; he needs written employer consent before mining, and the firm should require disclosure and written permission for outside compensation (V10 p.266).
V(A) Diligence and Reasonable Basis: two weekends of research is rudimentary and rushed; he should produce a written evaluation of risks and benefits, and the firm should restrict recommendations to an approved list backed by written research (V10 p.266, p.267).
VI(A) Avoid or Disclose Conflicts: he owns and mines the thinly traded asset he recommends, and client buying directly lifts his position; he must avoid the conflict or disclose it prominently in plain language before clients rely on the advice, and selling his own tokens to clients should be avoided outright (V10 p.267).
The solution's Actions Required paragraph for the conflict opens with "Standard I(E) requires Syyark to avoid or clearly disclose", a curriculum typo for VI(A), which the surrounding discussion makes clear (V10 p.267). ^ex-10-03-6

**Example 7: Agarway, constructed response** (V10 p.270 to p.272)

Four violations or possible violations, again with actions and policies.
I(A) Knowledge of the Law: Deko markets crowdfunded shares through emails to preteen and teen users, and collecting information on minors online without parental permission is illegal in many countries; Agarway must confirm compliance before listing, and the firm should require legal counsel review whenever a strategy targets minors or vulnerable adults (V10 p.270, p.271).
I(B) Independence and Objectivity: the founders' pressure for a 10% acceptance rate and faster reviews threatens the integrity of due diligence; the firm needs a board-approved, documented due diligence process Agarway can apply objectively (V10 p.271).
II(B) Market Manipulation: Agarway pays influencers to post positive comments and himself spreads unfounded talk of imminent features and a possible acquisition, information-based manipulation intended to lift Deko; public statements must be factual, research-based, and compliance-cleared (V10 p.271).
VI(A) Avoid or Disclose Conflicts: his personal investments in potential competitors of platform companies must be divested or disclosed to his employer and, if the companies list, to platform users; the firm should require quarterly holdings disclosure and pre-approval of employee investments (V10 p.272). ^ex-10-03-7

## Item-set patterns

> [!warning] Trap: the right verdict under the wrong sub-standard
> The vignette makes the violation obvious but offers two plausible standards, such as the discarded client list (I(D) Misconduct, not II(A) MNPI) or the SWNB tip (III(B) Fair Dealing, not III(E) Confidentiality or V(B) Communication) (V10 p.231, p.233).
> Correct approach: ask what duty the information itself engages; client identity data engages honesty and confidentiality, price-moving security information engages II(A), and pre-release recommendations engage III(B).
> The distractor standard always shares a keyword with the fact pattern, which is why it tempts.

> [!warning] Trap: disclosure cures versus prohibition
> Some conduct is fine once disclosed (referral arrangements, VI(C); conflicts, VI(A); differentiated service tiers, III(B)), while some is prohibited no matter what is disclosed (wash trading II(B), trading on MNPI II(A), stripping caveats I(C)) (V10 p.229, p.235, p.261).
> Correct approach: first classify the act as disclosable or prohibited; only then evaluate the timing and addressee of any disclosure.
> Distractors claim a prohibited act was "disclosed to the client" or that a disclosable act is an automatic violation.

> [!warning] Trap: the most-restrictive-law grid
> A two-country exhibit lists what each jurisdiction allows and prohibits, then asks who violated I(A) (V10 p.237, p.239).
> Correct approach: fix the applicable law for each person (here, client residence), then apply the stricter of that law and the Code and Standards act by act.
> Distractors apply the manager's home law to everyone, or forget that the Code itself can be the stricter rule, as with undisclosed referral benefits where no local law requires disclosure (V10 p.241).

> [!warning] Trap: supervisor versus employee responsibility
> When an employee breaches, the item set asks separately about the supervisor, and the tempting answer is that a policy existed (V10 p.258).
> Correct approach: IV(C) requires reasonable efforts that actually work, so an ineffective policy, praise for the conduct, or ignorance from unreviewed files still condemns the supervisor (Maduro, Omondi).
> Distractors: "no violation because a firm policy prohibited it" and "no violation because the supervisor did not know".

> [!warning] Trap: performance presentation and prior records
> Backtested results, prior-firm track records, and model performance are all allowed under III(D) if actual and simulated are clearly separated and the venue and role are disclosed (V10 p.248, p.260).
> Correct approach: look for the missing disclosure, not the data type; Singh's table passes, Kirabo's brochure fails only because the prior firm and his solo role are unnamed.
> Distractors assert backtests are banned or that a prior-firm record can never be shown.

> [!warning] Trap: fair dealing versus premium service
> Every case features favored big clients, and the answer flips on one fact: whether the favored access came before or after public dissemination and whether other clients are economically disadvantaged (V10 p.227, p.241, p.264).
> Correct approach: post-publication calls and roadshow seats are fine; selective pre-release tips, selective allocation of a scarce asset, and tiers offered only to some clients violate III(B).
> Distractors: "no violation because they are the largest clients" and "violation because others were not called".

> [!warning] Trap: what counts as a referral fee or additional compensation
> VI(C) covers benefits exchanged for recommending products or services, and IV(B) covers third-party benefits conflicting with the employer's interest; employer-paid bonuses are neither (V10 p.254).
> Correct approach: trace who pays whom and for what; JRA paying Othan USD500 per client is compensation for services, while lawyers' tickets and Carroll's donations are inducements to refer.
> Distractors relabel employer compensation as a referral fee, or charitable donations as IV(B) compensation when the issue is really I(B) influence (V10 p.253).

> [!warning] Trap: escalation order under I(A)
> When a member discovers a colleague's or supervisor's unethical plan, the answer choices are dissociate, tell clients, or escalate internally (V10 p.246).
> Correct approach: attempt to stop the conduct via supervisor, compliance, or senior management first; step away or resign only if that fails; contacting clients directly is never the prescribed intermediate step.
> Distractors make immediate resignation or client notification look principled.

> [!warning] Trap: materiality without certainty
> A vignette insider shares indefinite news (an unaccepted offer, a pending vote), and a model or analyst intermediates the trade (V10 p.261).
> Correct approach: materiality turns on source, substance, and specificity, not on certainty, and information laundered through a model or a colleague still contaminates the action under II(A).
> Distractors: "not definite", "the board has not voted", and "the decision came from the analyst's model".

## Self-test

**Vignette A.**
Priya Nair, CFA, manages private clients at Helvia Bank from country X, where applicable law is the client's residence.
Her clients live in X, where posting recommendations to a public web page is allowed and messaging apps for order confirmations are prohibited, and in country Y, where the web posting is prohibited and messaging apps are allowed.
Nair posts all recommendation changes to Helvia's public research page, then immediately phones her two largest clients (both in X) to discuss the changes, and confirms all executed orders to every client through a messaging app.
Her supervisor, Aldo Brun, CFA, congratulates her on the workflow in the firm newsletter.
A referring notary in Y receives opera tickets from Nair each season, disclosed to Brun in writing and to referred prospects in the second meeting, after they sign the advisory agreement.
Nair's largest client offers her family a ski chalet week in any year his account beats its benchmark, recorded in the client's IPS; Brun gives verbal approval.

1. Nair's public web posting of recommendations most likely violates the Code and Standards with respect to:
A. no clients.
B. her clients in Y only.
C. all clients, because the page is public.

2. The phone calls to her two largest clients most likely:
A. violate III(B) Fair Dealing.
B. violate II(A) Material Nonpublic Information.
C. violate no standard.

3. The messaging-app confirmations most likely violate:
A. I(A) with respect to clients in X.
B. I(A) with respect to clients in Y.
C. no standard, because confirmations are not recommendations.

4. The opera ticket arrangement most likely violates VI(C) because:
A. gifts to referral sources are prohibited.
B. disclosure to prospects occurred after they signed the agreement.
C. only cash compensation must be disclosed.

5. The chalet arrangement most likely violates IV(B) because:
A. performance-linked client gifts are prohibited outright.
B. the IPS documentation is invalid without a notary.
C. written consent from all parties, including the employer, was not obtained.

**Vignette B.**
At Quarry Capital, analyst Devi covers a mining firm whose CFO tells her privately that a divestiture term sheet reaches the board next week.
Devi updates her model with the divestiture proceeds, and the model turns bullish; she tells her PM, Ross, "the model says buy", without naming the source.
Ross buys for client accounts.
Marketing drafts a brochure showing Devi's backtested strategy returns next to her live returns from a prior employer, labeled "simulated" and "actual" respectively, with no mention of the prior firm or her role there.
Quarry's head trader seats the firm's proprietary trader beside the client desk "to share best practices"; the firm handbook states client orders are confidential.
Junior analyst Kim, who passed Level III in August and has two years of experience, signs research notes "Kim, CFA, pending".
Compliance head Vora learns everything above in one afternoon.

6. Ross's client purchases most likely:
A. violate II(A), because the model's signal embeds material nonpublic information.
B. do not violate II(A), because Ross never heard the CFO's information.
C. violate II(B) Market Manipulation.

7. Devi's own conduct most likely violates:
A. II(A) only.
B. II(A) and, by causing others to act, the prohibition extends through her.
C. no standard, because she only updated a model.

8. The brochure most likely violates III(D) because:
A. backtested results may never appear beside actual results.
B. the prior firm and Devi's role in the actual record are not disclosed.
C. simulated results must be net of fees.

9. The seating arrangement most likely means:
A. only the proprietary trader violates III(E).
B. the client-desk traders and the head trader risk violating III(E) and IV(C) respectively, despite the handbook.
C. no violation, because the handbook prohibits misuse.

10. Kim's signature most likely:
A. violates VII(B), because no partial or pending designation may be used.
B. is acceptable, because Level III is passed.
C. violates VII(A), because it discloses exam information.

> [!success]- Answer key
> 1. B.
> Applicable law is the client's residence; Y prohibits the public posting, and the stricter law binds under I(A) for Y clients only, so A understates and C overstates the reach.
> 2. C.
> The changes were publicly posted before the calls, so the calls are permitted premium service under III(B) (the Fabre and Zeissl logic, V10 p.241, p.242); A ignores the prior public posting and B mislabels firm research as MNPI.
> 3. A.
> X prohibits messaging-app confirmations and Nair has X clients, so I(A) is violated for them (the Banco Libertad IM logic, V10 p.240); B picks the wrong jurisdiction because Y allows the practice, and C is wrong because the prohibition covers trade communications, not just recommendations.
> 4. B.
> VI(C) requires disclosure to prospects before entry into the formal agreement (V10 p.251); A is false because disclosed benefits are allowed, and C is false because any compensation, consideration, or benefit counts.
> 5. C.
> IV(B) demands written consent from all parties, and Brun's verbal approval plus client-side IPS documentation falls short (V10 p.242, p.243); A is false because such arrangements are permitted with consent, and B invents a formality.
> 6. A.
> Acting on analysis that embeds material nonpublic information violates II(A) even at one remove, exactly as with the Mtume model output (V10 p.261); B fails because the taint travels with the signal Devi caused him to act on, and C mislabels the conduct.
> 7. B.
> II(A) prohibits acting or causing others to act, and Devi caused Ross to act by passing the contaminated recommendation; A misses the causation limb, and C ignores that the model update was itself use of the information.
> 8. B.
> III(D) permits prior records and simulations only with disclosure of venue and role (the Kirabo logic, V10 p.260, p.261); A is false because labeled coexistence is fine (the Singh table, V10 p.248), and C invents a rule where clear disclosure of gross-of-fees suffices.
> 9. B.
> Client-desk traders must keep order information from need-to-know outsiders including colleagues, and the head trader's ineffective arrangement fails IV(C) despite the handbook (the Magadi logic, V10 p.258, p.259); A blames the wrong desk first, and C repeats the paper-policy fallacy.
> 10. A.
> VII(B) prohibits using any partial designation; passing all levels without the experience requirement confers nothing (the Ode logic, V10 p.255); B repeats Othan's error, and C confuses the sub-standards.

## Common mistakes

1. Treating every favor to a big client as a Fair Dealing violation; III(B) is breached only by selective pre-release information, selective allocation, or tiers not offered to all, not by post-publication calls, roadshow seats, or fee discounts (V10 p.241, p.242, p.250).
2. Grabbing the standard that shares a keyword with the vignette, such as answering II(A) for the stolen client list (it is I(D)) or III(E) for the selective tip (it is III(B)) (V10 p.231, p.233).
3. Forgetting that the Code and Standards can be the stricter rule under I(A); no local disclosure requirement still means referral benefits must be disclosed (V10 p.241).
4. Applying the manager's home law to every client instead of fixing applicable law client by client before applying the most-restrictive rule (V10 p.239, p.240).
5. Excusing the supervisor because a policy existed; IV(C) requires reasonable efforts that are actually effective, and co-location or public praise of the practice defeats a paper policy (V10 p.258, p.259).
6. Accepting client-side documentation (an IPS entry, a client's written acknowledgment) as a substitute for the employer's written consent under IV(B) (V10 p.242, p.243).
7. Calling employer-paid per-client bonuses referral fees; VI(C) covers benefits exchanged with third parties for recommendations, not the employer's own compensation (V10 p.254).
8. Believing indefinite information cannot be material; source, substance, and specificity make an unvoted board offer material under II(A), and routing it through a model or an analyst changes nothing (V10 p.261).
9. Making dissociation or client notification the first response to a blocked fix; the ladder is supervisor, then compliance or senior management, then stepping away (V10 p.246).
10. Banning backtests or prior-firm records outright; III(D) permits both with clear labeling, the venue, and the manager's role disclosed (V10 p.248, p.260).
11. Assuming disclosure timing is a technicality; VI(C) disclosure after the client signs is a violation, full stop (V10 p.251).
12. Treating "the researchers did not know about the conflict" as a defense; an unknown conflict still exists and still requires avoidance or disclosure (V10 p.228).
