# LM1: Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct

Difficulty 2: no calculation and only two LOS, but the exam tests the exact wording and the exact boundary between neighbouring standards, so the memorisation must be precise rather than approximate.

## LOS map

- **describe** the six components of the Code of Ethics and the seven Standards of Professional Conduct (V10 p.3) ^los-10-01-a
    - Examiner asks: which quoted sentence is the complete text of a Code principle, or which sub-standard (for example II(A) versus II(B), III(A) versus VI(A)) a described obligation actually belongs to.
- **explain** the ethical responsibilities required of CFA Institute members and candidates in the CFA Program by the Code and Standards (V10 p.3) ^los-10-01-b
    - Examiner asks: what the Professional Conduct Program can and cannot do at each stage (inquiry source, investigation, staff disposition, DRC panel, sanction) and what a firm or nonmember must say when it claims compliance.

## Core intuition

> [!abstract] The Code is the aspiration, the Standards are the conduct
> The Code of Ethics contains high-level aspirational principles that push members toward a positive and reputable profession, and the Standards contain the practical rules of conduct members must follow to get there (V10 p.4).
> The curriculum insists they be read as an "interwoven tapestry", not as isolated principles, because applying one principle alone rarely captures the complexity of a real dilemma (V10 p.4).

Start with why a professional body needs a code at all.
Society benefits when capital flows freely to its most productive use, and that only happens when investors trust that markets are fair and transparent and that risk is rewarded (V10 p.9).
Laws, regulations, and enforcement are necessary but are explicitly "insufficient alone" to guarantee that trust, so the markets depend on an ethical foundation to guide participants' judgment and behaviour (V10 p.9).
CFA Institute's stated purpose in maintaining the Code and Standards is to create a culture of ethics "for the ultimate benefit of society", and that phrase is part of the Institute's mission statement (V10 p.9, p.10).
Hold on to that phrase, because practice problem 2 tests whether you know that the fifth Code principle ends with it (V10 p.14).

The curriculum defines ethics as a set of moral principles or rules of conduct that provide guidance for our behaviour when it affects others, and it names four fundamental principles: honesty, fairness, diligence, and care and respect for others (V10 p.9).
Ethical conduct balances self-interest against both the direct and indirect consequences of behaviour for other people (V10 p.9).
The economic mechanism behind this is trust priced as a risk premium.
If investors believe professionals cannot be trusted with their assets or that only insiders win, they will not invest, or they will demand a higher risk premium, and reduced capital means less innovation, fewer jobs, and a smaller investment industry (V10 p.10).
That is why unethical conduct by one participant is a negative externality on the whole industry, and why ethics for a global industry must be universal rather than deferring to local or regional custom (V10 p.10).

> [!abstract] Legal is what is required; ethical is what is right
> The curriculum distinguishes legal behaviour ("what is required") from ethical behaviour ("conduct that is morally correct"), and states that ethical principles go beyond what is legally sufficient (V10 p.11).
> Compliance with regulation alone is insufficient to earn investor trust; firms need a "culture of integrity" that permeates all levels (V10 p.10).

Why can regulation not do the job on its own?
Regulators often lack the resources to enforce well-conceived rules, laws do not cover all unethical behaviour, and some individuals will always succeed at circumventing rules for personal gain (V10 p.11).
Knowing which rule applies is important but not sufficient; individuals must be able to recognise situations prone to ethical pitfalls and identify the influences that impair judgment (V10 p.11).
That is the argument for an ethical decision-making framework: a structured internal thought process before acting, so that a dilemma leads neither to equivocation nor to fraudulent conduct (V10 p.11).
Investment professionals habitually analyse decisions from a profit-and-loss outlook, and the framework forces a parallel analysis from an ethical perspective in the presence of the conflicts of interest that are routine in research, recommendation, and money management (V10 p.11).

Two extensions of this idea matter for the exam.
First, market sustainability: apparently isolated and innocuous decisions can, in aggregate, precipitate a market crisis in an interconnected global system, so those in positions of authority carry a special responsibility when approving risk-management and product-development policies, and compensation strategies must not push otherwise ethical people into questionable conduct for financial gain (V10 p.10, p.11).
Second, the firm: a code of ethics risks becoming a "dusty compilation" unless it is integrated into the business, and the curriculum says that senior management's development, maintenance, and demonstration of a culture of integrity "may be the single most important factor" in promoting ethical behaviour among employees (V10 p.12).
A code is described as necessary but insufficient; ethical decision-making must be practised until it is "muscle memory" (V10 p.12).

Now the documents themselves.
The Code and Standards were created in the 1960s, and members and candidates have been required to abide by them for more than 60 years (V10 p.12, p.13).
They apply regardless of job function, cultural differences, or local laws and regulations, and every member and candidate has a personal responsibility to uphold them and is encouraged to notify their employer of that responsibility (V10 p.13).
The Preamble lists the sanctions in headline form: revocation of membership, revocation of candidacy in the CFA Program, and revocation of the right to use the CFA designation (V10 p.13).
The Standards of Practice Handbook is the fundamental element of the Institute's ethics education effort and the primary resource for interpreting and implementing the Code and Standards; its "Application of the Standard" examples are hypothetical, and unless stated otherwise every individual in an example is subject to the Code and Standards (V10 p.4).
The Handbook also warns that any set of ethical standards can create a "false sense of security" unless it is fully understood, enforced, and made part of everyday professional activity (V10 p.5).

> [!abstract] The 2023 revisions are the new testable edges
> In 2023 the Board of Governors added one standard and revised two: new Standard I(E) Competence, revised V(B) requiring disclosure of the nature of services and their costs to the client, and VI(A) renamed "Avoid or Disclose Conflicts" with an explicit option to avoid rather than only disclose (V10 p.5).

Understand the reasoning behind each 2023 change, because the exam likes to test the reasoning, not just the label.
Standard I(E) makes explicit what the Code already required ("act with integrity, competence, and diligence" and "maintain and improve their professional competence"); the competence expected varies with the nature and complexity of each member's duties, so the test is fact-specific, and I(E) does not require any particular program of professional development or continuing education (V10 p.5, p.6).
The revised V(B) exists because the financial impact of services was never explicitly part of the required process disclosure; clients cannot make fully informed decisions about whether to engage a member without knowing the nature of the services and their cost (V10 p.6).
The revised VI(A) codifies what the guidance had long said was best practice: avoid conflicts whenever reasonably possible, and when avoidance is not reasonable, disclose clearly and completely in a prominent, plain-language, effective way (V10 p.6, p.7).

Who maintains and enforces all of this?
The Standards of Practice Council (SPC), a group of CFA charterholder volunteers from many countries and organisations, maintains and interprets the Code and Standards and continually evaluates them against six criteria: representative of high standards of professional conduct, relevant to the changing nature of the profession, globally applicable, sufficiently comprehensive, practical, and specific, enforceable, and testable for the CFA Program (V10 p.8, p.9).
Enforcement is separate: the Board of Governors has oversight of the Professional Conduct Program (PCP), which together with the Disciplinary Review Committee (DRC), a volunteer committee of charterholders, enforces the Code and Standards under the Bylaws and Rules of Procedure for Professional Conduct (V10 p.7).
The Professional Conduct division also enforces testing policies of other CFA Institute education programs and the conduct of CIPM certificants (V10 p.7).
The mechanics of an inquiry are in the worked examples below.

Finally, scope.
The Code and Standards bind individual members and candidates, not firms; firms are encouraged to adopt them, and a firm that claims compliance must use a prescribed statement that includes the words "This claim has not been verified by CFA Institute" (V10 p.8).
The separate Asset Manager Code is the firm-level document and covers six areas: loyalty to clients, the investment process, trading, compliance, performance, and disclosure (V10 p.8).
Keeping the individual-level Code and the firm-level Asset Manager Code apart is a reliable exam distinction.

## Formulas and derivations

Ethics has no formulas.
The equivalent of the formula sheet is the exact structure of the Code (six components) and the Standards (seven, with sub-standards I(A) through VII(B)), because the exam tests the wording and the assignment of an obligation to its sub-standard.

**The Code of Ethics: six components** (V10 p.13, p.14)

| # | Component (Members and Candidates must) | One-line obligation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Act with integrity, competence, diligence, and respect and in an ethical manner with the public, clients, prospective clients, employers, employees, colleagues in the investment profession, and other participants in the global capital markets | Ethical conduct toward every counterparty, not only clients |
| 2 | Place the integrity of the investment profession and the interests of clients above their own personal interests | Profession and clients rank above self |
| 3 | Use reasonable care and exercise independent professional judgment when conducting investment analysis, making investment recommendations, taking investment actions, and engaging in other professional activities | Care and independence in all four professional activities |
| 4 | Practice and encourage others to practice in a professional and ethical manner that will reflect credit on themselves and the profession | Personal conduct plus encouragement of others |
| 5 | Promote the integrity and viability of the global capital markets for the ultimate benefit of society | Markets are protected for society, and the sentence ends with "society" |
| 6 | Maintain and improve their professional competence and strive to maintain and improve the competence of other investment professionals | Own competence plus others' competence |

**The Standards of Professional Conduct: seven Standards and every sub-standard** (V10 p.14 to p.17)

How to hold the structure in memory.
Standards I and II look outward at the law and the market, III and IV are the two duty holders (clients first, then employer), V is the production of research and communication, VI is the conflicts that corrupt it, and VII is the Institute itself.
The Code says "clients above self" in component 2, and Standard III(A) says "clients before employer or self"; the Standards never say clients and employer are balanced equally (V10 p.15).
Two sub-standards contain the word "loyalty": III(A) to clients and IV(A) to the employer; Standard VII contains no loyalty requirement and no supervisory requirement (V10 p.15, p.16, p.17).

## Worked examples

The curriculum has no numbered examples in this module.
The following scenarios walk through the Professional Conduct Program and the adoption mechanics exactly as the text describes them.

**Example 1: How a matter reaches the Professional Conduct Program** (V10 p.7)

Scenario: a Level II candidate was named as a defendant in a civil lawsuit brought by a former client; separately, a journalist publishes an article alleging that a charterholder front-ran client orders; and a proctor observes a candidate consulting notes during the exam.
Question: through which inquiry sources does each matter reach Professional Conduct staff?

Step 1: list the five sources the curriculum names.
(1) Self-disclosure on the annual Professional Conduct Statement of all matters that question professional conduct, such as involvement in civil litigation or a criminal investigation, or being the subject of a written complaint.
(2) Written complaints received by Professional Conduct staff.
(3) Staff becoming aware of questionable conduct through the media, regulatory notices, or another public source.
(4) Proctor reports on candidates suspected of violating testing rules on exam day.
(5) Post-exam analyses of scores and exam materials, and monitoring of online and social media for disclosure of confidential exam information.

Step 2: map each fact.
The candidate in civil litigation must self-disclose it on the annual Professional Conduct Statement, so source (1) applies even if nobody complains.
The article about the charterholder is source (3); the media is a legitimate trigger for an inquiry, which is why practice problem 1 option A is wrong.
The proctor's observation is source (4), and if the same candidate later posts exam content online it becomes source (5).

Step 3: note what the exam wants.
An inquiry can be opened without any complaint from a client and without any regulator, and the member's own annual statement is itself a source. ^ex-10-01-1

**Example 2: The investigation and the three staff dispositions** (V10 p.7)

Scenario: Professional Conduct staff open an inquiry into a member following a written complaint.
Question: what may the investigation involve, and what can staff do when it ends?

Step 1: the investigation toolkit.
Staff may request a written explanation from the member or candidate, interview the member or candidate, the complaining parties, and third parties, and collect documents and records relevant to the investigation.

Step 2: the three staff outcomes after reviewing the material.
(a) Conclude the inquiry with no disciplinary sanction.
(b) Issue a cautionary letter.
(c) Continue proceedings to discipline the member or candidate.

Step 3: the charge stage.
If staff believe a violation of the Code and Standards or of testing policies occurred, the member or candidate has the opportunity to accept or reject the charges and the proposed sanctions.
The member does not go before a panel at this stage; a panel is convened only on rejection.

Step 4: exam framing.
Practice problem 1 option C ("investigations may include requesting a written explanation") is a direct lift of step 1, and option B is wrong because a DRC panel is not automatic. ^ex-10-01-2

**Example 3: Rejecting the charges, the DRC panel, and sanctions** (V10 p.7, p.8)

Scenario: staff propose a public censure; the member rejects both the charges and the sanction.
Question: what happens next, and what is the range of sanctions?

Step 1: referral.
The matter is referred to a panel composed of DRC members.
The DRC is a volunteer committee of CFA charterholders who serve on panels to review conduct and who partner with Professional Conduct staff on conduct policy.

Step 2: the panel's task.
The panel reviews materials and presentations from Professional Conduct staff and from the member or candidate, and it determines two things: whether a violation of the Code and Standards or testing policies occurred, and if so, what sanction to impose.

Step 3: the sanction menu.
For members: public censure, suspension of membership and of use of the CFA designation, and revocation of the CFA charter.
For candidates: suspension or prohibition from further participation in the CFA Program.
The Preamble states the same idea as revocation of membership, revocation of candidacy, and revocation of the right to use the designation (V10 p.13).

Step 4: the governance layer above the panel.
The Board of Governors maintains oversight and responsibility for the PCP, and the Bylaws and Rules of Procedure for Professional Conduct form the basic structure for enforcement (V10 p.7). ^ex-10-01-3

**Example 4: A firm adopts the Code and claims compliance** (V10 p.8)

Scenario: an asset manager rewrites its internal code of ethics so that it meets every principle of the Code and Standards, and wants to tell clients it complies.
Question: may it, and what must it say?

Step 1: scope.
The Code and Standards apply to individual members and candidates; CFA Institute encourages, but does not require, firms to adopt them as part of the firm's own code of ethics.

Step 2: the mandatory statement.
Once the party, whether a nonmember or a firm, ensures its code meets the principles of the Code and Standards, it should state whenever claiming compliance: "[Insert name of party] claims compliance with the CFA Institute Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct. This claim has not been verified by CFA Institute."

Step 3: what the firm may not do.
It may not imply that CFA Institute has verified the claim; the statement's second sentence exists precisely to prevent that.
It may distribute the one-page copies of the Code and Standards, including translations, from the CFA Institute website.

Step 4: the firm-level alternative.
If the firm wants a document written for firms, the Asset Manager Code is the relevant instrument; it covers loyalty to clients, the investment process, trading, compliance, performance, and disclosure. ^ex-10-01-4

**Example 5: Classifying the 2023 revisions** (V10 p.5, p.6, p.7)

Scenario: a compliance officer asks a member to summarise what changed in 2023 and why.
Question: identify each change, its standard, and its rationale.

Step 1: Standard I(E) Competence, entirely new.
Rationale: the Code already required competence; I(E) makes it explicit and emphasises continuous maintenance.
Boundary: expected competence varies with the member's duties, and no specific continuing-education program is mandated.

Step 2: Standard V(B), revised.
New requirement: disclose the nature of the services provided and the costs to the client of those services.
Rationale: the financial impact of services was never explicitly part of the process disclosure, and clients need it to make an informed decision about whether to engage the member or firm.

Step 3: Standard VI(A), renamed "Avoid or Disclose Conflicts" and revised.
New element: the option to avoid conflicts, with avoidance preferred when reasonably possible; disclosure remains required when avoidance is not reasonable.
Additional requirement retained: disclosures must be prominent, in plain language, and effective.

Step 4: count.
One new standard plus two revised standards equals three areas of change, and new Handbook guidance and examples accompany them. ^ex-10-01-5

**Example 6: Distinguishing the SPC from the PCP and the DRC** (V10 p.7, p.8, p.9)

Scenario: a vignette states that "the Disciplinary Review Committee revised the Standards in 2023" and that "the Standards of Practice Council imposed a sanction".
Question: which statements are wrong?

Step 1: roles.
The Board of Governors approved the 2023 revisions and has oversight of the PCP.
The SPC, made up of charterholder volunteers from many countries, maintains and interprets the Code and Standards and evaluates them against the six criteria (high standards, relevant, globally applicable, comprehensive and practical and specific, enforceable, testable).
The PCP with the DRC enforces the Code and Standards; DRC members sit on the panels that decide contested cases.

Step 2: verdict.
Both statements are wrong: the DRC enforces rather than revises, and the SPC interprets rather than sanctions. ^ex-10-01-6

## Item-set patterns

> [!warning] Trap: III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care versus VI(A) Avoid or Disclose Conflicts
> The vignette shows a manager whose firm earns higher fees on a proprietary fund and who places clients into it anyway.
> If the harm is that the client's interest was subordinated to the firm's or the manager's, the violated standard is III(A), which requires placing client interests before the employer's or own interests (V10 p.15).
> If the harm is that the fee arrangement was never disclosed or avoided, the violated standard is VI(A) (V10 p.17).
> Distractor: choosing VI(A) because the words "conflict" appears in the vignette; the question stem usually asks which duty was breached by the action taken, and acting against the client is III(A) even when the conflict was disclosed.

> [!warning] Trap: I(B) Independence and Objectivity versus VI(B) Priority of Transactions
> A vignette in which an analyst accepts lavish travel from a covered company points to I(B), which prohibits accepting any gift or benefit that could reasonably compromise independence (V10 p.14).
> A vignette in which an analyst buys shares for a personal account before releasing a report points to VI(B), which requires client and employer transactions to have priority over transactions in which the member is beneficial owner (V10 p.17).
> Distractor: VI(A) is offered for both; it is tempting because both are conflicts, but the sub-standard that names the specific behaviour (gifts, personal trading) wins.

> [!warning] Trap: II(A) Material Nonpublic Information versus II(B) Market Manipulation
> Acting or causing others to act on information that is material and nonpublic is II(A); the standard also covers "cause others to act", so tipping a colleague is a II(A) violation even if the tipper never trades (V10 p.14).
> Distorting prices or artificially inflating volume with intent to mislead is II(B), and the intent element is part of the wording (V10 p.14).
> Distractor: practice problem 5 offers "price distortion" as a II(A) answer and "inadequately maintaining records" (V(C)) as another; both are real obligations placed under the wrong roman numeral.

> [!warning] Trap: I(B) versus IV(B) Additional Compensation Arrangements
> Both address gifts and compensation, and the split is who is being protected.
> I(B) protects the member's independence and objectivity toward the market and clients, so gifts from issuers or from parties trying to influence research are I(B) (V10 p.14).
> IV(B) protects the employer, so compensation from a client or third party that competes with or conflicts with the employer's interest is IV(B), and the cure is written consent from all parties involved, not from the employer alone (V10 p.16).
> Distractor: practice problem 8 offers "written consent of their employer only", which misstates the all-parties requirement, and offers gifts compromising independence as a Standard IV item when it is Standard I.

> [!warning] Trap: III(C) Suitability versus V(A) Diligence and Reasonable Basis versus III(E) Confidentiality
> "Reasonable and adequate basis, supported by appropriate research" is V(A) language and applies to analyses, recommendations, and actions (V10 p.16).
> "Reasonable inquiry into experience, risk and return objectives, and constraints", judged against the total portfolio, is III(C) and applies only in an advisory relationship; when managing to a mandate the duty is to stay consistent with the mandate, not to check the mandate is suitable for every investor (V10 p.15).
> III(E) confidentiality has no "reasonable basis" test; it has three fixed exceptions (illegal client activity, disclosure required by law, client permission) (V10 p.15).
> Distractor: practice problem 4 splices "reasonable and adequate basis" onto "client confidentiality" to make a plausible but non-existent obligation.

> [!warning] Trap: I(C) Misrepresentation versus VII(B) Reference to the CFA designation
> An exaggerated performance claim in a pitch book, or a plagiarised research model, is I(C) (V10 p.14).
> An exaggerated claim about what the CFA designation or candidacy implies, such as saying it guarantees superior performance, is VII(B) (V10 p.17).
> Distractor: VII(A) is offered because it also mentions the CFA designation, but VII(A) concerns conduct that compromises the integrity, validity, or security of CFA Institute programs, for example sharing exam content, not how the designation is described.

> [!warning] Trap: what the Professional Conduct Program can do at each stage
> The vignette states that a complaint "automatically results in a DRC hearing" or that "media reports cannot trigger an inquiry" or that "staff must impose a sanction".
> Each is wrong: staff may close with no sanction, issue a cautionary letter, or continue proceedings; a DRC panel is convened only if the member rejects the charges and proposed sanction; media, regulatory notices, and other public sources are valid inquiry triggers (V10 p.7, p.8).
> Distractor: the answer that names a real body (DRC, Board of Governors, SPC) doing the wrong job, as in Example 6.

> [!warning] Trap: the exact wording of a Code component
> Practice problem 2 shows the pattern: three versions of the fifth principle differing only by the final clause, and only "for the ultimate benefit of society" is complete (V10 p.14).
> Expect the same trick with component 6 ("strive to maintain and improve the competence of other investment professionals"), component 2 ("integrity of the investment profession and the interests of clients above their own personal interests"), and component 1 (the long list of counterparties ending with "other participants in the global capital markets").
> Distractor: the truncated version that sounds complete and the paraphrase that swaps in "uphold the rules", which the Code never says.

> [!warning] Trap: firm-level claims and the Asset Manager Code
> A vignette says a firm "is certified by CFA Institute as compliant with the Code and Standards" or that "the Code and Standards apply to the firm as an entity".
> Both are wrong: the Code and Standards bind individual members and candidates, firms may only claim compliance using the prescribed statement that expressly says the claim has not been verified by CFA Institute, and the firm-level document is the Asset Manager Code with its six areas (V10 p.8).
> Distractor: the answer that lists the six SPC evaluation criteria as if they were the six Asset Manager Code areas; the SPC list is about the Standards themselves (V10 p.9).

## Self-test

Vignette (use for Q1 to Q10).
Marin Osei, CFA, is a portfolio manager at Corvid Capital, a firm whose internal code of ethics was recently rewritten by its compliance officer, Dana Pike, to match every principle of the CFA Institute Code and Standards.
Corvid's marketing brochure now states: "Corvid Capital's ethics program has been reviewed and approved by CFA Institute."
Osei manages both discretionary advisory accounts and a small-cap mandate fund.
Six months ago, without telling anyone, Osei began receiving quarterly consulting fees from a client, Halvard Trust, for reviewing the trust's outside managers.
Osei's analyst, Teo Brandt, a Level II candidate, learned over dinner with a friend at an audit firm that a covered company's earnings will be restated downward next week; Brandt did not trade but told Osei, who sold the stock from client accounts the next morning.
Brandt was later named in a civil lawsuit by a former employer and did not mention it on his annual Professional Conduct Statement, reasoning that no complaint had been filed with CFA Institute.

> [!question] Q10
> Corvid's new fee disclosure describes the investment process and its risks but omits the cost of the advisory service. Under the current Standards, Corvid's members are:
> A. compliant with V(B), because process and risk disclosure is all that V(B) requires.
> B. non-compliant with V(B), because disclosure of the nature of services and their costs is now required.
> C. non-compliant with VI(A), because fees are a conflict of interest that must be avoided.

> [!success]- Answer key
> Q1: B.
> The Code and Standards apply to individuals, but firms are encouraged to adopt them and may claim compliance provided they use the prescribed statement whose second sentence is "This claim has not been verified by CFA Institute"; saying the program was "reviewed and approved by CFA Institute" contradicts that (V10 p.8).
> A is wrong because adoption by firms is encouraged, not prohibited.
> C is wrong because the Asset Manager Code is an additional firm-level document, not the only one a firm may reference.
>
> Q2: B.
> Compensation from a client for services that compete with or conflict with the employer's interest requires written consent from all parties involved; Osei told no one, so IV(B) is breached (V10 p.16).
> A is tempting because a fee could impair objectivity, but I(B) targets gifts and consideration that compromise independence in professional activities such as research, and the specific behaviour here (outside compensation competing with the employer) is named by IV(B).
> C is wrong because VI(C) concerns compensation for recommending products or services, and Osei was paid to review managers, not to refer business.
>
> Q3: B.
> II(A) prohibits both acting and causing others to act on material nonpublic information; Brandt caused Osei to act, so the absence of a personal trade does not save him (V10 p.14).
> A is wrong for that reason.
> C is wrong because II(B) requires practices that distort prices or inflate volume with intent to mislead, and selling on inside information is not manipulation under the curriculum's wording.
>
> Q4: A.
> Osei possessed material nonpublic information and acted on it, which is the direct II(A) prohibition (V10 p.14).
> B is tempting because Osei was trying to help clients, but III(A) is not violated by benefiting clients; the violation is against market integrity.
> C is wrong because VI(B) concerns personal (beneficial-owner) transactions relative to client and employer transactions, and no personal trade occurred.
>
> Q5: B.
> Members and candidates must self-disclose on the annual Professional Conduct Statement all matters that question their professional conduct, and the curriculum names involvement in civil litigation explicitly (V10 p.7).
> A is wrong because written complaints are only one of five inquiry sources.
> C is wrong because the self-disclosure requirement is not limited to client disputes.
>
> Q6: B.
> Proctor reports on candidates suspected of violating testing rules are an explicit inquiry source (V10 p.7).
> A is wrong because a DRC panel is convened only after staff propose charges and the candidate rejects them (V10 p.8).
> C is wrong because no written complaint is needed for a proctor-initiated inquiry.
>
> Q7: A.
> Rejection of the charges and proposed sanction refers the matter to a panel composed of DRC members, which reviews materials and presentations from both sides and decides whether a violation occurred and what sanction to impose (V10 p.8).
> B is wrong because the SPC maintains and interprets the Code and Standards and has no enforcement role (V10 p.8, p.9).
> C is wrong because a cautionary letter is one of the staff's dispositions before charges, not a mandatory outcome after rejection (V10 p.7).
>
> Q8: A.
> In an advisory relationship, III(C)(1)(c) requires judging suitability in the context of the client's total portfolio, which cannot be done without inquiring into other holdings (V10 p.15).
> B is wrong because nothing in the facts says the research basis was inadequate.
> C is wrong because the mandate rule in III(C)(2) applies to portfolios managed to a specific mandate, strategy, or style, not to advisory accounts (V10 p.15).
>
> Q9: B.
> I(E) requires acting with and maintaining the competence necessary for one's professional responsibilities, which varies with the nature and complexity of duties, and the curriculum states expressly that I(E) does not require any specific program of professional development or continuing education (V10 p.6).
> A is wrong for that reason.
> C is wrong because I(E) is a Standard added in 2023 to make the Code's competence principle explicit (V10 p.5).
>
> Q10: B.
> The 2023 revision to V(B) added the requirement to disclose the nature of the services provided and the costs to the client associated with those services (V10 p.6, p.16).
> A is wrong because process, material changes, and limitations and risks were already required and the cost element is now added.
> C is wrong because a fee is not a conflict that must be avoided; VI(A) addresses matters that impair independence and objectivity, and the failure here is a missing V(B) disclosure.

How many components does the Code of Ethics have, and how many Standards of Professional Conduct are there?::Six Code components and seven Standards (I through VII), with sub-standards I(A) to VII(B) (V10 p.13 to p.17).

Complete the fifth Code principle: "Promote the integrity and viability of the global capital markets ..."::"... for the ultimate benefit of society" (V10 p.14).

Complete the sixth Code principle: "Maintain and improve their professional competence and ..."::"... strive to maintain and improve the competence of other investment professionals" (V10 p.14).

The first Code principle requires acting with integrity, competence, diligence, and respect and in an ethical manner with whom?::The public, clients, prospective clients, employers, employees, colleagues in the investment profession, and other participants in the global capital markets (V10 p.13).

The third Code principle requires reasonable care and independent professional judgment when doing which four things?::Conducting investment analysis, making investment recommendations, taking investment actions, and engaging in other professional activities (V10 p.13).

Which three areas of the Standards were changed in 2023?::New I(E) Competence; V(B) revised to require disclosure of the nature of services and their costs; VI(A) renamed "Avoid or Disclose Conflicts" and revised to allow avoidance (V10 p.5).

Does Standard I(E) Competence require a specific continuing-education program?::No; expected competence varies with the nature and complexity of the member's duties and no specific program of professional development is mandated (V10 p.6).

Why was V(B) revised in 2023?::The financial impact (cost) of services was never explicitly part of the required process disclosure, and clients need it to decide whether to engage the member or firm (V10 p.6).

Under revised VI(A), when is disclosure (rather than avoidance) of a conflict acceptable?::When it is not reasonable to avoid the conflict; disclosure must then be prominent, in plain language, and effective (V10 p.7).

Who has oversight and responsibility for the Professional Conduct Program?::The CFA Institute Board of Governors (V10 p.7).

What is the Disciplinary Review Committee?::A volunteer committee of CFA charterholders who serve on panels to review conduct and partner with Professional Conduct staff on conduct policies; with the PCP it enforces the Code and Standards (V10 p.7).

What three things may a Professional Conduct investigation include?::Requesting a written explanation from the member or candidate; interviewing the member, complaining parties, and third parties; and collecting relevant documents and records (V10 p.7).

What three dispositions are available to Professional Conduct staff after reviewing an investigation?::Conclude with no disciplinary sanction, issue a cautionary letter, or continue proceedings to discipline the member or candidate (V10 p.7).

When is a matter referred to a panel of DRC members?::Only when the member or candidate does not accept the charges and proposed sanction (V10 p.8).

What two things does a DRC panel determine?::Whether a violation of the Code and Standards or testing policies occurred and, if so, what sanction should be imposed (V10 p.8).

What sanctions may CFA Institute impose on members?::Public censure, suspension of membership and use of the CFA designation, and revocation of the CFA charter (V10 p.8).

What sanctions apply to candidates who violate the Code and Standards or testing policies?::Suspension or prohibition from further participation in the CFA Program (V10 p.8).

What must a firm or nonmember state when claiming compliance with the Code and Standards?::"[Name] claims compliance with the CFA Institute Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct. This claim has not been verified by CFA Institute." (V10 p.8).

Do the Code and Standards apply to firms?::No; they apply to individual members and candidates, though firms are encouraged to adopt them (V10 p.8).

Name the six areas of the Asset Manager Code.::Loyalty to clients, the investment process, trading, compliance, performance, and disclosure (V10 p.8).

What does the Standards of Practice Council do?::Maintains and interprets the Code and Standards and ensures they remain effective; it is made up of charterholder volunteers from many countries (V10 p.8).

## Common mistakes

1. Assigning "price distortion" or "artificially inflated volume" to II(A) when it is II(B); II(A) is about acting or causing others to act on material nonpublic information (V10 p.14).
2. Treating II(A) as violated only by trading; the standard also prohibits causing others to act, so a tip without a trade is a violation (V10 p.14).
3. Reading III(A) as balancing client and employer interests; the wording is client interests before the employer's or one's own (V10 p.15).
4. Confusing the advisory-relationship duties in III(C)(1) with the mandate duty in III(C)(2); managing to a mandate never requires checking that the mandate suits all investors (V10 p.15).
5. Attaching "reasonable and adequate basis" to confidentiality or suitability; the phrase belongs to V(A), and III(E) has three fixed exceptions instead (V10 p.15, p.16).
6. Sending gift-related conflicts to IV(B) or VI(A) when the gift threatens independence and objectivity, which is I(B); IV(B) is about compensation competing with the employer and needs written consent from all parties, not the employer only (V10 p.14, p.16).
7. Putting loyalty or supervision into Standard VII; loyalty lives in III(A) and IV(A), supervision in IV(C), and VII covers program conduct and references to the designation (V10 p.15 to p.17).
8. Truncating the fifth Code component; it ends "for the ultimate benefit of society", and the exam offers the truncated version as a distractor (V10 p.14).
9. Believing an inquiry needs a written complaint or that a DRC panel hears every case; inquiries have five sources, staff have three dispositions, and a panel is convened only on rejection of charges (V10 p.7, p.8).
10. Confusing the bodies: the Board of Governors approves revisions and oversees the PCP, the SPC maintains and interprets the Standards, and the PCP with the DRC enforces them (V10 p.7 to p.9).
11. Saying a firm is "verified" or "approved" by CFA Institute as compliant; the prescribed statement says the claim has not been verified, and the firm-level document is the Asset Manager Code (V10 p.8).
12. Claiming I(E) mandates continuing education; the curriculum states it does not require any specific program of professional development (V10 p.6).
13. Equating legal with ethical; the curriculum warns that compliance with the law is insufficient and that a code of ethics without a culture of integrity is a "dusty compilation" (V10 p.11, p.12).
14. Forgetting that a firm's code is described as necessary but insufficient, and that senior management's demonstrated culture of integrity may be the single most important factor (V10 p.12).
