# LM2: Guidance for Standards I-VII

Difficulty 3: no calculation anywhere, but this is the largest module in the curriculum, and the exam demands that you assign a fact pattern to the exact sub-standard among 22 near neighbours and then name the procedure that would have prevented the violation.

## LOS map

- **demonstrate** a thorough knowledge of the CFA Institute Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct by applying the Code and Standards to specific situations (V10 p.23) ^los-10-02-a
    - Examiner asks: given a vignette actor's conduct, which sub-standard is violated, or whether there is no violation at all, with the two nearest sub-standards offered as distractors.
- **recommend** practices and procedures designed to prevent violations of the Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct (V10 p.23) ^los-10-02-b
    - Examiner asks: which policy, disclosure, consent, firewall, or restricted list from the Recommended Procedures would most likely have prevented the violation, or what the member should do next (dissociate, disclose, obtain written consent, refuse).

## Core intuition

> [!abstract] The exam tests assignment, not recitation
> Level II Ethics vignettes never ask you to recite a standard; they show conduct and ask two things: which sub-standard among I(A) through VII(B) the conduct violates (or that it violates none), and which practice or procedure would have prevented it (V10 p.23).
> Every distractor is the nearest-neighbour sub-standard, so mastery means knowing the boundary between neighbours, not the centre of each standard.

The seven Standards form an architecture, and holding the architecture makes assignment fast.
Standard I looks outward at the legal and professional environment: obey the strictest applicable rule (I(A)), keep your judgment unbought (I(B)), tell no untruth (I(C)), commit no discreditable act (I(D)), and stay competent (I(E)) (V10 p.23).
Standard II protects the market itself: do not act on material nonpublic information (II(A)) and do not distort prices or volume with intent to mislead (II(B)) (V10 p.64).
Standard III owes duties to clients: loyalty, prudence, and care (III(A)), fair dealing across all clients (III(B)), suitability (III(C)), fair and complete performance presentation (III(D)), and confidentiality (III(E)) (V10 p.81).
Standard IV owes duties to employers: loyalty in matters related to employment (IV(A)), written consent from all parties before outside compensation (IV(B)), and reasonable supervision (IV(C)) (V10 p.117).
Standard V governs the production process: diligence and reasonable basis (V(A)), communication that separates fact from opinion and discloses process, cost, limitations, and risk (V(B)), and record retention (V(C)) (V10 p.139).
Standard VI polices conflicts: avoid or disclose them fully and fairly (VI(A)), put client and employer trades ahead of your own (VI(B)), and disclose referral arrangements (VI(C)) (V10 p.165).
Standard VII protects CFA Institute programs: program integrity and exam conduct (VII(A)) and honest reference to membership, the designation, and candidacy (VII(B)) (V10 p.179).

> [!abstract] Three recurring discriminators decide most questions
> First, who is owed the duty: clients before employer before self, so a gift from a grateful client needs disclosure to the employer, while a gift from an entity trying to influence future conduct must be refused (V10 p.34).
> Second, what the member knew or should have known: "knowingly" in I(A) and I(C) turns honest error into non-violation and turns wilful blindness into violation (V10 p.25, p.47).
> Third, what paper would have prevented it: preclearance, restricted lists, disclosure, written consent, firewalls, and records are the answers to the second LOS (V10 p.28, p.69).

The second LOS is mechanical once you see the pattern.
Almost every Recommended Procedures list is a combination of six devices: a written policy adopted in advance, disclosure to the party who bears the risk, consent (written where the employer is involved), a physical or informational barrier (firewall, restricted list, quiet period), preclearance or prior approval of the risky act, and records that prove the process happened (V10 p.28, p.69, p.85, p.133).
When the question asks "which procedure would most likely have prevented the violation", pick the device that operates before the conduct, not the one that punishes it afterwards.

Why the boundaries matter more than the centres.
The classic confusions the exam farms are: I(B) versus IV(B) (independence bought by an outside party versus outside compensation competing with the employer's interest), II(A) versus the mosaic theory (nonpublic material fact versus conclusions built from public and nonmaterial pieces), III(A) versus VI(A) (breach of loyalty in managing client assets versus failure to disclose the conflict that motivated it), III(B) versus III(C) (treating clients unequally in dissemination versus recommending something inconsistent with one client's IPS), IV(A) versus III(A) (harming the employer versus harming the client), and VI(B) versus III(B) (front-running your own account versus favouring one client over another) (V10 p.33, p.65, p.83, p.91, p.101, p.119, p.171).
The Formulas section below is therefore built as a master reference: for each sub-standard, the obligation, the guidance highlights, the recommended procedures, and the boundary against its nearest neighbours.

A final orientation point: the Standards are conduct rules, not outcome rules.
A trade that happens to make money can still violate II(A), and an honest process that loses money violates nothing; the Professional Conduct Program sanctions process failures, and so does the exam (V10 p.24, p.65).
Whenever a vignette shows a good outcome, re-check the process; whenever it shows a loss, do not assume a violation.

### Standard I(A) Knowledge of the Law (V10 p.23)

**Obligation.** Understand and comply with all applicable laws, rules, and regulations (including the Code and Standards) of any government, regulatory organization, licensing agency, or professional association governing your professional activities; in a conflict, comply with the more strict rule; do not knowingly participate or assist in violations, and dissociate from them (V10 p.23).

Key guidance:
- "Applicable law" is the law that governs your conduct; "more strict" means the rule imposing greater restriction or a greater duty protecting investors (V10 p.24).
- You are not required to be a compliance expert or to know every law, but you must comply on a reasonable, good-faith understanding, and stay vigilant during regulatory change, including new technology and social media rules (V10 p.24).
- Global application (Exhibit 1): if applicable law is stricter than the Code and Standards, follow the law; if less strict or absent, follow the Code and Standards; a locality-governs or client-home-country-governs clause changes which law applies, then apply the same test (V10 p.26, p.27).
- Participation: you are responsible when you know or should know your conduct contributes to a violation; honest ignorance of facts is a defence, wilful blindness is not (V10 p.25).
- Dissociation ladder: first attempt to stop the conduct via supervisor or compliance; if unsuccessful, step away (remove name from reports, ask for reassignment, refuse the client); in extreme cases resign; inaction plus continued association can be construed as participation (V10 p.25).
- Reporting violations to CFA Institute is encouraged; reporting to regulators (whistleblowing) is not required by the Code unless law mandates it, though it may be prudent (V10 p.25).
- Cross-border products: understand the laws of the countries of origination and expected distribution (V10 p.26).

Recommended procedures: stay informed via compliance memos and continuing education; review the firm's written compliance procedures regularly; maintain current reference files of statutes and rules; understand distribution-area laws; seek advice of counsel or compliance when in doubt; when dissociating, document the violation and urge the firm to stop it; firms should adopt a code of ethics, distribute information on applicable laws, and establish written protocols for reporting suspected violations (V10 p.28, p.29).

Boundary cases:
- Versus I(D): I(A) needs a law, rule, or standard violated or assisted; I(D) needs dishonesty, fraud, deceit, or an act reflecting adversely on professional reputation, integrity, or competence, legal or not (V10 p.57).
- Legal counsel's blessing does not absolve compliance: relying on advice that "the regulator cannot prove wrongdoing" does not excuse assisting a misleading filing (V10 p.29).
- Following the strictest rule includes local law stricter than the Code even when the home country is laxer: a US adviser in a country banning personal IPO participation must obey the ban, not merely disclose (V10 p.30, p.31).

### Standard I(B) Independence and Objectivity (V10 p.32)

**Obligation.** Use reasonable care and judgment to achieve and maintain independence and objectivity; do not offer, solicit, or accept any gift, benefit, compensation, or consideration that reasonably could be expected to compromise your own or another's independence and objectivity (V10 p.32).

Key guidance:
- Gifts from a client are distinguishable from gifts from entities seeking influence: client gifts are supplementary compensation in an existing relationship and require disclosure to the employer (before acceptance where possible, after otherwise); gifts intended to influence must be refused (V10 p.33).
- Allocation of oversubscribed IPO shares to managers' personal accounts is prohibited; modest gifts and customary entertainment are acceptable, best practice is to reject anything that could threaten independence (V10 p.33).
- Pressure sources catalogued: buy-side clients pressuring sell-side analysts (ratings inflation, retaliation threats), fund manager and custodial relationships, investment banking relationships (enhanced firewalls, separate reporting lines, compensation not tied to banking assignments), performance measurement analysts pressured to alter composites, public companies retaliating against negative coverage, credit rating agencies paid by issuers, and manager selection on both the hiring and the soliciting side, including pay-to-play political contributions (V10 p.34 to p.37).
- Issuer-paid research: fraught with conflicts; must be thorough independent analysis with full disclosure of compensation, and pay must be a flat fee negotiated in advance, never contingent on conclusions or paid in equity (V10 p.37).
- Travel funding: best practice is commercial transport at your own or your firm's expense; accept modestly arranged travel only when commercial transport is unavailable (V10 p.38).

Recommended procedures: protect the integrity of opinions with compensation systems that reward objectivity; put a controversial corporate client on a restricted list and distribute only factual information; restrict special cost arrangements (pay your own travel and hotel, limit corporate aircraft to necessity); limit gifts to token items with a strict value limit; require prior approval for employee IPO and private placement participation; implement supervisory review; adopt a formal written independence policy with reporting structures that keep analysts outside the control of departments that could pressure them; appoint a senior compliance officer (V10 p.38, p.39).

Boundary cases:
- Versus IV(B): a benefit that buys influence over your analysis or actions for other clients is I(B); compensation that competes with or creates a conflict with the employer's interest (for example a client performance bonus) is IV(B) and needs written consent from all parties (V10 p.33, p.127).
- Versus VI(A): a conflict you can and should refuse is I(B); an unavoidable interest that must be prominently disclosed is VI(A) (V10 p.165).
- Modest, business-related hospitality with no strings (chartered flight to a remote mine, modest motel) does not violate; lavish packages (World Cup tickets, resort stays) do, and even a compliant policy firm like the analyst who pays his own room is simply best practice (V10 p.39).
- Offering inducements is also a violation: wining and dining a pension consultant or contributing to a trustee's re-election campaign to win a mandate violates I(B) from the giving side (V10 p.44, p.45).

### Standard I(C) Misrepresentation (V10 p.46)

**Obligation.** Do not knowingly make any misrepresentation relating to investment analysis, recommendations, actions, or other professional activities (V10 p.46).

Key guidance:
- A misrepresentation is any untrue statement or omission of fact or any statement that is otherwise false or misleading; "knowingly" means you know or should have known (V10 p.46).
- Guaranteeing a specific return or capital preservation on a volatile investment is prohibited; describing structural guarantees built into a product (insured deposits, guaranteed structured products) is fine (V10 p.46, p.47).
- Covers qualifications, credentials, firm services, performance record, and investment characteristics; you are responsible for third-party information (credit ratings, research, marketing material) you incorporate into your practice, and for disclosing use of external managers (V10 p.47).
- Performance reporting: benchmark must be comparable to strategy; disclose reasons for unusual reference indexes; do not shop valuation providers for higher marks on illiquid securities (V10 p.47, p.48).
- Omissions: knowingly omitted model inputs mislead; model outputs must not be presented as fact; composite construction must prevent cherry picking (V10 p.48).
- Plagiarism: no copying in substantially the same form without acknowledgment; includes "leading analysts" quotes without naming, forecasts without qualifying caveats, charts without sources, and proprietary spreadsheets without authorization; applies to oral and electronic communication (V10 p.48, p.49).
- Two safe harbours: widely used factual and statistical data from recognized reporting services need no attribution, and research or models developed for your employer remain firm property that the firm may reissue without crediting departed analysts, though you cannot reissue a prior report solely under your own name (V10 p.49).

Recommended procedures: know the limits of your firm's and your own capabilities and keep a written list of firm services and qualifications; prepare a personal qualification summary; verify third-party information; monitor and secure webpages; against plagiarism, maintain copies of source material, attribute quotations, and attribute summaries (V10 p.50, p.51).

Boundary cases:
- Versus III(D): misstated performance shown to clients and prospects is the specific province of III(D); credential inflation, guarantees, and plagiarism fall to I(C); the classic Taylor case violates both, III(D) for the cherry-picked period and I(C) for the promised 25 percent (V10 p.109).
- The "knowingly" gate: a typographical error distributed unknowingly is not a violation, but you must correct it once discovered; using material you should have known was wrong for years (a degree never earned) is a violation (V10 p.51, p.52).
- Repackaging third-party research is permitted if clients know you are selecting outside research; presenting it as your own work is a violation (V10 p.49, p.54).

### Standard I(D) Misconduct (V10 p.57)

**Obligation.** Do not engage in any professional conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, or deceit, or commit any act that reflects adversely on your professional reputation, integrity, or competence (V10 p.57).

Key guidance:
- Aimed at professional life: lying, cheating, stealing, and dishonest conduct violate the standard when the offence reflects adversely on professional activities (V10 p.57).
- Conduct need not be illegal: intoxication during business hours can violate; personal bankruptcy does not unless it involved fraudulent or deceitful business conduct (V10 p.57).
- Absence of sufficient effort or due diligence can itself be misconduct because it betrays the trust clients place in the professional (V10 p.57).
- The PCP must not be abused to settle personal or political disputes unrelated to professional ethics (V10 p.57).

Recommended procedures: adopt a code of ethics that makes clear personal behaviour reflecting poorly on the individual, firm, or industry is not tolerated; disseminate a list of potential violations and sanctions; check references of potential employees (V10 p.58).

Boundary cases:
- Versus I(A): civil disobedience in support of personal beliefs generally does not violate I(D) because it does not reflect on professional reputation, even though it is illegal (V10 p.59).
- Fraud outside client work still counts when it shows dishonesty at work: padding insurance reimbursement claims or skimming a charity purchase both violate I(D) (V10 p.58, p.59).

### Standard I(E) Competence (V10 p.60)

**Obligation.** Act with and maintain the competence necessary to fulfil your professional responsibilities (V10 p.60).

Key guidance:
- Competence is sufficient knowledge, skills, and abilities for the specific role; it varies by role and circumstances (V10 p.60).
- A negative outcome does not prove incompetence: a diligent manager who loses money or fails to foresee a hidden fraud is not thereby incompetent (V10 p.60, p.63).
- The duty is continuous: role changes and expansions (a new supervisory post, a new product class like cryptocurrency) demand new competence before acting (V10 p.61, p.62, p.63).
- No particular continuing education program is mandated; competence can be attained in a variety of ways (V10 p.61).

Recommended procedures: continuing education programs, professional designations, conferences and seminars, employer training, self-study, expert groups, and deliberately acquiring any new skill when responsibilities change (V10 p.61).

Boundary cases:
- Versus V(A): I(E) asks whether the person is capable of the role at all; V(A) asks whether one particular recommendation had a reasonable basis; investing clients in a product you do not understand violates I(E) even if a competent person could have justified the trade (V10 p.63).
- Passing a licensing exam without learning the underlying rules does not establish competence (V10 p.62).

### Standard II(A) Material Nonpublic Information (V10 p.64)

**Obligation.** Members and candidates who possess material nonpublic information that could affect the value of an investment must not act or cause others to act on the information (V10 p.64).

Key guidance:
- Material: disclosure would probably affect the price, or reasonable investors would want it before deciding; the list includes earnings, mergers and tender offers, asset-quality changes, innovations, ratings changes, large orders before execution, defaults, and government economic reports (V10 p.65).
- Reliability gates materiality: a fact from a corporate insider is likely material; speculation by a competitor or conjecture by unconnected experts is likely not; ambiguous price effect and the passage of time also reduce materiality (V10 p.65).
- Nonpublic: until disseminated to the marketplace in general; a room full of analysts is still a select group; issuer guidance interpreting public filings can itself be selective disclosure; you need not wait for the slowest delivery channel (V10 p.66).
- Due-diligence carve-out: insider information provided legitimately by the source company for mergers, loan underwriting, credit ratings, or offering engagements may be used for that purpose only (V10 p.66).
- Mosaic theory: conclusions built from public information plus nonmaterial nonpublic information may be acted on even if the conclusion would have been material coming from the company; document the research trail under V(C) (V10 p.66, p.67).
- Social media groups with membership limits are not public; verify that material information is also available publicly (V10 p.67).
- Expert networks are permitted, but signed agreements do not absolve you; you must make your own materiality determination before trading (V10 p.67, p.68).
- A famous analyst's own conclusions from public information are his clients' property; the report need not be made public even though it will move prices (V10 p.68).

Recommended procedures: try to achieve public dissemination (encourage the issuer to disclose; if impossible, tell only compliance and do not act); adopt compliance and disclosure procedures; issue press releases before analyst calls; firewall elements: control interdepartmental communication through compliance, watch lists and restricted lists and rumor lists, documented enforcement, heightened review of proprietary trading; physically separate departments; no personnel overlap (an analyst helping bankers goes "over the wall" and stays there until the information is public); a reporting system with a designated compliance officer deciding materiality; personal trading limits with periodic reports; keep records; market makers continue but stay passive, risk arbitrage should be suspended; train all employees (V10 p.68 to p.71).

Boundary cases:
- Mosaic versus tipping: designers' and retailers' opinions plus public financials producing an earnings-drop conclusion is mosaic; the CFO stating the workforce will strike Friday is material nonpublic fact (V10 p.72, p.73).
- The tippee chain violates at every link: the person who conveys, and the person who trades, both violate, even family members, and even if no one trades (V10 p.72, p.74).
- Unreliable sources are not material: a doctor's takeover hunch or a rival trader's unverified text is market noise you may trade through (V10 p.73, p.75).
- A journalist trading ahead of publishing an influential analyst's recommendation change trades on material nonpublic information (V10 p.74).
- Good faith about the tipper is irrelevant: assuming a senior executive "would not disclose inside information" does not excuse the trade, even one that loses money (V10 p.74).

### Standard II(B) Market Manipulation (V10 p.76)

**Obligation.** Do not engage in practices that distort prices or artificially inflate trading volume with the intent to mislead market participants (V10 p.76).

Key guidance:
- Two species: information-based manipulation (false or misleading information, pump and dump, rumor campaigns) and transaction-based manipulation (trades creating a false impression of activity or price, or securing a dominant position to exploit a derivative or underlying) (V10 p.77).
- Intent is critical: legitimate trading strategies exploiting perceived inefficiencies are not violations even when they move prices (V10 p.77).
- A disclosed liquidity-pumping arrangement (an exchange paying members to trade a new contract) is permissible if fully disclosed; undisclosed, it misleads participants about actual liquidity (V10 p.79).

Recommended procedures: none listed separately; prevention flows from the firewall, trading review, and personal trading procedures of II(A) (V10 p.77).

Boundary cases:
- Manipulating your own holdings' price through wash trades across family accounts plus blog rumors is both transaction-based and information-based manipulation (V10 p.78).
- Timing a speculative negative report during an issuer's quiet period to benefit short clients is manipulation plus a V(A) violation for lacking a reasonable basis (V10 p.78, p.79).
- Trading between two funds you manage to raise volume and price violates even when fund participants would benefit (V10 p.79).
- Feeding rating models deliberately rosy inputs, or posting rumors under a false name with no personal profit, still violates: profit is not an element, intent to mislead is (V10 p.80, p.81).

### Standard III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care (V10 p.81)

**Obligation.** Act with reasonable care and exercise prudent judgment; act for the benefit of clients and place clients' interests before your employer's or your own (V10 p.81).

Key guidance:
- Prudence is the care, skill, and diligence of a reasonable person in a like capacity; follow the client's investment parameters and balance risk and return; custody or effective control of client assets raises the duty (V10 p.82).
- The standard is a minimum benchmark for all members regardless of job title; it does not make everyone a fiduciary, but any legally imposed fiduciary duty must be followed via I(A) (V10 p.82, p.83).
- Execution-only professionals owe skill and diligence in seeking best execution within client instructions, not a fiduciary duty (V10 p.83, p.90).
- Identify the actual client: for pension plans and trusts, the duty runs to beneficiaries, not the hiring management; for a mandate or index fund the duty is to invest consistently with the stated mandate; sometimes the client is the investing public (V10 p.84).
- Judge investments in the context of the total portfolio (V10 p.85).
- Soft dollars: client brokerage is the client's asset; paying up for research without corresponding client benefit violates loyalty; directed brokerage is not a loyalty violation but requires seeking best execution and telling the client it may not be achieved (V10 p.85).
- Proxies have economic value and must be voted informedly and responsibly; a cost-benefit analysis may justify not voting all proxies; disclose proxy policies (V10 p.85).

Recommended procedures: submit itemized quarterly statements, disclose where assets are kept and moved, separate client assets; when in doubt ask what you would want as the client and get written approval; firm policies: follow rules and laws, establish client objectives, consider suitability in portfolio context, diversify, regular reviews, deal fairly, disclose conflicts and compensation, vote proxies in clients' best interest, maintain confidentiality, seek best execution, place client interests first (V10 p.85, p.86, p.87).

Boundary cases:
- Versus IV(A): the question is who was harmed; using a pension plan's assets to entrench management harms beneficiaries, III(A); taking the employer's files harms the employer, IV(A) (V10 p.87, p.121).
- Versus VI(A): failing to disclose the motive (fee arrangement, friendship) is VI(A); the client harm from acting on that motive is III(A); vignettes often violate both.
- Excessive trading to hit a commission target with suitable securities still violates III(A): churning uses client assets to benefit the manager (V10 p.89).
- Fee-paying family accounts are full clients: excluding a brother's account from an IPO allocation "to avoid appearances" disadvantages a client and violates III(A) (V10 p.89).
- A fund manager owes loyalty to the fund's mandate, not to any individual fund investor; the investor's own adviser owes the personal duty (V10 p.90).

### Standard III(B) Fair Dealing (V10 p.91)

**Obligation.** Deal fairly and objectively with all clients when providing investment analysis, making investment recommendations, taking investment action, or engaging in other professional activities (V10 p.91).

Key guidance:
- Fairly, not equally: simultaneous reach of all clients is impossible, and premium service levels are permitted if disclosed and available to everyone; different levels must never disadvantage other clients (V10 p.91, p.92).
- Recommendations: disseminate so all clients have a fair opportunity to act; material changes to prior recommendations deserve extra care, and clients who placed orders contrary to the current recommendation must be told before the order is accepted (V10 p.92).
- Investment action: distribute new issues to all suitable customers per firm policy; prorate oversubscribed issues on a round-lot basis; forgo your own and immediate-family sales in oversubscribed issues, but family accounts managed like ordinary client accounts must not be excluded; no withholding "hot issues" for personal benefit (V10 p.92, p.93).
- Client consent cannot bless patently unfair allocation procedures (V10 p.93).

Recommended procedures: limit the people who know a recommendation is coming; shorten the decision-to-dissemination window (flash reports); publish pre-dissemination guidelines prohibiting trading ahead; disseminate simultaneously with trading restrictions until wide distribution; maintain a list of clients and holdings to notify all holders; documented trade allocation procedures: time-stamped orders, first-in-first-out processing, block trades at one execution price and commission, pro rata partial fills by order size subject to minimum lots, allocate new issues by client not by portfolio manager with advance indications of interest; disclose allocation procedures and service levels; systematic account review for favoritism (V10 p.94, p.95, p.96).

Boundary cases:
- Versus VI(B): favouring one client over another is III(B); favouring your own or your firm's account over clients is VI(B); Morris filling his own order in a hot IPO while cutting institutional blocks manages to do both (V10 p.97).
- Versus III(C): fair dealing is about the distribution process across clients; suitability is about one client's fit; a pro rata allocation to an account for which the issue is unsuitable violates III(C) not III(B).
- Discussing a distributed recommendation in more depth with the largest clients afterwards is fine; lunching them on it a week before distribution is a violation (V10 p.96, p.98).
- Minimum-lot allocations below strict pro rata are compliant when the issue is oversubscribed and odd lots would hurt liquidity (V10 p.98).
- Allocating trades after seeing which ones won (cherry-picking winners into a favoured account) is a classic III(B) violation (V10 p.97).

### Standard III(C) Suitability (V10 p.100)

**Obligation.** In an advisory relationship: (a) make reasonable inquiry into investment experience, risk and return objectives, and financial constraints before recommending or acting, and reassess and update regularly; (b) determine that an investment is suitable to the client's financial situation and consistent with the client's written objectives, mandates, and constraints; (c) judge suitability in the context of the total portfolio. When managing to a specific mandate, strategy, or style: make only recommendations and take only actions consistent with the stated objectives and constraints of the portfolio (V10 p.100).

Key guidance:
- The inquiry-and-IPS duty falls on those in advisory relationships; execution-only brokers and sell-side analysts may have no opportunity to judge suitability (V10 p.101).
- The written IPS covers risk tolerance, return requirements, and constraints (time horizon, liquidity, taxes, legal and regulatory factors, unique circumstances), plus roles, responsibilities, and review schedules; update at least annually and before material recommendation changes (V10 p.101).
- Leverage and limited liquidity in synthetic and derivative vehicles bear directly on suitability (V10 p.101).
- A high-risk investment can be suitable in a diversified total portfolio; you assess suitability only on the information the client actually provides (V10 p.102).
- Unsolicited unsuitable trade requests: for minimal-impact requests, educate the client and follow firm approval policy with the client acknowledging the discussion; for material-impact requests, update the IPS first; if the client refuses to update the IPS but insists, execute in a separate unmanaged account or reconsider the relationship (V10 p.102, p.103).
- Mandate managers are not responsible for the suitability of the fund for any particular investor; that duty belongs to the investor's adviser (V10 p.103).

Recommended procedures: a written IPS covering client identification, objectives, constraints, and performance benchmarks; regular updates comparing constraints with capital market expectations; suitability test policies checking impact on diversification, risk versus the client's tolerance, and fit with the strategy (V10 p.103, p.104).

Boundary cases:
- Versus III(A): buying illiquid private equity against an IPS demanding liquid AA bonds violates both, III(C) for the mandate breach and III(A) for imprudence (V10 p.105).
- One strategy for two different clients violates III(C) for the client whose circumstances differ (V10 p.104).
- A zero-dividend growth stock in a high-income fund breaches the mandate branch even if the stock is attractive (V10 p.105).
- Changing strategy to chase a short-term bonus (high-beta stocks in long-horizon pension accounts) violates III(C) and points to a VI(A) compensation-conflict disclosure failure (V10 p.106, p.169).

### Standard III(D) Performance Presentation (V10 p.107)

**Obligation.** When communicating investment performance information, make reasonable efforts to ensure it is fair, accurate, and complete (V10 p.107).

Key guidance:
- Covers presentation and measurement, past and reasonably expected performance, separate accounts, pooled funds, and analysts promoting the accuracy of their recommendations; do not state or imply clients will benefit from past returns (V10 p.108).
- Brief presentations must make detailed supporting information available on request and note their limited nature (V10 p.108).

Recommended procedures: comply with the GIPS standards, or else consider audience knowledge, present weighted composites rather than a single representative account, include terminated accounts through their final period, disclose whether results are simulated, from a prior entity, gross or net of fees, and maintain the underlying data and records (V10 p.108, p.109).

Boundary cases:
- Versus I(C): a false claim of GIPS compliance, a track record from a prior firm shown without disclosure, or undisclosed simulated results are III(D); the accompanying guarantee of continued returns is I(C) (V10 p.109, p.110).
- Composite discipline: excluding a poorly performing account that fits the composite definition, changing composite membership over time, or switching attribution or return methodology to flatter results all violate; a methodology change requires notification and ideally parallel presentation (V10 p.110, p.111).
- Prior-firm performance may be shown with disclosure of where it was earned and your specific role; team performance claimed as your own violates (V10 p.109).

### Standard III(E) Preservation of Confidentiality (V10 p.112)

**Obligation.** Keep information about current, former, and prospective clients confidential unless it concerns illegal activities by the client, disclosure is required by law, or the client permits it (V10 p.112).

Key guidance:
- Applies when you receive information through your special ability to conduct the client's business or in the confidential relationship; survives the end of the relationship (V10 p.112).
- If law requires disclosure, disclose; if law requires confidentiality even about illegal acts, stay silent; when in doubt consult compliance or counsel (V10 p.113).
- Electronic security: know your firm's policies; you need not become a security expert; encourage regular confidentiality training for all staff (V10 p.113).
- The CFA Institute PCP is treated as an extension of yourself: providing client information to a PCP investigation, when permissible under law, is not a violation (V10 p.113).

Recommended procedures: avoid disclosing anything received from a client except to authorized colleagues working for the same client; before any other disclosure ask whether the information is relevant background whose disclosure improves service; follow and improve firm electronic-communication procedures; tell clients which channels are inappropriate for confidential information (V10 p.113, p.114).

Boundary cases:
- Versus IV(A): client information is III(E); the employer's own confidential business information is IV(A).
- Good intentions do not excuse: recommending a client as a charity donor prospect from knowledge of her tax planning violates III(E) (V10 p.115).
- Illegal-activity exception is procedural, not a free pass: consult supervisor, compliance, and counsel about reporting duties rather than disclosing unilaterally (V10 p.115).
- A client's own accidental self-disclosure on a properly warned social platform is not your violation if you acted promptly to remove it (V10 p.115, p.116).

### Standard IV(A) Loyalty to Employer (V10 p.116)

**Obligation.** In matters related to your employment, act for the benefit of your employer and do not deprive the employer of the advantage of your skills and abilities, divulge confidential information, or otherwise cause harm to the employer (V10 p.116).

Key guidance:
- Client interests still come first, and personal and family obligations are not subordinated; the duty covers employment matters (V10 p.117).
- Independent practice for compensation requires notifying the employer of the services, duration, and compensation and receiving consent before undertaking it; preparation is allowed, practising is not (V10 p.117, p.118).
- Leaving an employer: act in the employer's interest until resignation is effective; violations include misappropriating trade secrets, misusing confidential information, soliciting clients before departure, self-dealing, and taking records or client lists without written permission (V10 p.118).
- After departure: skills, experience, and simple knowledge of client names are not confidential; contacting former clients from public information is permitted absent a noncompete or nonsolicitation agreement (V10 p.118).
- Social media: firm-approved business accounts are firm assets; keep personal and professional accounts separate; update profiles per the employer's departure procedures (V10 p.119).
- Whistleblowing contrary to the employer's interest is justified only to protect clients or market integrity, never for personal gain (V10 p.119).
- Independent contractors owe the duties in their agreement; degree of control determines employee status (V10 p.119, p.120).

Recommended procedures: understand competition policies and any noncompete before signing; understand termination policies including client notification and social media updates; know the firm's whistleblowing and incident-reporting procedures; firms should classify employees clearly (V10 p.120, p.121).

Boundary cases:
- The line at departure: registering a company or renting an office on your own time is preparation and permitted; soliciting your employer's clients or prospects before resigning, responding to an RFP against your employer, or copying anything (client lists, models, spreadsheets you built) violates (V10 p.121, p.123, p.124).
- Even unpaid interns are employees for this purpose: copying software you helped build is misappropriation (V10 p.122, p.123).
- After leaving, recreating contacts from public records and soliciting them is compliant; using remembered lists is fine but using taken records never is (V10 p.124, p.125).
- Versus V(C): the records themselves are firm property under V(C); the act of taking them to compete harms the employer under IV(A); exam answers often accept either, so read the options.

### Standard IV(B) Additional Compensation Arrangements (V10 p.127)

**Obligation.** Do not accept gifts, benefits, compensation, or consideration that competes with or might reasonably be expected to create a conflict of interest with your employer's interest unless you obtain written consent from all parties involved (V10 p.127).

Key guidance:
- Covers direct client compensation and indirect third-party benefits; written consent includes any documentable communication such as e-mail (V10 p.127, p.128).
- The employer is entitled to know the true cost of your services and to assess how outside arrangements affect loyalty and objectivity (V10 p.128).
- Part-time arrangements should be negotiated at hiring (V10 p.128).

Recommended procedures: make an immediate written report to supervisor and compliance stating the terms: nature, approximate amount, and duration of any proposed additional compensation, confirmed by the offering party (V10 p.128).

Boundary cases:
- Versus I(B): a client's performance bonus (a Monaco vacation for beating the benchmark) is IV(B), needing written employer consent; a broker's World Cup package rewarding order flow is I(B) (V10 p.128, p.41).
- Nonmonetary counts: free club memberships for board service must be disclosed even without cash (V10 p.129).
- The compliant pattern: gain approvals in advance, decline offered travel, disclose the dinner afterwards (V10 p.129).

### Standard IV(C) Responsibilities of Supervisors (V10 p.129)

**Obligation.** Make reasonable efforts to ensure that anyone subject to your supervision or authority complies with applicable laws, rules, regulations, and the Code and Standards (V10 p.130).

Key guidance:
- Anyone with employees subject to control or influence has supervisory responsibility, whether or not the employees are members; supervisors need in-depth knowledge of the Code (V10 p.130).
- Supervisory duties may be delegated to subordinates, but delegation does not relieve responsibility; instruct delegates in prevention and detection methods (V10 p.130).
- Reasonable supervision means an effective compliance system plus education, training, and incentives that reward ethical conduct (V10 p.130).
- If the compliance system is absent or inadequate, decline supervisory responsibility in writing until reasonable procedures are adopted (V10 p.131).
- Once a violation is discovered: promptly investigate its extent; do not rely on the wrongdoer's assurances; limit the employee's activities or increase monitoring pending the outcome; reporting up the chain and a warning are not enough (V10 p.131).
- Detection failures despite adequate procedures are not violations, but knowing procedures are not followed is (V10 p.131).

Recommended procedures: a plain-language principle-based code of ethics kept separate from detailed compliance procedures; adequate compliance procedures are written, understandable, designate an empowered compliance officer, describe the supervision hierarchy, implement checks and balances, outline scope, permissible conduct, and reporting of violations; once in place, disseminate, update, educate, remind, incorporate professional conduct in performance reviews, review employee actions, enforce promptly; establish incentive structures that reward how outcomes are achieved (V10 p.132, p.133, p.134).

Boundary cases:
- Advising executives of a pending recommendation change without procedures preventing trading on it is a IV(C) failure by the head of research (V10 p.134).
- Passing a subordinate's memo to portfolio managers without reviewing its basis violates IV(C) via V(A)'s substance (V10 p.134, p.135).
- Supervision includes yourself as system-builder: no trade tickets, no compliance manual, no review equals a IV(C) violation by the president, not just the trader (V10 p.135, p.136).
- A supervisor whose own compensation depends on the very trading she should investigate must be doubly alert to the conflict (V10 p.135).

### Standard V(A) Diligence and Reasonable Basis (V10 p.138)

**Obligation.** Exercise diligence, independence, and thoroughness in analyzing investments, making recommendations, and taking investment actions; have a reasonable and adequate basis, supported by appropriate research and investigation (V10 p.138, p.139).

Key guidance:
- The required level of diligence depends on the investment philosophy, your role in the process, and the resources provided (V10 p.139).
- Attributes of a reasonable basis include macro conditions, operating and financial history, business-cycle stage, fee structures, quant model limitations, securitization asset quality, and peer-group appropriateness (V10 p.139, p.140).
- Secondary and third-party research: make reasonable efforts to determine soundness using assumptions, rigor, timeliness, and objectivity; you may rely in good faith on the firm's vetting process unless you have reason to doubt it, and the firm should review providers regularly (V10 p.140).
- Quant research users must understand assumptions and limitations and test outputs; scenarios should include negative outcomes and go beyond the observable database; developers of quant models owe a higher level of diligence than users (V10 p.140, p.141).
- Selecting external advisers and subadvisers takes the same diligence as selecting securities: review the adviser's code of ethics, compliance and internal controls, quality of published returns, and adherence to stated strategy (V10 p.141, p.142).
- Group research: you need not dissociate from a consensus report that differs from your view if you believe the process produced a reasonable and objective basis (V10 p.142, p.145).

Recommended procedures: written policies requiring substantiated bases with supervisory-analyst or committee approval before circulation; detailed due-diligence guidance; measurable criteria for research quality; minimum scenario-testing standards for computer models including cash flow sensitivity; measurable criteria for outside providers; standardized criteria for external advisers with review frequency (V10 p.142, p.143).

Boundary cases:
- Versus V(B): presenting the maximum production scenario as the base case is a diligence failure in communication; describing the model but omitting its limits is V(B); Dhaliwal's optimal-scenario pitch violates V(A) (V10 p.143).
- Versus II(A): a well-tested model plus checked inputs is a reasonable basis; overhearing a deal in an elevator is what poisons the trade, not the model.
- A respected analyst's overheard opinion is not a reasonable basis to reverse your own documented research (V10 p.207 solution set).
- Selection shortcuts violate: choosing a subadviser on lowest fee alone, on greatest raw return alone, on RFP answers alone, or a "black box" attribution system because complexity impresses clients (V10 p.145, p.148, p.149).
- Diligent process with a bad outcome complies: the hedge fund that implodes a week after a full analysis of track record, principals, fees, and risk was still a reasonable-basis recommendation (V10 p.147, p.148).
- Model maintainers are accountable for output quality even if they never trade; users who cannot explain the model violate too (V10 p.149, p.150).

### Standard V(B) Communication with Clients and Prospective Clients (V10 p.150)

**Obligation.** Disclose to clients and prospects: (1) the nature of services and their costs; (2) the basic format and general principles of the investment process, and promptly disclose material changes; (3) significant limitations and risks of the process; (4) use reasonable judgment about which factors are important and include them; (5) distinguish between fact and opinion (V10 p.150, p.151).

Key guidance:
- Cost disclosure covers all costs to the client including affiliates and third parties, is ongoing when services or costs change, cannot lean on client sophistication, and is best made in writing at the outset; it applies to client-facing members (V10 p.151, p.152).
- Communication takes any form: one word, a phone call, a broadcast, a capsule list (offer the fuller analysis) (V10 p.153).
- Significant risks and limitations: leverage is a significant risk in general; also counterparty, country, sector, credit; limitations include liquidity and capacity; judged ex ante on what you knew at the time, so a later loss does not prove a disclosure failure (V10 p.153, p.154).
- Report presentation may emphasize some areas and omit unimportant ones if the scope limits are stipulated; quant advice must be supported by reference material and consistent methodology, with changes highlighted (V10 p.154).
- Fact versus opinion: earnings estimates, dividend outlooks, and future prices are opinions; quantitative projections and statistical conjecture must be identified with their limits (V10 p.154, p.155).

Recommended procedures: case-by-case judgment rather than a checklist; encourage a rigorous review methodology for published research; maintain records of the nature of research and be able to supply omitted factors on request (V10 p.155).

Boundary cases:
- Versus I(C): "the dividend will increase" when it depends on pending legislation is opinion stated as fact, V(B); a knowing lie about the security's terms ("guaranteed by the US government" when it is not) is I(C) (V10 p.157, p.52).
- Material process changes requiring prompt client notice: lifting a small-cap ceiling, adding non-US names, replacing a trailing-P/E screen with a DDM, moving from analysts to a committee, shifting to external submanagers, redirecting the firm to passive management (V10 p.158, p.159).
- Undisclosed fee-calculation methodology changes violate even if fees fall (V10 p.156).
- Capacity limits must be disclosed even when far from binding (V10 p.161).
- Promoting a VaR model as "extremely effective" without inputs and limits fails the risk-disclosure duty (V10 p.161).

### Standard V(C) Record Retention (V10 p.162)

**Obligation.** Develop and maintain appropriate records to support your investment analyses, recommendations, actions, and other investment-related communications with clients and prospective clients (V10 p.162).

Key guidance:
- Retention covers buys, sells, and reviews that lead to no change; hard copy or electronic; examples include meeting notes, press releases, model outputs and input parameters, risk analyses, external-adviser selection criteria, IPS review notes, and outside research (V10 p.162, p.163).
- New media: e-mails, texts, blog and social media posts used in analysis or client communication must be retained even where regulation lags (V10 p.163).
- Records created for the employer are firm property; departing members may not take them without express consent and must re-create supporting records at the new firm from public or direct sources, not from memory or taken material (V10 p.163).
- Meeting local regulatory and firm requirements satisfies the standard; absent guidance, CFA Institute recommends at least seven years (V10 p.163).

Recommended procedures: the firm generally bears the retention responsibility, but individuals must archive research notes supporting their current communications (V10 p.164).

Boundary cases:
- Versus IV(A): copying files to your home computer and using them after termination violates V(C) (property of the firm) even though the work was done on your own time (V10 p.207 solution set).
- Mosaic research must be documented so the conclusion can be shown to rest on public and nonmaterial pieces (V10 p.67).
- A model you developed and documented at one firm cannot travel with you; re-create it (V10 p.164, p.165).

### Standard VI(A) Avoid or Disclose Conflicts (V10 p.165)

**Obligation.** Avoid or make full and fair disclosure of all matters that could reasonably be expected to impair your independence and objectivity or interfere with duties to clients, prospective clients, and employer; disclosures must be prominent, in plain language, and communicate the relevant information effectively (V10 p.165).

Key guidance:
- Best practice is avoidance; when a conflict cannot reasonably be avoided, clear and complete disclosure, updated when the conflict materially worsens (V10 p.166).
- To employers: report ownership of covered stocks, outside board participation, and financial pressures promptly and comply with employer restrictions (V10 p.166).
- To clients: always avoid or disclose issuer relationships (directorships, consultancies, investment banking and underwriting, market making, material beneficial ownership); disclose nonstandard fee arrangements, subadvisory agreements, and service-fee rebates; beneficial ownership means pecuniary interest, voting power, or disposition power (V10 p.166, p.167).
- Cross-departmental conflicts (sell-side pressure from banking, broker-sponsored partnerships) must be resolved or disclosed (V10 p.167).
- Stock ownership: simplest cure is prohibition; minimum is disclosure of any beneficial interest in recommended securities (V10 p.167).
- Board service creates three conflicts: duties to clients versus shareholders, equity compensation, and access to material nonpublic information; isolate directors from investment decisions with firewalls (V10 p.168).

Recommended procedures: none listed separately; the operative devices are avoidance, prominent plain-language disclosure, firewalls for directors, and employer reporting (V10 p.166 to p.168).

Boundary cases:
- Versus I(B): a conflict you should have refused (accepting inducements) is I(B); an existing interest you failed to disclose (your wife's inherited stock in a covered company) is VI(A) (V10 p.169).
- Versus VI(C): referral compensation has its own standard; cite VI(C) when the benefit is for recommending services.
- Firm-level interests count: the underwriting relationship, the firm's 25 percent owner, or the firm's own stock position must be disclosed in research reports even if the analyst owns nothing (V10 p.168, p.208 solution set).
- Compensation-structure conflicts must be disclosed to clients: a new quarterly bonus scheme that changed the manager's incentives violated VI(A) when clients were not told (V10 p.169).
- Trivial links (using the company's products, a brother-in-law who is a supplier) are too tangential to require disclosure (V10 p.208 solution set).

### Standard VI(B) Priority of Transactions (V10 p.170)

**Obligation.** Investment transactions for clients and employers must have priority over investment transactions in which a member or candidate is the beneficial owner (V10 p.170).

Key guidance:
- Personal investing is not inherently unethical if the client is not disadvantaged, you do not benefit personally from client trades, and regulations are followed; personal trades contrary to current recommendations (a forced sale for tuition) are judged by the same three criteria (V10 p.171).
- You may trade only after clients and employers have had adequate opportunity to act; beneficial ownership includes personal, family, and any account with direct or indirect pecuniary interest (V10 p.171, p.172).
- Family accounts that are fee-paying client accounts are treated like any other client account, neither favoured nor disadvantaged; your beneficial ownership triggers preclearance and reporting (V10 p.172).
- Do not convey nonpublic information about pending trades to anyone whose securities you beneficially own (V10 p.171).

Recommended procedures: limit or preclear employee IPO participation and never benefit from clients' market position; strict limits and supervision on private placements (immediate conflict: incentive to recommend post-IPO regardless of suitability); blackout and restricted periods sized to the firm (in a small firm they prevent front-running); reporting requirements: disclosure of holdings on hire and at least annually, duplicate trade confirmations to the firm, preclearance procedures; disclose the firm's personal investing policies to clients without boilerplate (V10 p.172, p.173, p.174).

Boundary cases:
- Versus III(B): disadvantaging one client for another is III(B); disadvantaging clients for yourself, your husband's account, or the firm's proprietary book is VI(B) (V10 p.174).
- Withholding a recommendation so you can buy first violates even before any client trade occurs (V10 p.174).
- Treating your parents' fee-paying account worse than other clients violates VI(B); it is a client account first (V10 p.174, p.175).
- The firm's own trading desk closing calls and buying puts seven minutes after an internal broadcast, before clients could act, violates (V10 p.175).

### Standard VI(C) Referral Fees (V10 p.176)

**Obligation.** Disclose to your employer, clients, and prospective clients, as appropriate, any compensation, consideration, or benefit received from or paid to others for the recommendation of products or services (V10 p.176).

Key guidance:
- Disclosure lets clients and employers evaluate partiality in the recommendation and the full cost of the service (V10 p.176).
- Disclose before entry into any formal agreement, including the nature of the consideration (flat fee, percentage, one-time or continuing, performance-based, research, or other noncash benefit) and its estimated value; consideration includes cash, soft dollars, and in-kind payment (V10 p.176).

Recommended procedures: encourage firms to restrict or approve referral programs; notify clients of approved programs; give the employer at least quarterly updates on the amount and nature of referral compensation received (V10 p.176).

Boundary cases:
- In-kind counts: research services and directed commissions exchanged for referrals must be disclosed to the referred prospect (V10 p.177).
- Interdepartmental referrals inside one firm are covered exactly like third-party referrals: disclose in writing at the time of referral (V10 p.177).
- The employer must know too: directing soft commissions to a broker who refers wealthy clients to you, undisclosed to your firm, violates (V10 p.178).
- A transparent, employer-approved, client-disclosed charity donation program for referrals complies (V10 p.178).
- A consultant paid by both the sponsor and the managers he recommends violates even if the recommendation was objectively sound (V10 p.178, p.179).

### Standard VII(A) Conduct as Participants in CFA Institute Programs (V10 p.179)

**Obligation.** Do not engage in any conduct that compromises the reputation or integrity of CFA Institute or the CFA designation or the integrity, validity, or security of CFA Institute programs (V10 p.179).

Key guidance:
- Covered conduct: cheating, violating testing policies (calculator, personal belongings, Candidate Pledge), disclosing confidential program or exam information, circumventing security, improperly using an association with CFA Institute for personal or professional goals, and misrepresenting information on the Professional Conduct Statement (V10 p.180).
- Confidential information includes specific exam questions and broad topical areas and formulas tested or not tested, until CFA Institute releases them; volunteers, proctors, and graders are bound about questions, deliberations, and scoring (V10 p.180, p.181).
- Expressing opinions about CFA Institute, the Program, or exam difficulty is allowed; content-specific information is not (V10 p.181).

Recommended procedures: none listed separately; the Candidate Pledge and program policies define the perimeter (V10 p.181).

Boundary cases:
- "It was really difficult but fair" is fine; "18 derivatives questions and nothing on IPO allocations" violates (V10 p.183).
- Post-exam forum threads reconstructing answers to specific questions violate; so does e-mailing the hardest questions to a prep provider (V10 p.183).
- Volunteers: a society program chair booking only his own clients as presenters, or a GIPS committee member marketing advance knowledge of standard changes, compromises CFA Institute integrity (V10 p.184).
- Versus VII(B): exam and program conduct is VII(A); how you describe your designation or candidacy is VII(B).

### Standard VII(B) Reference to CFA Institute, the CFA Designation, and the CFA Program (V10 p.185)

**Obligation.** When referring to CFA Institute, membership, the designation, or candidacy, do not misrepresent or exaggerate the meaning or implications of membership, holding the designation, or candidacy (V10 p.185).

Key guidance:
- Factual statements about the Program's rigor and the commitment to ethics are fine; statements implying superior performance are not; opinions must be identified as opinions (V10 p.185).
- Membership maintenance requires the annual Professional Conduct Statement and dues; lapse suspends the right to present yourself as a member or charterholder (past-tense references remain permitted, and a retired classification restores use at reduced dues) (V10 p.186, p.189).
- Candidacy: you are a candidate only while registered and enrolled for a specified exam or awaiting its results; no partial designation exists; "CFA, Level II" and "CFA expected 2011" are improper; "I passed Level I" and "passed all three levels in consecutive years" are facts, but claiming that implies superior ability violates (V10 p.186, p.187).
- No pseudonymous use: anonymous online profiles must not carry the designation (V10 p.186).

Recommended procedures: circulate written guidance on VII(B) to legal, compliance, public relations, and marketing; create centrally approved templates for references to the designation (V10 p.187, p.188).

Boundary cases:
- "All principals passed all three exams on the first try" is permitted as fact; linking it to superior fund performance violates (V10 p.188).
- A candidate awaiting Level III results may not claim to have "finished the CFA Program" or eligibility for the charter (V10 p.190 solution set).
- Order of designations on a business card is personal preference; separate multiple designations with commas (V10 p.189).

## Worked examples

The extraction found 17 numbered curriculum examples; they are reworked first, in curriculum order, followed by 18 further application cases chosen for exam-likeness.
Ethics examples have no arithmetic; the "work" is the three-step assignment: identify the actor's duty, name the sub-standard engaged, and state the verdict with the preventing procedure.

**Example 1: Notification of known violations, Michael Allen** (V10 p.29)
Allen, responsible for an underwriting, learns from a company official that the financial statements he filed overstate the issuer's earnings; the firm's general counsel says the regulator would find it hard to prove wrongdoing.
Step 1: the filing is a continuing misstatement Allen now knows about, so I(A)'s "do not knowingly participate or assist" is engaged.
Step 2: reliance on legal counsel does not absolve compliance with law.
Verdict: Allen must report to his supervisor, seek an independent legal opinion, and determine whether the regulator should be notified; doing nothing would violate Standard I(A). ^ex-10-02-1

**Example 2: Dissociating from a violation, Lawrence Brown** (V10 p.29)
Brown's investment bank is principal underwriter of convertible debentures, and Brown discovers the issuer concealed severe third-quarter losses after the preliminary prospectus was distributed.
The prospectus is misleading and Brown knows it, so I(A) requires escalation: report to supervisory persons in the firm.
If the firm does not remedy the disclosure or dissociate from the underwriting, Brown must sever all his connections with the underwriting and seek legal advice on further reporting.
Verdict: violation of I(A) unless Brown escalates and, failing remedy, dissociates. ^ex-10-02-2

**Example 3: Dissociating from a violation, Kamisha Washington** (V10 p.30)
Washington's firm advertises a 10-year composite that silently omits accounts that left the firm, inflating performance, and she is asked to use the material to solicit business.
Using the number would be knowingly assisting a misrepresentation, so I(A) attaches to her even though she never calculated the figure.
The ladder: raise it with the responsible person or supervisor or compliance; if the firm will not recalculate, refuse to use the material and notify the firm why; if the firm insists, consider whether dissociation requires seeking other employment.
Verdict: she assists a violation of I(A) if she uses the material; the underlying advert also violates III(D). ^ex-10-02-3

**Example 4: Following the highest requirement, James Collins** (V10 p.30)
Collins works in a developing country whose minimal securities laws do not prohibit insider trading.
No applicable law or laxer law means the Code and Standards govern, so II(A) applies to him in full despite the local vacuum.
Verdict: no violation yet, but Collins must abide by the Code and Standards, factor the thin information environment into his advice, and handle any accidental material nonpublic information under II(A). ^ex-10-02-4

**Example 5: Following the highest requirement, Laura Jameson** (V10 p.30, p.31)
Jameson, a US citizen working as a registered adviser in Karramba, ignores the local law banning advisers from personal IPO participation, believing US law plus Code-level disclosure suffices.
Applicable law is where she is registered and working, and that law is stricter than the Code and Standards, so the strict-law branch of I(A) controls.
Verdict: violation of I(A); disclosure under the Code does not substitute for compliance with a stricter local prohibition. ^ex-10-02-5

**Example 6: Laws based on religious tenets, Amanda Janney** (V10 p.31)
Janney's fixed-income hedge fund is marketed globally, including to clients seeking Islamic-law compliance, but the marketing materials do not say whether the fund complies.
Verdict: no violation stated, but the compliant move under I(A) awareness and suitability principles is proactive disclosure of whether the fund complies with religious law where it will be distributed. ^ex-10-02-6

**Example 7: Reporting potential unethical actions, Krista Blume** (V10 p.31)
Blume notices a new broker on the research payment list who produces no research and whose country's securities the portfolios never buy, and suspects research payments are disguised payment for new business; her supervisor waves it off.
Suspected misuse of client brokerage engages I(A) dissociation duties through firm channels.
Verdict: Blume should follow the firm's procedures for reporting potential unethical activity (supervisor, then designated compliance channel), advocating disclosure of the broker-research link; silence plus continued association could be construed as participation. ^ex-10-02-7

**Example 8: Failure to maintain knowledge of the law, Colleen White** (V10 p.32)
White posts performance reports, opinions, and stock touts on social media after the regulator issued new guidance on electronic communication, and her posts conflict with it.
I(A) includes staying current on evolving regulation applicable to your activities; ignorance of fresh guidance is no defence when she should have known.
Verdict: violation of I(A); the preventing procedures are compliance-department guidance and monitoring of legal and regulatory trends. ^ex-10-02-8

**Example 9: Travel expenses from external manager, Tom Wayne** (V10 p.42)
Wayne, pension plan investment manager, recommends Penguin Advisors after an Asia fact-finding trip paid for by an academy that Penguin sponsors, with Penguin's president among the travellers.
Accepting sponsored travel and daily contact with a vendor seeking his allocation could reasonably be expected to compromise objectivity under I(B).
Verdict: to avoid violating I(B), his employer or plan should have paid his basic travel, vendor contact should have been limited to informational events, and the trip and sponsor made a matter of public record; perception matters even absent proof of bias. ^ex-10-02-9

**Example 10: Research independence and compensation, Javier Herrero** (V10 p.43)
An investor-relations firm offers Herrero a flat fee plus a bonus for any new investors the report attracts.
The bonus ties compensation to conclusions, which reasonably compromises independence in issuer-paid research.
Verdict: accepting the arrangement violates I(B); the compliant structure is a flat fee only, not tied to conclusions, with conflict disclosures in the report. ^ex-10-02-10

**Example 11: Recommendation objectivity and service fees, Bob Wade** (V10 p.43)
Wade accepts a 0.25 percent service fee from Western Funds for promoting its fund class, does not disclose it, directs portfolio managers to promote the funds exclusively, and keeps pushing them after two years of underperformance because his profit targets depend on the fee.
The fee arrangement has captured the recommendation process.
Verdict: violation of I(B), with VI(A) also implicated for nondisclosure of the arrangement. ^ex-10-02-11

**Example 12: Recommendation objectivity, Bob Thompson** (V10 p.43, p.44)
Thompson insists on running negative housing-price-appreciation scenarios on subprime securitizations against his manager's protest that the database contains no negative HPA year; the worst case persuades the desk to pass, and the portfolio outperforms when housing collapses.
Verdict: no violation; Thompson's refusal to let market trends and internal pressure trim his scenario set is exactly the conduct I(B) protects, and the scenario work also models V(A) diligence. ^ex-10-02-12

**Example 13: Influencing manager selection, Adrian Mandel** (V10 p.44)
Mandel donates to a union leader's re-election fund and lavishly entertains him to win and keep a pension mandate, booking it all as reimbursed marketing expense.
Offering benefits reasonably expected to compromise the decision maker's objectivity violates I(B) from the giving side.
Verdict: violation of I(B); disclosure to his own management does not cure the corruption of the counterparty's judgment. ^ex-10-02-13

**Example 14: Influencing manager selection, Adrian Mandel and the consultant** (V10 p.44, p.45)
Mandel wines and dines the celebrity pension consultant orchestrating the search, and his firm is short-listed.
The target of the inducement is different (consultant rather than trustee), the analysis is identical.
Verdict: violation of I(B); entertainment intended to influence a gatekeeper's recommendation is a prohibited consideration. ^ex-10-02-14

**Example 15: Fund manager relationships, Amie Scott** (V10 p.45)
Scott finds an external manager concealed a large account's bad performance by moving it to a residual composite, but the manager is the CEO's close friend and holds a large allocation.
Her duty is an independent performance analysis regardless of relationships or allocation size.
Verdict: omitting the composite change from her report would violate I(B). ^ex-10-02-15

**Example 16: Intrafirm pressure, Jill Stein** (V10 p.45)
The head of sales asks Stein to drop a departed client's account from the composite because the underperformance came from a fired research team.
Composite membership is defined by strategy, not by whose fault the performance was.
Verdict: Stein must refuse; excluding the account would violate I(B) and corrupt the composite that III(D) protects. ^ex-10-02-16

**Example 17: Technical model requirements, Jerome Dupont and John Smith** (V10 p.149, p.150)
Dupont, a credit-model developer who never trades, neglects updating the US model, patches it with a coded quick fix that turns out to be erroneous, and alerts the fund manager Smith only after diagnostics turn nonsensical; Smith, who barely understands the model, quietly rebalances without reporting.
Step 1: Dupont's models generate recommendations, so he is accountable for output quality and must test before deployment; the neglected update and untested fix violate V(A).
Step 2: Smith relies on a model he cannot explain, violating V(A)'s requirement to understand the technical aspects of products used for clients, and his silent rebalancing without disclosure of the material error also engages V(B).
Verdict: both violate V(A); the preventing procedures are minimum scenario-testing and validation standards for models and error-notification policies. ^ex-10-02-17

The following additional cases are the ones most like exam vignettes.

**Example 18: Modest versus lavish hospitality, Steven Taylor and John Adams** (V10 p.39)
Taylor accepts an issuer-paid group tour of remote mining facilities with chartered flights and Spartan motels; Adams pays his own room per firm policy.
Necessity, modesty, and business purpose mean Taylor does not violate I(B); Adams's firm simply follows best practice.
Verdict: no violation; the discriminator is lavishness and influence, not payment itself. ^ex-10-02-18

**Example 19: Restricted list as the cure, Walter Fritz** (V10 p.40)
Fritz thinks Metals and Mining is overpriced while his firm pitches its underwriting; he fears a negative report will cost the relationship.
His report must reflect his objective view; pressure from the banking division is inappropriate.
Verdict: issuing a softened rating would violate I(B); the preventing procedure is placing the company on a restricted list and distributing only factual information. ^ex-10-02-19

**Example 20: Gifts from a related party versus a client, Edward Grant and Theresa Green** (V10 p.41)
Grant accepts World Cup tickets, resort nights, meals, and limousine service from a brokerage he rewards with commission business, and tells no one; Green receives Wimbledon tickets and a London flat from a satisfied client and discloses to her employer.
Grant violates I(B): substantial gifts from a party seeking order flow compromise objectivity, and nondisclosure removes the employer's chance to monitor.
Green complies: client gifts are supplementary compensation, permitted with disclosure so the employer can watch for favouritism.
Verdict: Grant violates, Green does not; the payer's identity and the disclosure decide it. ^ex-10-02-20

**Example 21: Correcting versus perpetuating errors, Hijan Yao and Syed Muhammad** (V10 p.51, p.52)
Yao distributes GIPS marketing that misstates composite assets by a factor of ten through a typographical error; Muhammad distributes for years material crediting him with a degree he never earned.
Yao did not knowingly misrepresent, so no I(C) violation, but he must stop distribution and correct recipients.
Muhammad should have known after years of use, so the "knowingly" gate closes on him.
Verdict: Yao no violation with a duty to correct; Muhammad violates I(C). ^ex-10-02-21

**Example 22: Plagiarism plus a tip, Cindy Grant** (V10 p.52)
Grant copies extensive statistics from another analyst's report, phones a company officer who gives her nonpublic initial drilling results tripling mine life, adds them, and circulates the package as her own work.
Copying without acknowledgment violates I(C); the drilling results are material nonpublic information under II(A).
Verdict: I(C) violation for plagiarism, and acting on the drilling data would violate II(A). ^ex-10-02-22

**Example 23: The tipping chain, Frank Barnes to Alex Halsey** (V10 p.72)
A controlling shareholder's decision to accept a tender offer at nearly double the market price travels from sister to niece to her husband Staple to broker Halsey, who buys.
The information is material and nonpublic at every link.
Verdict: Staple violates II(A) by communicating it; Halsey violates II(A) by trading on it; family relationships provide no exemption. ^ex-10-02-23

**Example 24: Mosaic in action, Jagdish Teja** (V10 p.73)
Teja combines public financial reports, plant visits, and designers' and retailers' style opinions to conclude that Swan Furniture's earnings will drop, and issues a sell.
Each nonpublic piece is nonmaterial; the material conclusion is his own assembly.
Verdict: no violation; this is the mosaic theory operating as intended, provided the research is documented under V(C). ^ex-10-02-24

**Example 25: The analyst's report as inside information, Roger Clement and Mary Zito** (V10 p.73, p.74)
Star analyst Clement builds a mosaic downgrade of Turgot Chariots, keeps his support material, and previews the changed recommendation to a TV producer and journalist Zito before airtime; Zito sells her holdings and phones her father.
Clement's mosaic is compliant and his report need not be made public.
Zito receives early access to a market-moving recommendation, which is material nonpublic information in her hands.
Verdict: Clement no violation; Zito violates II(A) both by trading and by relaying, whether or not her father trades. ^ex-10-02-25

**Example 26: Expert networks, Tom Watson** (V10 p.76)
Through a placement agent with signed confidentiality policies, a testing scientist tells Watson of his disappointment with a semiconductor still under test; the fund sells its position and buys puts.
The signed agreements do not transfer his materiality duty.
Verdict: Watson violates II(A) by passing along and acting on material nonpublic product-test results; contrast the analyst who uses experts only for general treatment insights. ^ex-10-02-26

**Example 27: Liquidity theatre, John Gray** (V10 p.78)
Gray splits his large small-cap position across accounts in relatives' names, runs a blog rumor campaign, and trades the stock between the accounts to lift volume and price while he exits.
Both manipulation species are present: false information and wash-trade volume.
Verdict: violation of II(B); a real economic motive to exit does not sanitize fabricated liquidity. ^ex-10-02-27

**Example 28: The entrenched pension purchase, Julian Wiley** (V10 p.87)
Target-company managers persuade Wiley, whose bank is pension trustee, to buy the employer's stock in the plan to defeat a hostile bid, hinting at future business; he buys stock he considers overvalued, and the bid dies.
The duty runs to plan participants and beneficiaries, and requires independent evaluation of the takeover's merits.
Verdict: violation of III(A); loyalty went to corporate managers and his own business interest instead of beneficiaries. ^ex-10-02-28

**Example 29: Allocating after the fact, Eleanor Preston** (V10 p.97)
Preston buys mortgage-backed securities across accounts without immediate allocation, then days later assigns the winners to the client threatening to leave and the losers to everyone else.
Allocation must be decided before or systematically at execution, pro rata as practical.
Verdict: violation of III(B); the preventing procedures are pre-trade allocation, time-stamped tickets, and same-price block allocation. ^ex-10-02-29

**Example 30: The lockup that broke the IPS, Max Gubler** (V10 p.105)
Gubler, CIO of an insurance subsidiary whose IPS demands highly liquid large-cap equities and short AA bonds, invests 4 percent in a venture seed fund with a three-year lockup, keeping total equity below its ceiling.
Staying under the equity limit does not cure an asset-class and liquidity breach.
Verdict: violation of III(C) and III(A); private equity with a lockup fails the IPS's liquidity requirement outright. ^ex-10-02-30

**Example 31: The imported track record, Aaron McCoy** (V10 p.109)
McCoy advertises at his new firm the equity performance he achieved at G&P Financial without identifying where it was earned.
Prior-firm performance may be shown only with disclosure of its source and his role.
Verdict: violation of III(D); the cure is full disclosure, not exclusion. ^ex-10-02-31

**Example 32: Soliciting before resigning, Samuel Magee** (V10 p.121)
Before resigning, Magee asks four large accounts to move to his next employer and signs up prospects he had pitched for his current firm.
Until resignation is effective, loyalty in employment matters runs to the current employer.
Verdict: violation of IV(A); preparation is permitted, pre-departure solicitation never is. ^ex-10-02-32

**Example 33: The Monaco condominium, Geoff Whitman** (V10 p.128)
A client offers Whitman a week in Monaco any year her portfolio beats 15 percent; he goes without telling his employer.
Performance-contingent client compensation competes with the employer's interest in even-handed effort across accounts.
Verdict: violation of IV(B); written consent from all parties would have made the identical arrangement compliant. ^ex-10-02-33

**Example 34: Supervision without a system, Samantha Tabbing and Charles Henry** (V10 p.135, p.136)
Tabbing places index futures orders for two mutual funds and the employee profit-sharing plan with no account designation, no trade tickets, no compliance manual, and assigns favourable executions to the employee plan after seeing the market.
Henry, who directed her to manage everything, has no record-keeping or review procedures.
Verdict: Henry violates IV(C) twice over, for failing to supervise the trading and for failing to establish procedures that would have prevented or detected the cherry-picking; Tabbing's own conduct violates III(B) and VI(B) principles. ^ex-10-02-34

**Example 35: Punishing your parents, Erin Toffler** (V10 p.174, p.175)
Toffler allocates IPO shares to all other clients first and gives her parents' fee-paying retirement account only leftovers, to avoid accusations of favouritism.
Family accounts that are ordinary fee-paying client accounts must be treated like any other client.
Verdict: violation of VI(B) by disadvantaging her parents; beneficial ownership would additionally trigger preclearance and reporting, not worse treatment. ^ex-10-02-35

## Item-set patterns

> [!warning] Trap 1: the nearest-neighbour distractor
> The vignette shows a clear violation and offers three sub-standards, two of them adjacent to the right one (I(B) versus IV(B), III(A) versus VI(A), III(B) versus VI(B)).
> Correct approach: ask who is owed the duty and who is harmed; the payer's identity (client versus influencer), the harmed party (client versus employer versus other clients versus own account) selects the standard.
> The distractors are tempting because the conduct genuinely touches both standards; pick the one whose element the facts complete (V10 p.33, p.127, p.171).

> [!warning] Trap 2: "least likely violates"
> One question per item set flips the stem to least likely; candidates lock onto the obvious violation and pick it.
> Correct approach: mark each option violated or clean against the facts; the answer is the standard whose elements the facts do not complete, as when Acertado's tainted trade still had a diligent model basis, so V(A) was the "least likely" (V10 p.197).
> The trap options are the standards the actor really did violate.

> [!warning] Trap 3: mosaic versus material nonpublic information
> The vignette mixes public filings, expert opinions, and one overheard or tipped fact.
> Correct approach: test the decisive input alone for materiality and nonpublic status; a reliable insider fact poisons the action even if a compliant model or mosaic also supported it (V10 p.66, p.73).
> Distractors claim the model provides a reasonable basis (true but irrelevant) or that no violation occurs because information was assembled (only true when every nonpublic piece is nonmaterial).

> [!warning] Trap 4: disclosure cures everything
> An option offers disclosure as the fix for unfair allocation, unsuitable trades, or accepting inducements.
> Correct approach: disclosure cures conflicts of interest (VI(A), VI(C)) and client gifts (I(B)); it never cures unfair dealing (client consent cannot bless patently unfair allocation), unsuitability, or market abuse (V10 p.93).
> The distractor is tempting because disclosure is the right answer so often elsewhere.

> [!warning] Trap 5: outcome bias
> The vignette ends with a profit (Thompson's subprime call, Newbury's imploding hedge fund) or a loss and asks whether standards were violated.
> Correct approach: judge the process ex ante; diligent research that loses money complies, and a profitable trade on tainted information violates (V10 p.44, p.147, p.148).
> Distractors reward or punish the outcome.

> [!warning] Trap 6: the departing employee
> A manager quits; options mix permitted preparation, permitted post-departure contact, and prohibited pre-departure solicitation or record-taking.
> Correct approach: timeline plus source of information; before resignation is effective, no solicitation and no competition; after departure, public-source contact is fine absent a nonsolicitation agreement, but taken records and files are never fine, even ones the member created (V10 p.118, p.121, p.124).
> Distractors blur "skills and knowledge" (never confidential) with "records and lists" (always firm property).

> [!warning] Trap 7: supervisor liability
> A subordinate misbehaves; the question asks who violated what.
> Correct approach: the supervisor violates IV(C) if procedures were absent, inadequate, unenforced, or the response to a red flag stopped at a warning; adequate procedures plus genuine enforcement excuse a missed violation (V10 p.131).
> Distractors include "the supervisor did not trade, so no violation" and "reporting up the chain sufficed."

> [!warning] Trap 8: candidacy wording
> A business card or resume references the CFA Program.
> Correct approach: facts are fine (passed Level I, candidate in the CFA Program, passed three levels in consecutive years); implied partial designations ("CFA Level II"), expected dates ("CFA expected 2026"), finished-program claims before results, and superiority implications violate VII(B) (V10 p.187).
> Distractors punish factual statements or bless the plausible-looking "CFA, Level II."

> [!warning] Trap 9: exam confidentiality versus opinion
> A candidate posts about the exam.
> Correct approach: difficulty opinions and program criticism are protected; naming tested or untested topics, question counts, or specific questions violates VII(A) (V10 p.181, p.183).
> The distractor treats all exam talk as violation or all of it as free speech.

> [!warning] Trap 10: fee and cost disclosure scope
> A firm changes its fee calculation or omits affiliate costs, and an option pleads client sophistication or reduced fees.
> Correct approach: V(B) requires ongoing disclosure of the nature of services and all associated costs including third-party layers; sophistication is explicitly no excuse, and a change that lowers fees still requires notice (V10 p.152, p.156).
> Distractors argue no harm, no violation.

## Self-test

Vignette A (questions 1 to 5).
Priya Nair, CFA, is a sell-side pharmaceutical analyst at Deven Brokers, whose investment banking arm is pitching a secondary offering for Helix Bio.
Nair believes Helix is overvalued; her head of research suggests she "hold off on anything negative until the pitch concludes."
At a paid expert-network call arranged under signed confidentiality agreements, a trial statistician tells Nair that Helix's Phase III cardiology drug "is tracking below the efficacy bar; we all know it internally."
Nair also assembles public filings, prescription-data subscriptions, and interviews with three cardiologists unconnected to the trial, all of whom doubt the drug class.
She downgrades Helix to sell, e-mails the change to her forty institutional clients, and phones her five largest clients an hour before the e-mail goes out "so they can position themselves."
Her colleague Tomas, who saw the draft downgrade in the review queue, buys put options that morning for his own account.
Nair's assistant Lena, a Level I candidate, adds to Nair's report a sentence claiming Nair "has ranked top decile in forecast accuracy for ten years," based on a ranking service that covers only the last three years.

1. Nair's use of the statistician's remark, if she relies on it for the downgrade, most likely violates:
A. nothing, because signed expert-network agreements protect her.
B. Standard II(A), because trial results from involved personnel are material nonpublic information.
C. Standard V(A), because one expert call is an inadequate basis.

2. Setting the statistician aside, Nair's mosaic of filings, prescription data, and unconnected cardiologists is:
A. a II(A) violation because the conclusion is material.
B. compliant mosaic-theory research she should document under V(C).
C. a V(B) violation unless every source is named in the report.

3. Phoning the five largest clients before the e-mail most likely violates:
A. Standard III(B). B. Standard III(A). C. Standard VI(B).

4. Tomas's put purchase most likely violates:
A. Standard VI(B) only.
B. Standard II(A) only.
C. Standard VI(B), and the firm's missing pre-dissemination restrictions implicate IV(C).

5. Lena's added sentence most likely violates:
A. Standard I(C), attributable to Nair if she should have known.
B. Standard III(D) only, attributable to the ranking service.
C. Nothing, because a footnote citing the service cures it.

Vignette B (questions 6 to 10).
Marco Silva, CFA, runs balanced portfolios at Quill Advisers and also sits, with his employer's knowledge but no written consent, on the paid advisory board of custodian ClearSafe, which Quill recommends to clients; ClearSafe pays Silva in vouchers for free custody of his personal account.
Quill's new bonus plan pays managers on one-year peer-relative performance; Silva shifts several long-horizon endowment accounts into high-beta names without IPS changes.
Preparing to leave and launch his own firm, Silva registers the firm with the regulator on a weekend, memorizes no lists but photographs his top twenty clients' contact screens "for convenience," and after resigning e-mails those clients from the photographs.
His junior, Dev, a Level III candidate, prints business cards reading "Dev Rao, CFA (expected)" and tells prospects that "as a finishing candidate, my selections have institutional-grade accuracy."
Silva's departing act is to delete his research notes so "no one misuses my models."

6. Silva's ClearSafe board seat and voucher compensation most likely violate:
A. Standard IV(B) and VI(A) and VI(C) together. B. Standard I(B) only. C. Standard IV(A) only.

7. The high-beta shift under the new bonus plan most likely violates:
A. Standard III(C), with VI(A) for the undisclosed compensation conflict.
B. Standard III(B), because some clients got more beta than others.
C. Standard V(B) only.

8. Silva's weekend registration of his future firm is:
A. a IV(A) violation, competition with his employer.
B. permitted preparation under IV(A).
C. a violation only if he told clients.

9. The photographs and post-resignation e-mails most likely violate:
A. nothing, because he had resigned before e-mailing.
B. Standard IV(A) and V(C), because firm records were taken and used.
C. Standard III(E), because client information belongs to clients.

10. Dev's card and claim most likely violate:
A. VII(B) for the card; the accuracy claim is permissible opinion.
B. VII(B) for both the implied designation and the implied superior performance.
C. VII(A), because candidacy status is confidential.

> [!success]- Answer key
> 1. B. Involved trial personnel disclosing internal efficacy tracking hand over material nonpublic information; signed network agreements do not transfer the materiality judgment (guidance V10 p.67, p.68).
> A fails for that reason; C mislabels the problem, because her basis was otherwise ample, which is exactly why II(A), not V(A), is the violated standard.
> 2. B. Public filings plus nonmaterial nonpublic expert colour from unconnected physicians is the mosaic theory operating correctly, and documentation under V(C) strengthens the defence (V10 p.66, p.67).
> A confuses conclusion materiality with input materiality; C overstates V(B), which requires including important factors and separating fact from opinion, not naming every source.
> 3. A. Early selective release of a material recommendation change is the fair-dealing violation itself (V10 p.92, p.96).
> III(A) is the distractor for any client harm; VI(B) is wrong because Nair traded nothing personally.
> 4. C. Trading his own account ahead of client dissemination of a recommendation change is VI(B) front-running, and the firm's failure to restrict trading by those with prior knowledge points at IV(C) supervision and III(B) procedures (V10 p.94, p.173).
> II(A) is the tempting overreach: internal recommendation changes are handled by VI(B) and III(B) machinery, and Tomas is not trading on issuer inside information.
> 5. A. A ten-year claim built on a three-year ranking is a misrepresentation of performance accuracy; I(C) catches Nair if she knew or should have known her report carried it, and III(D) is implicated for the recommendation-accuracy claim (V10 p.46, p.108).
> B wrongly outsources responsibility; C fails because attribution does not make a false span true.
> 6. A. Paid outside service that competes with employer interests without written consent from all parties violates IV(B); the custody vouchers received for recommending ClearSafe are referral consideration requiring VI(C) disclosure to employer, clients, and prospects; the standing arrangement is also a VI(A) conflict to disclose prominently (V10 p.127, p.166, p.176).
> 7. A. Chasing a short-horizon bonus with high-beta names against long-horizon IPSs is the Snead pattern: III(C) violation plus VI(A) failure to disclose the changed compensation conflict (V10 p.106, p.169).
> B misapplies fair dealing; no cross-client favouritism is shown.
> 8. B. Registration and arrangements on personal time, without solicitation or operation, are permissible preparations (V10 p.123, p.124).
> 9. B. The client screens are firm records; photographing them is taking records without written permission (IV(A)), and using them post-departure compounds it; V(C) makes the records firm property (V10 p.118, p.125, p.163).
> A would hold only for public-source contact; C misstates ownership.
> 10. B. "CFA (expected)" implies a partial or pending designation, and "institutional-grade accuracy" from candidacy implies superior performance; both are VII(B) violations (V10 p.187, p.188).
> C confuses program confidentiality with reference rules.

## Common mistakes

1. Assigning gifts by size instead of by payer: the discriminator is whether the giver is a client (disclose to employer, I(B)) or a party seeking influence (refuse, I(B)) or a source of employer-competing compensation (written consent, IV(B)) (V10 p.33, p.127).
2. Treating disclosure as a universal cure: disclosure fixes conflicts (VI(A), VI(C)) but never unfair allocation, unsuitable trades, or trading on inside information (V10 p.93).
3. Calling every assembled conclusion inside information: mosaic conclusions are legal however material; test the individual nonpublic inputs for materiality instead (V10 p.66, p.67).
4. Believing "fairly" means "equally": premium service tiers disclosed and available to all are fine; simultaneous-second delivery to every client is not required (V10 p.91, p.92).
5. Forgetting the mandate branch of III(C): fund managers to a mandate violate suitability by drifting from the stated strategy, and are not responsible for investor-level suitability (V10 p.103).
6. Confusing skills with records at departure: experience and remembered names leave with the employee; files, lists, models, and photographs of screens never do without written permission (V10 p.118, p.163).
7. Letting supervisors off after a warning: discovering a violation requires investigation and interim restrictions; relying on assurances or reporting up the chain is itself a IV(C) violation (V10 p.131).
8. Punishing good process for bad outcomes and rewarding bad process for good outcomes: standards judge conduct ex ante; diligent research that loses money complies, tainted trades that profit violate (V10 p.147, p.44).
9. Blessing "CFA Level II" or "CFA expected" as factual: candidacy facts are fine, implied partial designations and superiority claims are not (V10 p.187).
10. Treating exam-difficulty opinions as violations and topic lists as free speech: VII(A) protects opinions and forbids revealing tested or untested content (V10 p.181, p.183).
11. Missing the firm-level conflict: the analyst who owns nothing must still disclose the firm's underwriting role, ownership stake, or market making in the covered security (V10 p.167, p.168).
12. Overlooking that causing others to trade is as bad as trading: recommending to a sister, a client, or a newsletter on tainted information violates II(A) without any personal trade (V10 p.64).
13. Assuming family accounts are not clients: a fee-paying relative's account gets equal treatment; disadvantaging it is the violation (V10 p.89, p.175).
14. Forgetting the strict-law direction runs both ways: a laxer home country does not soften stricter local law, and a laxer locality does not soften the Code (V10 p.26, p.27).
