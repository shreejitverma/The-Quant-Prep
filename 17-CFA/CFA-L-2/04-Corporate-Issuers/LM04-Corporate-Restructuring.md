# LM4: Corporate Restructuring

Difficulty 3 of 5: the taxonomy is memorization, but the exam lives in the pro forma arithmetic (combined operating income, EPS accretion or dilution, post-deal capital weights, debt to EBITDA) where one forgotten line item, a missed one-time cost or an unadded share issuance, flips the answer.

## Core intuition

Companies follow a life cycle: start-up, growth, maturity, decline, each with its own revenue growth, free cash flow, business risk and debt capacity (close to 0% debt in start-up, 0-20% in growth, 20%+ from maturity on) (V4 p.156).
Left alone, a maturing company would optimally milk cash flow and eventually liquidate, but managers and boards act to change their destiny instead (V4 p.156).
All of those actions fall into three buckets defined by what they do to the perimeter of the firm.
Investment actions increase size or scope through inorganic growth: equity investments, joint ventures, acquisitions (V4 p.156, p.159).
Divestment actions shrink size or scope by shedding slower-growing, lower-profitability or higher-risk operations: sales (divestitures) and spin offs (V4 p.157, p.162).
Restructuring actions change neither size nor scope but improve the cost or financing structure: cost restructuring, balance sheet restructuring, reorganization, in increasing order of financial impact (V4 p.157, p.159).
The leveraged buyout is the special case that combines all three: buy with heavy debt, restructure costs and the balance sheet, divest pieces, exit by sale or listing (V4 p.159, p.166).

> [!abstract] Idea 1: a large issuer is a portfolio of businesses, and restructuring is portfolio rebalancing
> Most large issuers own many lines of business at different life-cycle stages; common ownership creates synergies but also costs, and some businesses are worth more in other hands or standing alone (V4 p.157).
> Every action in this module is management editing that portfolio: buy what you lack (investment), sell what fits badly (divestment), fix what stays (restructuring).
> The analyst's question is never "is the deal big and exciting" but "does the redeployed capital now earn more than investors' required return".

Motivations split into issuer-specific and top-down (V4 p.157, p.158).
Issuer-specific motivations for investment are synergies, growth, improved capabilities or secured resources, and the chance to buy an undervalued target (V4 p.158, p.159).
Cost synergies come from economies of scale (one headquarters, consolidated support functions, higher capacity utilization and route density); revenue synergies come from economies of scope (cross-selling, bargaining power) (V4 p.160).
Issuer-specific motivations for divestment mirror those: focus, valuation (a unit that would be valued higher outside), liquidity needs, and regulatory requirements; the last two are forced, and forced sales tend to happen at low valuations that favor the buyer (V4 p.158, p.161, p.162).
Restructuring motivations are improved returns on capital (opportunistic, like franchising or a dividend recapitalization) or financial distress up to bankruptcy and liquidation (forced) (V4 p.158, p.163).
Top-down drivers span all types: high security prices (activity correlates 0.80 with the MSCI World index) via CEO confidence, cheaper financing, and the ability to spend overvalued stock; and industry shocks from regulation, technology, or growth changes (V4 p.158, p.159).
The counterintuitive empirical twist: deals struck in weak economies create roughly 10% more three-year shareholder return than strong-economy deals, so risk-taking pays when others are risk averse (V4 p.159).

Know the nine types cold, because classification questions are free points.
An equity investment buys a material stake below 50%; both firms stay independent, the investor gets exposure and maybe board influence (V4 p.160).
A joint venture is a new, jointly controlled company into which participants contribute assets and know-how; it is technically an equity investment but larger in size, control, and management time, and typical for entering new (often foreign) markets (V4 p.160).
An acquisition transfers control: the target becomes a consolidated subsidiary and every financial statement line aggregates (V4 p.161).
A sale hands control of a business to an acquirer for cash, ending the seller's exposure; a spin off distributes new shares of a separated business to the parent's shareholders as a stock dividend, creating two independent companies and raising no cash for the parent (V4 p.162, p.163).
Choosing between them is mostly a valuation and liquidity call: a sale gives definitive proceeds and suits a seller needing cash, while a spin off avoids regulatory scrutiny (it reduces market power concentration) but takes quarters to execute and leaves valuation to the market (V4 p.163).
Cost restructuring cuts operating costs to restore margins to history or peers, commonly via outsourcing (subcontract standardizable processes for scale economies) and offshoring (relocate operations to cheaper countries, sometimes both combined) (V4 p.163, p.164).
Balance sheet restructuring changes asset composition or capital structure: the sale leaseback (sell an asset, lease it back; cash up front, ownership risk gone, but lease expense exceeds the old depreciation because the lessor earns interest) and the dividend recapitalization (issue debt to fund dividends or buybacks, swapping expensive equity for cheaper debt; sensible only with stable cash flows) (V4 p.164).
Reorganization is court-supervised restructuring for insolvent issuers: operations continue, management usually stays, creditors negotiate debt-to-equity conversions and asset sales, and the firm exits with a lighter debt load; liquidation is the failure branch, where the court sells the assets and distributes proceeds by legal priority (V4 p.165).

> [!abstract] Idea 2: the analyst's funnel is what, why, material, when, then value, then model
> Initial evaluation answers four questions: what is happening, why, is it material, when will it close (V4 p.174).
> Materiality has two dimensions: size (rule of thumb: transaction value, meaning cash plus stock issued plus assumed debt, above 10% of the acquirer's pre-announcement enterprise value is large) and fit (even a small deal signals a strategy change, as Farfetch's 8%-of-EV acquisition that cost the stock 45% shows) (V4 p.174, p.175).
> Announcement-day price reactions do not predict long-run value creation, and closing takes quarters to years with shareholder, creditor and regulatory approvals as the key uncertainty; the market prices in the expected impact, including completion risk, immediately (V4 p.175, p.176).

Preliminary valuation uses three market-based methods, usually together (V4 p.176).
Comparable company analysis applies trading multiples (EV/EBITDA, EV/sales, P/E) of similar listed firms; it is market-derived and data-rich, but comparables may not exist, the market may misprice the whole set, and it yields a fair trading price, so a takeover premium must be added to get a fair acquisition price (V4 p.176, p.178, p.179).
It is therefore the natural tool for spin offs, which trade with no control premium (V4 p.176).
Comparable transaction analysis applies multiples from actual historical acquisitions of similar targets, so the premium is already embedded and no separate estimate is needed; but the market for corporate control is illiquid, old multiples embed old macro and industry conditions, and past acquirers may themselves have over- or underpaid (V4 p.179, p.180).
Premium paid analysis computes the takeover premium, the price of control, as the percentage by which the deal price exceeds the unaffected price; the unaffected price must predate rumor-driven run-ups (use the price a week or more before announcement, or a volume-weighted average) (V4 p.181).
Median premiums have run just over 30%, ranging 20%-40% by year (V4 p.181).

> [!abstract] Idea 3: pro forma modeling is bookkeeping discipline, not cleverness
> Combine acquirer and target line by line; subtract cost synergies and add one-time costs in operating expenses; add amortization of acquired intangibles to D&A; add interest on new debt; tax at the blended rate; add issued shares to the count (V4 p.182, p.183).
> EPS accretion or dilution then falls out of a division, and the usual culprits when a deal dilutes despite higher EBITDA are the new shares, the new interest, and the intangible amortization (V4 p.188, p.200).
> WACC changes because both the weights (debt-financed cash deals raise the debt weight) and the component costs move; profitability, volatility, leverage, collateral and prevailing rates drive the costs, and losing an investment-grade rating raises WACC by several hundred basis points, which is why acquirers structure consideration (more stock, less cash and debt) to defend the rating (V4 p.183, p.184).

For the case studies, the recurring analytics are worth internalizing as reflexes.
An equity-method stake adds one line, share of investee profit or loss minus amortization of fair value adjustments, so a loss-making investee dilutes EPS even with no new shares (V4 p.190, p.193).
A step-up from joint venture to control flips accounting from the equity method to consolidation, with the seller booking a gain of proceeds minus carrying value derecognized (V4 p.193, p.196, p.197).
An acquisition multiple normally exceeds the trading multiple because of control and synergies; adding expected synergies to target EBITDA can pull a 16x headline multiple back inside the 8x peer range (V4 p.199).
A conglomerate discount is measured by sum-of-the-parts minus trading EV, and the analyst allocates corporate costs and picks growth-matched comparables before believing it (V4 p.162, p.204, p.205).
A cost restructuring is sized by the operating expense cut needed to hit the peer median margin, phased over years and net of one-time costs that can swamp year-one gains (V4 p.209).
A sale leaseback trades depreciation for higher rent, retires debt, and can buy back shares with the residue; the valuation metric is the cap rate, net operating income over property value, the reciprocal of a multiple, so the seller wants a low cap rate (V4 p.211, p.212, p.213).
In an LBO, returns to the private equity fund come from purchase price, leverage, free cash flow during ownership, and exit price; the debt magnifies equity returns while creditors and the post-exit public shareholders inherit a far more leveraged company (V4 p.166, p.167).

## Formulas and derivations

**Formula 1: takeover premium** (V4 p.181)
$$
PRM = \frac{DP - SP}{SP}
$$
^f-04-04-1

$DP$ is the deal price per target share; $SP$ is the unaffected target share price, taken a week or more before announcement (or a volume-weighted average) to strip out rumor run-up.
The premium is the price of control: what shareholders demand to relinquish it.
Historical median is just over 30%, with annual medians of 20%-40% (V4 p.181).

**Formula 2: fair takeover price from a trading valuation** (V4 p.179, p.181)
$$
DP = SP \times (1 + PRM)
$$
^f-04-04-2

Comparable company analysis yields a fair trading price $SP$; to judge an acquisition you must add an estimated premium, because trading multiples carry no control value.
Comparable transaction multiples skip this step, since the premium is already embedded in historical deal prices (V4 p.180).

**Formula 3: transaction value and the materiality rule of thumb** (V4 p.174)
$$
TV = \text{Cash paid} + \text{Stock issued} + \text{Debt assumed}, \qquad \text{Large if } \frac{TV}{EV_{acquirer,\,pre}} > 10\%
$$
^f-04-04-3

All components at announcement values; $EV_{acquirer,\,pre}$ is the acquirer's enterprise value just before announcement.
Size is one dimension of materiality; fit (consistency with strategy and the analyst's expectations) is the other, so a sub-10% deal can still be material (V4 p.175).
For non-transactions such as cost programs, size is the announced cost cut as a percentage of revenue or operating expenses (V4 p.174).

**Formula 4: comparable company (sum-of-the-parts) enterprise value** (V4 p.176, p.204)
$$
EV_{est} = \sum_{i} M_i \times X_i
$$
^f-04-04-4

$M_i$ is the peer median (or mean) multiple for segment $i$ (EV/EBITDA, EV/sales); $X_i$ is the segment's metric, adjusted for allocated corporate and unallocated costs.
The estimate assumes like assets price alike; it inherits any market-wide mispricing of the peer set (V4 p.178, p.179).

**Formula 5: conglomerate discount** (V4 p.205)
$$
\text{Conglomerate discount} = EV_{SOTP} - EV_{trading}
$$
^f-04-04-5

A positive value means the market prices the whole below the sum of the parts, from diseconomies of scope, deficit of focus or investment, incompatible businesses, or neglect (V4 p.162).
A spin off or sale is the standard action to close it; sign convention matters, as subtracting in the wrong order is a listed distractor (V4 p.223).

**Formula 6: comparable transaction weighted average takeover value** (V4 p.180)
$$
V_0 = \sum_i w_i \, (M_i \times X_i), \qquad \sum_i w_i = 1
$$
^f-04-04-6

$M_i$ is the mean deal multiple (P/E, P/CF, P/BV, P/S) from comparable acquisitions; $X_i$ is the target's per-share metric; $w_i$ reflects the analyst's confidence in each value driver.
Because the multiples come from actual acquisitions, $V_0$ is a takeover value, premium included.

> [!tip] Calculator
> BA II Plus, Hofer-style with P/CF weighted 40%: 2.62 × 18.3 = 47.95 STO 1; 4.33 × 10.2 = 44.17 STO 2; 12.65 × 3.7 = 46.81 STO 3; 22.98 × 2.4 = 55.15 STO 4.
> Then 0.2 × RCL 1 + 0.4 × RCL 2 + 0.2 × RCL 3 + 0.2 × RCL 4 = 47.65.

**Formula 7: exchange ratio arithmetic for a stock-and-cash offer** (V4 p.185)
$$
\text{Consideration per target share} = C + ER \times P_A, \qquad \Delta N_A = ER \times N_T
$$
^f-04-04-7

$C$ is cash per target share, $ER$ the exchange ratio (acquirer shares per target share), $P_A$ the acquirer share price, $N_T$ target shares outstanding, $\Delta N_A$ new acquirer shares issued.
Raising $ER$ raises target holders' ownership of the combined company and lowers debt needs; raising $C$ does the reverse (V4 p.186).
Target holders' stake in the combined firm is $\Delta N_A / (N_A + \Delta N_A)$.

**Formula 8: pro forma combined operating income** (V4 p.172, p.183)
$$
OI_{pf} = (R_A + R_T + S_R) - (O_A + O_T - S_C + K)
$$
^f-04-04-8

$R$ and $O$ are standalone revenues and operating expenses of acquirer and target, $S_R$ revenue synergies, $S_C$ cost synergies, $K$ one-time cash costs to achieve them.
Synergies phase in (often thirds over three years) while costs to achieve are front-loaded and can exceed year-one synergies (V4 p.187, p.219).
Dis-synergies enter with opposite signs.

**Formula 9: pro forma EPS and accretion or dilution** (V4 p.188)
$$
EPS_{pf} = \frac{NI_{pf}}{N_A + \Delta N_A}, \qquad \text{Accretion} = EPS_{pf} - EPS_{A,\,standalone}
$$
^f-04-04-9

$NI_{pf}$ runs the combined operating income through added intangible amortization, new interest expense on acquisition debt (and forgone interest income on cash used), and the blended tax rate (V4 p.183).
Positive difference is accretion, negative is dilution; dilution despite higher EBITDA usually traces to issued shares, new interest, and amortization of acquired intangibles (V4 p.200).

**Formula 10: pro forma capital structure weights** (V4 p.183, p.185)
$$
w_d = \frac{D_{pf}}{D_{pf} + E_{pf}}, \qquad E_{pf} = (N_A + \Delta N_A)\times P_A
$$
^f-04-04-10

$D_{pf}$ is post-close debt (existing acquirer debt plus new borrowings plus assumed target debt), with book debt proxying market value; $E_{pf}$ assumes an unchanged share price unless told otherwise.
Back out pre-deal debt from the disclosed post-close total: $D_{pre} = D_{pf} - \text{new borrowings} - \text{assumed debt}$ (V4 p.185).

> [!tip] Calculator
> CN case: pre debt 33 - 3.8 - 19 = 10.2; pre equity 713 × 105 = 74,865 (USD millions), so wd = 10.2/85.065 = 12%.
> Post: equity (713 + 103) × 105 = 85,680; wd = 33/118.68 = 28%.

**Formula 11: weighted average cost of capital** (V4 p.183)
$$
WACC = w_d r_d (1 - t) + w_p r_p + w_e r_e
$$
^f-04-04-11

Weights are market-value proportions of debt, preferred and common equity; $t$ is the marginal tax rate.
A restructuring moves both weights and component costs; the cost drivers are profitability, revenue and EBITDA volatility, leverage, collateral quality, and prevailing rates and spreads (V4 p.184).
Falling from investment grade to speculative grade raises WACC by several hundred basis points, which is why bidders defend the rating by shifting consideration toward stock (V4 p.184, p.187).

**Formula 12: leverage and coverage ratios for the pro forma issuer** (V4 p.213, p.214)
$$
\frac{\text{Debt}}{EBITDA} = \frac{\text{Gross (or net) debt}}{EBIT + D\&A}, \qquad \text{Interest coverage} = \frac{EBIT}{\text{Interest expense}}
$$
^f-04-04-12

Map both ratios to a ratings grid (AAA/AA at 0-1.0x debt to EBITDA and coverage above 12x, down to B at 3.6-4.5x and 1.5-0.5x), then price debt as the Treasury rate plus the rating's average spread (V4 p.214).
Net debt subtracts cash; using cash on hand and borrowing have the same effect on net debt (V4 p.187).

**Formula 13: equity-method income from an associate or joint venture** (V4 p.190, p.193)
$$
\text{Income from associates} = \alpha \times NI_{investee} - \text{Amortization of FV adjustments}
$$
^f-04-04-13

$\alpha$ is the ownership fraction (material but below 50%).
One line on the investor's income statement, no revenue consolidation; a loss-making investee dilutes EPS with no change in share count.
On a step-up to control the acquirer switches to consolidation, recognizes assets, liabilities and any non-controlling interest, and the seller books a gain of proceeds minus carrying value derecognized (V4 p.196, p.197).

**Formula 14: capitalization rate for a sale leaseback** (V4 p.211, p.213)
$$
\text{Cap rate} = \frac{NOI}{\text{Property value}} \approx \frac{\text{Annual rent}}{\text{Sale proceeds}}
$$
^f-04-04-14

The cap rate is the reciprocal of a valuation multiple, so a lower cap rate means a higher price for the seller-tenant; rent approximates NOI when the tenant bears operating costs and taxes (V4 p.213).
Judge a deal's cap rate against the 25th/50th/75th percentile of comparable transactions: at the median it is supported, below the median it compares favorably for the seller (V4 p.213, p.217).

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Daimler splits into Daimler Truck and Mercedes-Benz (V4 p.161, p.162)

Daimler AG announced in February 2021 that it would spin off Daimler Trucks & Buses as a separate Frankfurt-listed company and rename the remaining segment Mercedes-Benz.
Mechanism: a stock dividend of newly created Daimler Trucks & Buses shares paid to Daimler AG shareholders, who end up holding two independent stocks.
Classification: divestment, spin off; no cash is raised and control passes to existing shareholders rather than a buyer.
Motivation: focus; the CEO cited different customer groups, technology paths and capital needs, and freedom from the constraints of a conglomerate structure.
This is the template answer for "why spin off": distinct businesses, management and employee focus, stock compensation tied to each business's own performance. ^ex-04-04-1

### Example 2: Novartis divestments and the Alcon spin off (V4 p.162)

Novartis divested its vaccines and OTC business to GlaxoSmithKline, sold animal pharmaceuticals to Eli Lilly, and spun off Alcon (eye care) via stock dividend on 9 April 2019.
At the spin off, Alcon was valued at over 30 times EPS while Novartis traded at roughly half that P/E; two years later Alcon shares were up over 35% while Novartis was flat.
Interpretation: the market valued the same business more highly outside the parent, evidence of a conglomerate discount inside; Alcon's growing, patent-cliff-free, lower-R&D device business merited a premium multiple the parent's pharma multiple suppressed.
Motivation classification: valuation (an undervalued unit inside, potentially higher-multiple outside), the mirror image of the undervalued-target motivation for investment actions. ^ex-04-04-2

### Example 3: Six Flags reorganization (V4 p.165, p.166)

Timeline: 2006 EBIT down 50% on fixed costs, shares to about USD5; 2007 EBIT down another 34%, shares to USD2.50, net debt to EBITDA near 13x (exhibit shows 12.1x in 2006, 12.7x in 2007 on net debt of USD2,217M and USD2,229M); 2008 cost restructuring improves profitability despite a 24% revenue fall, but the company misses interest and preferred dividend payments; shares below USD1.00 trigger NYSE delisting; Chapter 11 filed 13 June 2009 while parks keep operating.
Resolution, May 2010: bondholders invest USD725 million of new equity and convert over USD1 billion of debt to equity; the company emerges with USD784 million net debt, under 3.0x expected 2010 EBITDA; prior equityholders are wiped out; shares relist as Six Flags Entertainment Corp.
Lessons: reorganization is the forced end of the restructuring spectrum; operations continue during the process; creditors become the new owners; a cost restructuring alone cannot fix a balance sheet problem (leverage of 12.7x) when refinancing markets are shut. ^ex-04-04-3

### Example 4: Blackstone's LBO of Hilton (V4 p.166, p.167)

2007 deal: Blackstone funds pay USD47.50 per share, about USD20 billion for all equity, and assume USD6 billion of Hilton debt, a USD26 billion transaction.
Financing check: USD14.5 billion borrowed plus USD5.5 billion fund equity = USD20 billion cash portion (Python: `14.5+5.5 = 20.0`).
Long-term debt jumps from USD7,000M pre-LBO to USD21,157M in 2008, a factor of 3; FCF during ownership pays it down to USD15,068M by the 2013 IPO.
Blackstone sells nothing at the IPO, exits gradually over 2013-2018, and realizes cumulative net profit above USD11 billion on USD5.5 billion of equity, a 3x multiple of invested capital over 11 years.
Who bears the risk: the fund's small equity slice earns the levered upside; creditors carry the leverage through the holding period, and post-exit public shareholders inherit a materially more leveraged capital structure than pre-LBO. ^ex-04-04-4

### Example 5: corporate evolution and actions knowledge check (V4 p.168, p.169, p.170, p.171, p.172, p.173)

Q1: a company with growth decelerating from 8% to 1% should consider an acquisition of a faster-growing business, or divest sub-consolidated-growth segments.
Q2: acquisition versus internal investment; advantage is time to market, disadvantage is cost, since acquisition prices exceed market valuations by a control premium.
Q3: divestment least likely motivated by increasing revenue (A); divestments shrink revenue while increasing focus (B) and ROIC (C).
Q4 matching: distressed oil producer with maturities in 6 months, reorganization; growth saturation, acquisition; diverging segments, spin off; capital- and labor-intensive owned clinics, balance sheet restructuring (franchise or sale leaseback).
Q5: sell rather than spin when the issuer desires liquidity (A); a sale raises cash, a spin off does not.
Q6: JV versus equity investment: same accounting under IFRS and US GAAP, but a JV is a new entity, purpose-built, jointly controlled; an equity investment buys shares of an existing investee that keeps control.
Q7: Tyche's sale leaseback of 40 stores is a balance sheet restructuring; benefits are upfront cash for debt service and shed ownership costs; costs are interest embedded in lease expense and increased indebtedness.
Q8: two-thirds of acquisitions fail to create value because of overpaying (value transfers to the seller), under-realized synergies, and integration issues.
Q9 (AstraZeneca-Alexion): synergies as a share of target opex = 500/2,646 = 18.9%, call it 19% (Python: `500/2646 = 0.1890`).
Q10: combined operating income; revenues add, opex adds minus synergies plus one-time costs.
Year 1: (22,090 + 4,130) - (16,418 + 1,952 - 166 + 217) = 26,220 - 18,421 = 7,799.
Year 2: 29,374 - 20,249 = 9,125. Year 3: 32,686 - 22,073 = 10,613 (all recomputed in Python, match).
Q11: standalone AstraZeneca revenue growth 10.4% and 9.2% with margins 26%, 26%, 28%; combined growth 12.0% and 11.3% with margins 29.7%, 31.1%, 32.5%.
The deal adds roughly 200 bps of growth and 400-500 bps of margin even though one-time costs exceed synergies in every year, because Alexion is a higher-growth, higher-margin business (Alexion Year 1 margin is (4,130 - 1,952)/4,130 = 52.7%). ^ex-04-04-5

### Example 6: Farfetch acquires New Guards Group (V4 p.175)

Farfetch, an asset-lite luxury marketplace earning commissions, paid USD704 million for New Guards Group, about 8% of Farfetch's pre-announcement enterprise value, under the 10% size threshold.
Yet the shares fell 45% the next day: the deal meant Farfetch would compete with sellers on its own platform and shift toward an inventory-bearing retailer model with higher operating costs.
Lesson for the materiality LOS: fit dominates size when a deal signals a strategy change or an admission that the existing model has problems.
Companion lesson from the research: announcement-day returns still do not predict long-run outcomes; Rehm and West found no correlation between announcement effects and excess returns two or more years later, and more than half of negative-reaction deals later produced excess returns (V4 p.175). ^ex-04-04-6

### Example 7: spin off valuation by sum of the parts (V4 p.177, p.178)

Setup: Connectivity EBITDA EUR7,638M, Media EBITDA EUR2,000M; consolidated EV EUR96,380M at 10x EBITDA; peer medians 13x for Connectivity, 6x for Media.
Q1: SOTP = 13 × 7,638 + 6 × 2,000 = 99,294 + 12,000 = EUR111,294M, which is 15.5% above the trading EV of 96,380 (Python: `111294/96380 - 1 = 0.1547`), so a spin off has the potential to increase stakeholder value (B); the market is undervaluing one or both segments relative to peers.
Q2: the Media segment might not fetch the peer median because of differences in expected growth, profitability, or risk; under-investment by current management may force a heavy-investment period that depresses FCF.
Q3: corporate cost allocation; Media is 8,000/28,100 = 28.5% of revenue, so its share of EUR250M of corporate costs is 250 × 0.285 = EUR71M, and adjusted EBITDA is 2,000 - 71 = EUR1,929M (Python: `250*8000/28100 = 71.17`).
Q4: the colleague's capital structure objection mostly fails because the analysis uses enterprise value multiples, which are capital-structure-insensitive; it has force only if the spun entity's leverage is far above peers, raising its cost of capital and impairing even its EV.
This is the canonical spin off exam block: SOTP, corporate cost allocation, and the EV-multiple defense. ^ex-04-04-7

### Example 8: comparable transaction analysis for Medical Services (V4 p.179, p.180)

Hofer evaluates the USD55.00 per share paid by General Health for Medical Services using three closed acquisitions.

## Item-set patterns

> [!warning] Trap 1: misclassifying the action
> The vignette describes a sale leaseback, franchising move, dividend recap, or court process and asks for the restructuring type.
> Correct: sale leaseback and dividend recap are balance sheet restructurings; cost cutting via outsourcing or offshoring is cost restructuring; the court-supervised process for insolvency is reorganization; a spin off is a divestment, not a restructuring (V4 p.163, p.164, p.165).
> Distractors: "reorganization" for any distressed action (it requires the court process), "cost restructuring" for asset sales (no, that is balance sheet), and "divestment" for a sale leaseback (the asset use is retained).

> [!warning] Trap 2: the 10% materiality screen applied without fit
> A deal is quoted at some percentage of the acquirer's enterprise value and a statement calls it immaterial.
> Correct: size (transaction value = cash + stock + assumed debt, over 10% of pre-announcement EV is large) and fit are both dimensions; a sub-10% deal that signals a strategy change is material (Farfetch at 8% lost 45%), and a no-strategy-change deal above 10% is still material by size (V4 p.174, p.175).
> Distractors: judging size against market cap instead of EV, forgetting assumed debt in transaction value, or treating "no change in strategy" as settling materiality.

> [!warning] Trap 3: adding (or not adding) the takeover premium to the wrong method
> A question asks for a fair acquisition price from a set of multiples.
> Correct: comparable company multiples price a trading share, so add a premium for a takeover; comparable transaction multiples already contain the premium, so adding one double counts (V4 p.179, p.180).
> Distractors: "comparable transaction analysis requires a separately estimated premium" (false), "comparable company analysis is insensitive to market mispricing" (false, that is its listed disadvantage), and swapping the methods' advantages ("value approximated relative to similar transactions" belongs to transactions, not companies).

> [!warning] Trap 4: the unaffected price in premium paid analysis
> The target ran up on rumors before the announcement, and the vignette offers the day-before price and a week-before price.
> Correct: PRM = (DP - SP)/SP with SP taken before the rumor run-up, a week or more prior or a volume-weighted average (V4 p.181).
> Distractors: the day-before price (premium understated), the post-announcement price (premium near zero), or dividing by the deal price instead of the unaffected price.

> [!warning] Trap 5: pro forma operating income with synergies but not one-time costs
> A combined income statement question gives phased synergies and phased cash costs to achieve them.
> Correct: subtract the year's synergies from combined opex and add back that year's one-time costs; year Y operating income = combined revenue - (combined opex - synergies + costs) (V4 p.172, p.183).
> Distractors: omitting the one-time costs (answer too high, that is the C option in practice Q6), omitting synergies (too low, option A), or using the full run-rate synergy in year one instead of the phased amount.

> [!warning] Trap 6: EPS accretion arithmetic that forgets a line
> A stock-and-cash deal asks for pro forma EPS.
> Correct: net income must include new interest expense on acquisition debt, forgone interest income on cash used, and amortization of acquired intangibles, all tax-effected; the denominator adds the issued shares (exchange ratio times target shares, or the stated issuance) (V4 p.183, p.188, p.200).
> Distractors: dividing by old shares only, ignoring the intangible amortization (Tulor's EPS looks accretive without the 200), and concluding accretion because EBITDA rose.

> [!warning] Trap 7: capital structure weights from the wrong debt or share count
> The vignette gives post-close total debt, new borrowings, and assumed target debt, and asks for pre- or post-deal weights.
> Correct: pre-deal debt = post-close debt - new borrowings - assumed debt; post-deal equity = (old shares + issued shares) x unchanged price; weights are debt over debt plus equity (V4 p.185).
> Distractors: answering with the debt weight when equity is asked (29% versus 71% in practice Q8), using pre-deal shares for post-deal equity, or valuing equity at the target's price.

> [!warning] Trap 8: equity investment, JV, acquisition attribute swaps
> Three statements compare the structures.
> Correct: only acquisition delivers control; acquisitions need substantially more capital than equity investments; JVs give more equal governance representation and require larger investments than equity investments (V4 p.160, p.161, p.170, p.220).
> Distractors: "equity investments allow control of the target", "JVs are smaller commitments than equity stakes", and "equity investments provide more equal governance than JVs" (each reverses a true ranking).

> [!warning] Trap 9: conglomerate discount sign and inputs
> A sum-of-the-parts question gives segment metrics, peer medians, and corporate costs.
> Correct: apply each segment's own peer multiple (never the average of the multiples across dissimilar segments), deduct allocated corporate costs from segment EBITDA first, then discount = SOTP EV - trading EV; positive means undervalued as a conglomerate (V4 p.204, p.205, p.218).
> Distractors: averaging the three segment multiples and applying it to consolidated EBITDA (practice Q4's option A), subtracting in the reverse order (Q10's option A), and skipping the corporate cost allocation.

> [!warning] Trap 10: sale leaseback direction errors
> A sale leaseback vignette asks for the pro forma leverage, coverage, or credit conclusion.
> Correct: rent (in opex) up, depreciation down, a gain-on-sale amortization credit, interest down from debt retirement, share count down if proceeds fund buybacks; EBITDA usually falls but debt falls faster, so debt to EBITDA improves and the rating can rise, cutting the cost of debt (V4 p.212, p.213, p.214).
> Distractors: "the credit rating change will increase the cost of capital" (backwards, practice Q2's option C), treating the lower cap rate as a worse deal for the seller, and forgetting that the lessee books a lease liability near the PV of the payments.

> 9. Equity-method loss = 0.25 × (-160) - 8 = -48; interest = 0.07 × 900 = 63; pretax drag = 111; after tax at 25% = 83.25.

> 10. Exit debt = 9,000 - 3,500 = 5,500; equity proceeds = 13,500 - 5,500 = 8,000; MOIC = 8,000/3,000 = 2.7x.
> Downside falls first on the fund's thin equity, then on creditors holding the 9,000; returns hinge on purchase price, leverage, FCF for paydown, and exit price (V4 p.166, p.167).

## Flashcards

#flashcards/cfa-l2/04-Corporate-Issuers/lm04

The three general categories of corporate structural change::Investment (increase size or scope), divestment (decrease size or scope), restructuring (improve cost or financing structure without changing size or scope) (V4 p.156, p.157)

The three investment actions in increasing order of capital required and control::Equity investment (material stake below 50%), joint venture (new jointly controlled entity), acquisition (control and consolidation) (V4 p.159, p.160, p.161)

The two divestment actions::Sale (divestiture for consideration, exposure ends) and spin off (stock dividend of a new independent company to parent shareholders, no cash raised) (V4 p.162, p.163)

The three restructuring actions in increasing order of financial impact::Cost restructuring, balance sheet restructuring, reorganization (V4 p.159)

Which restructuring type is a sale leaseback?::A balance sheet restructuring; the seller gets cash, keeps use via the lease, and books a lease liability near the PV of lease payments (V4 p.164)

Which restructuring type is a dividend recapitalization?::A balance sheet restructuring; debt-financed dividends or buybacks swap equity for debt to cut WACC, viable only with stable cash flows (V4 p.164)

Reorganization vs liquidation::Reorganization is court-supervised negotiation with creditors while operations continue and the firm exits with less debt; liquidation is the court selling assets and distributing proceeds by legal priority after reorganization fails (V4 p.165)

An LBO combines which elements?::Investment (leveraged acquisition by a PE fund), restructuring (cost and balance sheet actions), divestment (asset sales), exiting via sale or listing (V4 p.166)

The four drivers of LBO returns::Purchase price, amount of leverage, free cash flow during ownership (used to pay down debt), exit price (V4 p.166, p.167)

Cost synergies arise from economies of {{scale}}; revenue synergies from economies of {{scope}} (V4 p.160)

Issuer-specific motivations for investment actions::Synergies, growth, improved capabilities or secured resources, undervalued target (V4 p.158, p.159)

Issuer-specific motivations for divestment actions::Focus, valuation (unit worth more outside), liquidity needs, regulatory requirements; the last two are forced and tend to occur at buyer-friendly prices (V4 p.158, p.161, p.162)

The two top-down drivers of restructuring activity::High security prices (CEO confidence, cheap financing, overvalued stock as currency) and industry shocks (regulation, technology, growth changes) (V4 p.158, p.159)

Weak-economy deals vs strong-economy deals::Weak-economy deals create about 10% more shareholder return over three years on average (V4 p.159)

The four questions of the initial evaluation::What is happening, why, is it material, when is it happening (V4 p.174)

Materiality's two dimensions::Size (transaction value over 10% of acquirer pre-announcement EV is large) and fit (consistency with strategy and analyst expectations) (V4 p.174, p.175)

Transaction value formula::Cash paid + value of stock issued + value of target debt assumed (V4 p.174)

Do announcement-day returns predict long-run deal value?::No; research finds no correlation with excess returns two or more years later, and over half of negative-reaction deals later earn excess returns (V4 p.175)

Main timing uncertainty for a large deal::Shareholder, creditor, and regulatory (antitrust) approvals; large deals can take over 12 months from announcement to close (V4 p.175, p.176)

Which valuation method suits spin offs best and why?::Comparable company analysis, because spin offs trade without a control premium, unlike acquisition or sale multiples (V4 p.176)

Two advantages of comparable company analysis::Market-derived estimates (unlike assumption-heavy DCF) and readily available data; it approximates value relative to similar companies (V4 p.178)

Three disadvantages of comparable company analysis::Good comparables may not exist, sensitivity to market-wide mispricing, and it yields a fair trading price so a takeover premium must be added for deals (V4 p.178, p.179)

Two advantages of comparable transaction analysis::Estimates come from actual deal prices, and no separate takeover premium is needed since it is embedded (V4 p.180)

Three disadvantages of comparable transaction analysis::Illiquid market for control means few comparables, historical multiples embed stale industry and macro conditions, and past acquirers may have over- or underpaid (V4 p.180)

Takeover premium formula::PRM = (DP - SP)/SP, with SP the unaffected price from a week or more before announcement or a volume-weighted average (V4 p.181)

Typical takeover premium range::Annual medians just over 30%, ranging about 20%-40% (V4 p.181)

Pro forma opex for an acquisition year::Acquirer opex + target opex - that year's cost synergies + that year's one-time costs to achieve them (V4 p.172, p.183)

Pro forma D&A for an acquisition::Combined D&A plus amortization of acquired intangible assets (V4 p.183)

Pro forma tax rate for a combined company::EBT-weighted average of acquirer and target tax rates, usually provided by the issuers (V4 p.183)

The five factors driving component costs of capital::Profitability, volatility of revenues and EBITDA, leverage, assets available as collateral, prevailing interest rates and spreads (V4 p.184)

Effect of losing investment-grade status on WACC::An increase of several hundred basis points, which is why acquirers shift consideration toward stock to defend the rating (V4 p.184, p.187)

To raise target shareholders' ownership of the combined company, the acquirer should {{increase the stock proportion (exchange ratio) of the consideration}} (V4 p.186)

Why is spending cash on hand no better for credit than borrowing?::Both raise net debt by the same amount; net debt = gross debt - cash (V4 p.187)

Equity-method income line::Ownership share of investee net income minus amortization of fair value adjustments of identifiable net assets (V4 p.190, p.193)

What changes when a JV partner steps up to control?::Accounting flips from equity method to consolidation, with recognition of assets, liabilities and a non-controlling interest; the seller books gain = proceeds - carrying value derecognized (V4 p.196, p.197)

Why do acquisition multiples exceed trading multiples?::Control (the premium buys decision rights) and synergies (crediting expected synergies to target EBITDA pulls the multiple back toward the peer range) (V4 p.199)

Conglomerate discount formula::Sum-of-the-parts estimated EV minus current trading EV; positive means the market underprices the conglomerate (V4 p.205)

In SOTP, corporate and unallocated costs should be::Deducted from segment metrics (allocated, often by revenue share) before applying peer multiples (V4 p.178, p.204)

Sale vs spin off decision in one line::A sale gives definitive cash proceeds (liquidity, certainty); a spin off may achieve a higher multiple but bears market valuation risk and takes quarters (V4 p.163, p.207)

Cost restructuring sizing::Required opex cut = target margin x revenue - current EBIT; express also as a percentage of current operating expenses (V4 p.209)

Why does year one of a cost restructuring often look worse?::One-time restructuring costs are front-loaded and can exceed the first year's margin improvement (V4 p.210)

Two standard risks of a big cost restructuring::Cutting the spending that drove superior growth, and political or public backlash from layoffs and closures (V4 p.210)

Cap rate definition and direction::NOI over property value, the reciprocal of a multiple; a lower cap rate means a higher price, so sellers in sale leasebacks want low cap rates (V4 p.211, p.213)

Sale leaseback income statement chain::Rent up in opex, depreciation down, gain-on-sale amortization credit, interest down from debt retirement, shares down if proceeds fund buybacks (V4 p.212, p.213, p.214)

Which is the trap answer when a question asks the equity weight after a deal, and why?::The debt weight (for example 29% when equity is 71%); the exam lists the complementary percentage as an option every time (V4 p.222, p.223)

Only {{acquisitions}} give the investor control of the target; joint ventures give more equal {{governance representation}} and larger investments than equity investments (V4 p.160, p.161, p.223)

Franchising as a restructuring::An opportunistic business-model change: divest assets, license IP for royalties (mid-single-digit percent of sales), shift store-level costs and risks to franchisees (V4 p.163)

Outsourcing vs offshoring::Outsourcing subcontracts standardizable processes to third parties for scale economies; offshoring relocates operations to cheaper countries while keeping them in-house; they are often combined (V4 p.164)

In the Opone case, why was P/FCF misleadingly cheap?::Dividends received from the JV far exceeded recognized JV income because the JV was draining its own cash balance; the FCF was not sustainable (V4 p.195)

## Common mistakes

1. Classifying a sale leaseback or dividend recap as a divestment or cost restructuring; both are balance sheet restructurings, and a spin off is a divestment, not a "restructuring" in the narrow sense (V4 p.163, p.164).
2. Testing materiality against market cap, or forgetting assumed debt in transaction value; the rule is cash + stock + assumed debt over 10% of pre-announcement enterprise value, and fit can make smaller deals material (V4 p.174, p.175).
3. Adding a takeover premium to comparable transaction multiples (double counting) or failing to add one to comparable company multiples when pricing an acquisition (V4 p.179, p.180).
4. Computing the takeover premium off a rumor-inflated price instead of the unaffected price from a week or more before the announcement (V4 p.181).
5. Building pro forma operating income with synergies but without the one-time cash costs, or with run-rate synergies instead of the phased amount for that year (V4 p.172, p.183, p.222).
6. Forgetting intangible-asset amortization, new interest expense, or forgone interest income in pro forma net income, then declaring a dilutive deal accretive because EBITDA rose (V4 p.183, p.200).
7. Dividing pro forma net income by the old share count, or reporting the debt weight when the question asks for the equity weight (V4 p.188, p.222).
8. Applying the average of segment peer multiples to consolidated EBITDA in a sum-of-the-parts, instead of each segment's own multiple, and skipping the corporate cost allocation (V4 p.204, p.221).
9. Subtracting trading EV and SOTP EV in the wrong order for the conglomerate discount, or claiming a credit upgrade raises the cost of capital (V4 p.205, p.221, p.223).
10. Reading a low cap rate as a bad price for the sale leaseback seller; the cap rate is a reciprocal multiple, so lower is better for the seller-tenant (V4 p.213).
11. Assuming equity investments confer control or that JVs are smaller commitments than equity stakes; only acquisitions give control, and JVs sit between the other two in size and governance (V4 p.160, p.161, p.223).
12. Trusting announcement-day price reactions as verdicts on long-run value creation; the research says they do not correlate (V4 p.175).
